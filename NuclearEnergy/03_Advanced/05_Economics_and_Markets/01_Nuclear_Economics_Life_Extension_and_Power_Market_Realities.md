# Nuclear Economics, Life Extension, and Power Market Realities

## Overview

Nuclear power plants are among the most capital-intensive infrastructure investments a country or utility can make. A large gigawatt-scale plant requires billions of dollars of upfront investment — paid mostly before the first kilowatt-hour is generated — and then operates for 40–80 years producing electricity at a cost structure dominated by that fixed capital charge rather than fuel or variable costs. This capital intensity creates a set of economic characteristics that are fundamentally different from gas turbines, solar panels, or wind farms, and understanding them is essential for engineers who work in or adjacent to the nuclear industry.

This page covers the economics of nuclear power — how costs are structured, why discount rates matter so much, how capacity factor affects revenue, and what the options look like for life extension versus new build. It covers the commercial realities of power markets: the difference between regulated cost-of-service utilities and deregulated wholesale markets, how nuclear plants compete (or struggle to compete) in each, and the emerging role of grid services and carbon premium revenue. It addresses SMR economics honestly — the case that proponents make and the counterarguments that remain unresolved. And it covers license renewal in the US and Long-Term Operation internationally, since life extension decisions are among the most commercially significant choices an operating nuclear program faces.

Engineers who understand these economics are better positioned to make technically sound decisions that are also commercially viable — a combination that is increasingly required in senior technical and leadership roles at nuclear operators, regulatory bodies, and advisory firms.

## Capital Cost Structure and the Overnight Cost Concept

Nuclear plant costs are typically decomposed into three categories: overnight cost, financing cost, and operating cost.

**Overnight cost** is the hypothetical cost to build the plant if construction were instantaneous — "overnight." It includes engineering, procurement, construction, commissioning, and owner's costs, but excludes interest during construction (IDC). Overnight cost is the standard metric for comparing plant designs and is published in studies by IAEA, NEA, EPRI, and DOE. For a large PWR completed in recent years, overnight costs have ranged from roughly $4,000–$8,000 per kWe in Western markets, with significant variation based on labor costs, regulatory environment, construction experience, and first-of-a-kind versus nth-of-a-kind learning effects.

**All-in cost** (or total construction cost) adds interest during construction to the overnight cost. Because nuclear plants have long construction schedules (5–12 years for large plants), and because IDC compounds at the cost of capital throughout construction, the all-in cost can be 20–50% higher than the overnight cost depending on the discount rate and schedule. This is why construction schedule overruns are financially devastating for nuclear projects — every additional year of construction adds not only direct construction costs but also accumulated interest on the entire invested capital.

**Operating costs** are dominated by fixed costs — staffing, maintenance, regulatory fees, property taxes — rather than variable costs. The fuel cost for nuclear power is modest compared to fossil fuels (uranium fuel accounts for perhaps 20–25% of total operating cost at US plants); most of the operating cost is fixed regardless of how much electricity the plant produces. This cost structure means that nuclear plants have a very strong incentive to run at high capacity factor — the fixed costs are paid regardless, so maximizing production amortizes those costs over more kilowatt-hours.

## LCOE: The Standard Metric and Its Limitations

Levelized Cost of Electricity (LCOE) is the constant price per megawatt-hour that, if received for every MWh of electricity produced over the plant's life, would exactly cover all costs (capital, fuel, O&M, decommissioning) at the specified discount rate. LCOE is the standard metric for comparing electricity generation technologies.

For nuclear plants, LCOE is heavily sensitive to two inputs: the discount rate and the capacity factor.

**Discount rate sensitivity**: Because nuclear has large upfront capital costs and long operating life, the present value of future revenues declines steeply with discount rate. At a 3% discount rate (as used in some regulated utility or government financing scenarios), nuclear LCOE may be $60–80/MWh for a new large PWR. At a 10% discount rate (representative of merchant financing), the same plant's LCOE may be $120–160/MWh — making it commercially uncompetitive against combined cycle gas or even new solar with storage. This extreme discount rate sensitivity is why low-cost government financing (sovereign loan guarantees, state-owned enterprise balance sheets) is central to the economics of new nuclear build in most of the countries pursuing it. Countries where nuclear economics work are mostly those where the cost of capital for energy infrastructure is low.

**Capacity factor**: LCOE improves approximately proportionally with capacity factor for a fixed-cost plant. A plant achieving 95% capacity factor has roughly 15% lower LCOE than the same plant at 82% capacity factor. World-class US plants consistently achieve 90–95% capacity factors, which is one reason existing US nuclear plants — with capital costs long amortized — have very low production costs and can compete against cheap natural gas. New plants, with capital still being amortized, have much higher all-in LCOE.

**LCOE's limitation**: LCOE compares technologies as if they produce equivalent electricity. It does not capture the value of dispatchability (nuclear is available when needed; solar is not available at night) or the system cost of intermittency (integrating high shares of variable renewable energy requires backup capacity, storage, or grid reinforcement that LCOE for the renewable does not capture). Nuclear's LCOE disadvantage versus solar in some studies reflects a technology comparison that omits these system costs.

## Power Market Structures and Nuclear's Competitive Position

Nuclear plants operate in two fundamentally different commercial environments:

**Regulated cost-of-service utilities** (common in the US Southeast, the UK pre-privatization, France, Japan, India): the utility is allowed to recover its prudently incurred costs through regulated rates, plus a regulated rate of return on equity. In this model, new nuclear construction is commercially viable as long as the regulator allows the capital to earn a return — the risk is borne by ratepayers, not shareholders. This is the model under which most of the world's existing nuclear fleet was built, and the model being used for Vogtle Units 3 and 4 in Georgia (US) and Hinkley Point C in the UK.

**Deregulated wholesale electricity markets** (common in the US Northeast and Midwest, Germany, Australia): nuclear plants sell electricity in an energy-only or capacity market at market-clearing prices. If the market price falls below the plant's variable operating costs (which are low for nuclear), the plant can continue operating. But the plant cannot recover its capital cost from the market if market prices are suppressed by low gas prices or subsidized renewables. This is why a number of US nuclear plants have been closed or threatened with closure in deregulated markets despite being safely operating and having the lowest carbon emissions of any generating source — the market structure did not support their fixed cost recovery. Zero-emissions credits (ZECs) and clean energy standard payments in New York, Illinois, and New Jersey were designed specifically to address this market failure.

**Load-following difficulty**: Nuclear plants are technically capable of power modulation (EDF operates much of its French fleet in load-following mode), but load-following is commercially unattractive in most market structures. Reducing output reduces revenue while fixed costs remain constant; increasing output to meet demand peaks requires reactor physics constraints (xenon transient limitations) to be managed. In deregulated markets, the economically optimal strategy for a nuclear plant is to run at maximum continuous rated power and sell every available megawatt. Operating flexibility has value only in markets that pay for it explicitly — currently a small minority.

## License Renewal and Long-Term Operation

The original US design life for most nuclear plants was 40 years, reflecting a combination of reactor physics assumptions (neutron embrittlement limits), regulatory practice, and financial amortization periods. Most US plants have obtained a 20-year license renewal (LR) from the NRC, extending operation to 60 years. Many are now pursuing subsequent license renewal (SLR), extending to 80 years — a 40-year extension over the original license.

**License renewal economics**: The decision to pursue license renewal rather than retire a plant is driven by a comparison between the cost of continued operation (including required aging management program investments, refurbishment costs, and increased maintenance of aging systems) and the value of the electricity production foregone if the plant closes. For a fully amortized plant with low variable operating costs, the incremental production cost of extending operation may be $20–30/MWh. If the market value of nuclear electricity — including capacity payments, clean energy credits, and wholesale energy — exceeds this, life extension is profitable.

**Aging management programs (AMPs)**: License renewal is contingent on demonstrating adequate management of time-limited aging effects — specifically, neutron embrittlement of the reactor pressure vessel, fatigue of primary system components, and environmentally-assisted cracking of buried piping and cable insulation. SLR applications must demonstrate that these aging mechanisms can be managed to 80 years, often requiring new inspection programs, replacement of specific components, and enhanced monitoring.

**IAEA Long-Term Operation (LTO)**: The IAEA uses the term Long-Term Operation (LTO) for operation beyond the original design life or original licensing period, corresponding to the US license renewal concept. IAEA Safety Guide SSG-48 provides guidance on LTO safety reviews. For NPCIL's PHWRs and for most operating fleets outside the US, the LTO framework — rather than the NRC-specific license renewal process — is the applicable reference.

## SMR Economics: The Case and the Counterarguments

Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) with output in the range of 50–300 MWe per unit are proposed as a solution to the capital cost barriers facing large nuclear construction. The core economic arguments for SMRs:

- **Factory fabrication and modular assembly** reduce construction schedule and labor cost by moving work to a controlled factory environment with repetition-based learning curves
- **Smaller unit size** reduces financing risk — you commit capital in smaller increments — and may fit the balance sheet of smaller utilities or industrial users
- **Higher learning rates** as units are replicated may drive cost reduction in ways that one-of-a-kind large plants cannot achieve
- **Passive safety systems** in some designs (e.g., NuScale VOYGR, GE-Hitachi BWRX-300) reduce operator staffing requirements

The counterarguments that remain genuinely unresolved in 2026:

- **Diseconomies of scale** are real: per-kW costs scale roughly with 0.6 power of output (economies of scale); a 300 MWe plant has higher per-kW costs than a 1,200 MWe plant of the same design if built in the same country at the same learning rate. SMR learning effects must overcome this disadvantage, and learning effects take hundreds of units to materialize
- **The first units set the price** and the first units of any new nuclear design are expensive. NuScale's first project (Carbon Free Power Project in Idaho) was cancelled in November 2023 after announced cost estimates rose significantly from initial projections. This is a real data point, not a rumor
- **SMR economics are not proven at commercial scale** in 2026. The IAEA and IEA are careful to distinguish between SMR economic projections (many) and commercial SMR operating cost experience (essentially none at scale)
- **Grid integration economics for small units** differ from large plants: a 300 MWe unit provides less capacity balancing value per unit and may not qualify for some capacity markets designed around large conventional generators

Honest assessment: SMRs may prove economic at scale in specific applications (remote communities, industrial heat, defense facilities, countries unable to finance large plants) without necessarily replicating the economics of existing large plants. The engineering community should treat SMR economic projections as projections, not benchmarks.

### Real-World Implementation

Nuclear economics is not an abstract academic topic — it directly shapes which plants get built, which stay open, and which close. The wave of US nuclear closures in the 2010s (Vermont Yankee, Kewaunee, Crystal River, San Onofre) occurred because market economics in deregulated regions could not recover the fixed operating costs of plants competing against cheap natural gas and subsidized renewables. The plants that remained open either operated in regulated markets or received state policy support (ZECs, clean energy standards). This is a case study in how market structure, not physical plant condition, determines economic survival.

The IEA's World Energy Outlook and the NEA/IEA publication "Projected Costs of Generating Electricity" are the authoritative international references for nuclear LCOE and construction cost data. The US DOE's "Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Nuclear" (2023) is the most detailed recent analysis of US nuclear economics and the conditions under which new build becomes financially viable. The NITI Aayog's India Energy Security Scenarios publications provide the Indian planning context for nuclear's role in the generation mix.

France presents the most complex case study in nuclear economics: EDF operates the world's largest single-operator nuclear fleet, runs it in regulated/quasi-regulated market conditions, has accumulated significant debt from simultaneous decarbonization and fleet life extension investments, and is pursuing a new large reactor program (EPR2) at a time when the economics of large nuclear new build are under significant scrutiny. EDF's annual reports are a primary source for real commercial nuclear economics data.

### Industry Tool Stack

- **IAEA PRIS** (Power Reactor Information System, pris.iaea.org) — the authoritative database of nuclear plant operating history, capacity factors, and outage data; free access; the starting point for any empirical nuclear economics analysis
- **NEA/IEA "Projected Costs of Generating Electricity"** — published every 3–4 years; compiles LCOE data across technologies and countries; the standard reference for comparative cost analysis
- **US DOE Energy Information Administration** (eia.gov) — annual energy review, monthly fuel cost data, wholesale electricity market price history; the primary data source for US nuclear economics analysis
- **Lazard LCOE Analysis** — annual publication (available free at lazard.com) comparing LCOE across technologies; widely cited in energy industry discussions; note the methodology assumptions carefully, particularly discount rates
- **IAEA OECD NEA nuclear economics studies** — "The Costs of Decarbonisation" and related reports on the system value of nuclear and the economics of flexibility
- **Primavera P6 / Oracle Crystal Ball** — same project scheduling tools used in outage management; nuclear new build projects use these for construction schedule and cost risk analysis
- **Python with pandas, NumPy, matplotlib** — standard stack for LCOE sensitivity analysis, DCF models, and electricity market analysis; energy system modeling frameworks like PyPSA (open source) are used for grid integration analysis
- **PLEXOS / PROMOD / Aurora** — commercial electricity market simulation tools; used by utilities and consultants for long-term capacity planning, nuclear dispatch optimization, and market revenue forecasting

### Step-by-Step Applied Workflow

1. **Compute a simple LCOE for a nuclear plant** — define: overnight cost ($6,000/kWe), construction period (7 years), discount rate (8%), capacity factor (90%), O&M cost ($25/MWh), fuel cost ($8/MWh), operating life (60 years); use the standard LCOE formula (annualized capital cost + fixed O&M per MWh + variable O&M + fuel); compute at 5%, 8%, and 12% discount rates; observe the sensitivity.

2. **Reproduce the NEA published LCOE** — obtain the "Projected Costs of Generating Electricity 2020" from the NEA website; reproduce the LCOE calculation for one nuclear data point using the published inputs; understand which input assumptions drive the largest differences between country estimates.

3. **Analyze IAEA PRIS capacity factor data** — download historical capacity factor data from IAEA PRIS for the US fleet (available as CSV); compute: (a) fleet average capacity factor by year 1990–2023, (b) the distribution of plant-level capacity factors in 2023, (c) the correlation between plant age and capacity factor.

4. **Build a life extension decision model** — model the decision to pursue 20-year license renewal versus decommissioning for a fully amortized plant: inputs are incremental license renewal capital cost, annual O&M cost, capacity factor, electricity revenue per MWh, and discount rate; compute the NPV of continued operation versus avoided decommissioning liability; identify the breakeven electricity price.

5. **Compare regulated vs. deregulated market economics** — for the same plant, model revenue under: (a) a cost-of-service regulated tariff (plant recovers all costs at a defined return on equity), (b) a wholesale market scenario with electricity price from EIA historical data; compute operating cash flow in each scenario; identify at what wholesale price the plant would make a closure decision.

6. **Evaluate an SMR economic projection critically** — obtain a published SMR LCOE projection (DOE Pathways to Commercial Liftoff document, or a publicly available vendor study); identify the key assumptions (overnight cost, construction schedule, discount rate, learning rate assumptions, capacity factor); compute the LCOE under alternative assumptions where you vary each key input by ±25%; assess how robust the conclusion is.

7. **Analyze the economics of carbon pricing for nuclear** — model how a $50/tonne CO2 price (as in the EU Emissions Trading System) changes the competitive position of a nuclear plant versus a combined cycle gas plant; use EIA carbon intensity data for gas generation; compute the additional annual revenue for the nuclear plant per MWh.

8. **Read and interpret an EDF annual report** — EDF publishes detailed financial disclosures including segmented generation costs; identify the reported generation cost per MWh for the French nuclear fleet; compare to the French wholesale electricity price; calculate the generation margin; note the accounting treatment of provision for long-term waste management and decommissioning.

### AI Integration

AI applications in nuclear economics and market analysis are growing in three areas. First, electricity price forecasting: ML models (gradient boosting, LSTM) trained on historical wholesale market price data, weather, generation mix, and demand patterns can produce short-term and medium-term electricity price forecasts that feed nuclear dispatch optimization models. These models are widely used in energy trading but are being adapted for nuclear-specific applications where the goal is not trading optimization but long-term investment decision support.

Second, construction cost risk modeling: ML models trained on historical major infrastructure project data — tracking cost overruns and schedule delays as a function of project characteristics, contracting structure, regulatory environment, and workforce factors — can provide more realistic probability distributions for nuclear construction cost estimates than the deterministic schedules typically presented in project financing. EPRI and DOE have invested in research on this type of probabilistic cost modeling.

Third, policy impact analysis: natural language processing tools applied to regulatory filings, utility earnings calls, and policy documents can extract structured data about nuclear plant operating cost pressures, regulatory interactions, and closure risk — generating intelligence that supports investment decisions and policy advocacy. This is not a specifically nuclear application but is being used by energy analysts who cover the sector.

The limitation of AI in nuclear economics is the same as in any infrastructure domain: the data is sparse, heterogeneous, and confounded by policy changes. There are fewer than 450 operating nuclear reactors globally; their construction and operating histories span 60 years and multiple regulatory and market regime changes. This is a small dataset for ML by any standard, which limits the generalizability of ML-based economic models.

### Case Studies

**DOE Pathways to Commercial Liftoff — Nuclear (2023)**: The US Department of Energy's Pathways to Commercial Liftoff report for nuclear is the most detailed recent analysis of US nuclear economics, covering both existing fleet economics and new build scenarios. It quantifies the conditions under which new nuclear becomes financially viable: high clean energy credits, low cost of capital, standardized licensing, and learning from early commercial units. The report explicitly acknowledges the challenges and does not overclaim for SMR economics. Engineers working in nuclear business development or policy should treat this document as required reading.

**NEA/IEA System Cost Studies**: The Nuclear Energy Agency has published several studies on the "system costs" of different generation technologies — the total cost to the electricity system including backup capacity, grid reinforcement, and balancing costs. These studies consistently show that nuclear has low system costs (because it is dispatchable and produces no weather-dependent variability), while high-penetration solar and wind have substantial system costs. This analysis is the economic basis for the argument that LCOE comparisons systematically understate nuclear's value relative to variable renewables.

**NPCIL Regulated Model and India's Nuclear Capacity Planning**: NPCIL operates in a cost-plus regulated structure administered by AERB and governed by the Atomic Energy Act. Their electricity tariffs are set by the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) based on approved capital costs and allowed returns. NITI Aayog's energy planning documents (India Energy Security Scenarios, Optimal Generation Capacity Mix reports) show nuclear playing a baseload role in India's long-term grid expansion through 2047, with the specific capacity target driven by both economics (low LCOE at Indian cost-of-capital for government entities) and energy security (uranium diversification vs. imported coal and gas). This regulated model provides a contrasting case study to the US deregulated experience.

### Failure Modes & Safety

**Overnight cost estimates as anchor bias in project financing**: Nuclear project proponents consistently present overnight cost estimates that are below eventual outturn costs. The Vogtle 3&4 project in the US was initially estimated at approximately $14B total cost and completed at approximately $35B. This pattern — which also appears in UK (Hinkley Point C), Finland (Olkiluoto 3), and France (Flamanville 3) — reflects both genuine uncertainty in first-of-a-kind construction and incentives to present optimistic estimates during the project approval phase. Engineers engaged in nuclear project economics should apply explicit contingency reserves to overnight cost estimates and treat historical cost escalation as a baseline assumption rather than a risk to be mitigated away.

**Discount rate assumptions that are inconsistent with the project's financing structure**: An LCOE calculation using a 5% discount rate may be technically justifiable for a government-financed project but is misleading if presented as the LCOE for a privately financed merchant plant where the equity investor requires a 12% return. The discount rate assumption must match the actual cost of capital for the financing structure being analyzed, not the theoretically lowest possible rate.

**Carbon revenue projections that assume durable policy**: Life extension decisions and new build decisions are sometimes justified partly on projected future carbon revenue (from carbon pricing, clean energy credits, or equivalent policy instruments). Carbon pricing policy is politically variable: the EU Emissions Trading System price has ranged from €5/tonne to €100/tonne over its history. Treating a current or projected carbon price as a durable baseline for a 40-year investment is risky — sensitivity analysis should show the investment NPV with and without carbon revenue.

**SMR economics that omit first-of-a-kind costs**: Economic projections for new SMR designs sometimes present nth-of-a-kind costs (after the learning curve has been traversed) as if they apply to the first commercial units. The first NuScale units, the first TerraPower Natrium units, and the first GE-Hitachi BWRX-300 units will be priced based on first-of-a-kind costs — which are historically 2–4× the projected nth-of-a-kind costs. Investors and policymakers who do not distinguish between these are likely to be surprised.

### Business & Commercial Layer

Nuclear economics underpins the commercial viability of the entire nuclear industry — not just power generation, but the manufacturing, services, and regulatory sectors that depend on it. A commercially viable nuclear fleet creates sustained demand for fuel enrichment, reactor components, inspection services, engineering consulting, and waste management. A contracting fleet creates declining demand and workforce attrition.

The commercial landscape for nuclear power in 2026 has three distinct dynamics. In the US, the existing fleet is economically supported by a combination of clean energy policy (Inflation Reduction Act production tax credits for nuclear), state-level zero-emissions credits, and the long-term transition of merchant plants to regulated structures or power purchase agreements with clean-energy-seeking industrial customers. New build is focused on SMRs and advanced reactors (Natrium, BWRX-300) with DOE cost-share support.

In Europe, the post-Ukraine energy crisis reversed a decade-long trend toward nuclear closure. EDF's EPR2 program in France is the largest new nuclear investment in Western Europe since the 1990s; Poland, the Netherlands, and several Eastern European countries are pursuing new nuclear build for energy security reasons that supplement the economics case. The EU taxonomy's inclusion of nuclear as a sustainable finance-eligible technology (under conditions) has improved access to green capital markets for nuclear operators.

In India, NPCIL's 10 new PHWRs (fleet mode construction) and the joint venture with Westinghouse for AP1000 units at Kovvada represent the most concrete new large nuclear build program outside China. The economics are supported by NPCIL's low cost of capital (government balance sheet), India's rapidly growing electricity demand, and the strategic importance of diversifying from coal and imported gas.

### Interview Questions

1. **Why is the discount rate so much more important for nuclear LCOE than for natural gas LCOE?**
   *Answer*: Nuclear is highly front-loaded in its cost structure: most of the capital is spent during construction, years before any revenue is received. Natural gas combined cycle plants have lower capital costs and shorter construction periods, but significant ongoing fuel costs. When you apply a higher discount rate, future revenues (decades away) are discounted more heavily, while near-term costs are relatively unaffected. For nuclear, which requires recovery of large upfront capital through revenues spread over 40–60 future years, a higher discount rate dramatically reduces the present value of those revenues, requiring a higher electricity price to cover costs. For gas, where fuel costs are an ongoing variable expense rather than a buried upfront capital charge, the discount rate effect is smaller. This is why a nuclear plant might be economically competitive at a 5% discount rate but uncompetitive at a 10% discount rate in the same market.

2. **What is the difference between overnight cost and all-in construction cost for a nuclear plant, and why does the construction schedule matter?**
   *Answer*: Overnight cost is the hypothetical construction cost if the plant were built instantaneously — it excludes interest during construction (IDC). All-in cost adds IDC, which accrues on the invested capital throughout the construction period. For a 7-year construction schedule at an 8% cost of capital on a $6B overnight cost project, IDC can easily add $1.5–2B to the total cost. If the schedule extends to 10 years due to delays, additional IDC accumulates on the full invested balance for 3 extra years, adding hundreds of millions more. This is why schedule overruns in nuclear construction are financially devastating: every delay adds both direct additional construction cost and accumulated interest on the entire invested capital.

3. **Explain zero-emission credits (ZECs) and why several US states created them for nuclear plants.**
   *Answer*: ZECs are state policy instruments that provide nuclear plants with an additional revenue stream on top of wholesale electricity market revenues, denominated per MWh of zero-carbon electricity generated. They were created because nuclear plants in deregulated US markets were facing closure despite having the lowest carbon emissions of any generating source, because deregulated wholesale markets price electricity on short-run marginal cost and do not reward carbon-free attributes unless a carbon price exists. New York (2016), Illinois (2016), and New Jersey (2018) created ZEC programs after specific plants announced imminent closure. The ZECs are valued at a fraction of the social cost of carbon and are designed to provide enough revenue to cover fixed operating costs while keeping the plants open. They represent a policy correction to a market failure — the market's failure to price carbon externalities — rather than a subsidy to nuclear specifically.

4. **What is the case for SMR economics, and what is the most important unresolved counterargument?**
   *Answer*: The case: factory fabrication and modular construction reduce site labor and construction schedule; smaller unit size lowers financing risk per increment; passive safety systems reduce staffing requirements; replication learning effects can drive cost reduction over many units. The most important unresolved counterargument is that economies of scale work against SMRs — per-kW costs for a 300 MWe unit are inherently higher than for a 1,200 MWe unit of the same design, and SMR learning effects must overcome this structural cost disadvantage. As of 2026, no commercial-scale SMR unit in an OECD country has completed construction at a verified production cost. The economic case rests on projections and learning rate assumptions that have not yet been validated by actual construction experience. The cancellation of the NuScale Carbon Free Power Project in 2023 after cost estimate revision is the most significant data point challenging the SMR economic case to date.

5. **What does a nuclear plant's "fixed cost recovery" challenge look like in a deregulated market during a period of low natural gas prices?**
   *Answer*: A nuclear plant's total operating cost includes fixed costs (staffing, maintenance, regulatory fees — roughly $15–25/MWh at 90% capacity factor) and variable costs (fuel, variable O&M — roughly $5–10/MWh). The plant's avoidable shutdown threshold is approximately the variable cost — it should continue operating as long as the market price exceeds variable cost, since fixed costs are incurred regardless of whether the plant runs. The problem is fixed cost recovery, not shutdown: the market price (which is set by the short-run marginal cost of the marginal generator — often a gas plant at $30–50/MWh during low gas price periods) may not be high enough to cover both variable and fixed costs, so the plant operates at an economic loss. Eventually this is unsustainable — the plant needs capital replacement and maintenance investment that cannot be justified economically. The result is deferred maintenance, announced closure, or a policy intervention (ZEC, PPA with a clean energy buyer) that provides additional fixed-cost revenue.

### Further Depth

- **IEA/NEA "Projected Costs of Generating Electricity"** — published by the Nuclear Energy Agency; the authoritative international reference for nuclear LCOE with multi-country data; free download at iea.org and oecd-nea.org
- **US DOE "Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Nuclear" (2023)** — detailed analysis of US nuclear economics, SMR prospects, and the policy conditions for new build viability; free at energy.gov
- **IAEA PRIS** (pris.iaea.org) — Power Reactor Information System; global nuclear plant operating data including capacity factors, energy availability factors, and outage history; free access
- **"The Economics of Nuclear Power" — NEA Nuclear Energy Agency** — foundational reference on nuclear project economics; covers capital costs, financing structures, discount rate analysis, and international comparisons
- **Lazard LCOE Analysis** (lazard.com) — annual free publication comparing LCOE across generation technologies; read the methodology appendix carefully before citing the numbers
- **EU Emissions Trading System data** (climate.ec.europa.eu/eu-action/eu-emissions-trading-system-eu-ets_en) — carbon price history and current price; essential context for the carbon premium value of nuclear
- **"Nuclear Power: A Very Short Introduction" — Maxwell Irvine** — concise non-specialist introduction that covers economics alongside physics; good orientation before the technical references
- **PyPSA** (pypsa.org) — open-source Python for Power System Analysis; used for electricity system modelling including nuclear capacity expansion and dispatch optimization; the open-source alternative to commercial tools like PLEXOS
