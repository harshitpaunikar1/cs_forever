# Practice Q&A — Advanced

Critical evaluation and strategic application covering Q141 to Q210.

## Q141. How should a portfolio leader decide which projects to kill?

**Level:** Advanced

Review projects against strategic fit, expected benefits, and delivery confidence at every gate. A project with weak benefits or declining confidence should be paused or killed, even if it has already consumed budget; sunk cost is not a reason to continue. Publish the criteria so decisions feel fair.

**Real-life applications:**
- Quarterly portfolio kill-list review
- Stage-gate no-go decisions
- Product roadmap pruning
- R&D pipeline rationalisation

**Key concepts:** `portfolio`, `sunk cost`, `stage gate`, `benefits`, `governance`

---

## Q142. How do you balance predictability and adaptability at programme level?

**Level:** Advanced

Plan at two horizons: a committed horizon (6-12 weeks) where dates and scope are firm, and a forecast horizon where scope is flexible. Communicate each differently to the business. This gives executives the commitments they want while allowing the teams to learn.

**Real-life applications:**
- SAFe PI planning with rolling-wave backlog
- Product roadmaps with committed quarter and directional year
- Capital programme with annual commit and five-year guide
- Rolling-wave marketing calendar

**Key concepts:** `rolling wave`, `commit horizon`, `SAFe`, `governance`, `predictability`

---

## Q143. How do you design governance for a multi-vendor programme?

**Level:** Advanced

Set a single programme board, clear escalation paths, shared KPIs, and named integration leads. Bake integration milestones into every vendor contract. Ambiguity between vendors is the top cause of delay in such programmes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Smart-city programmes with multiple suppliers
- Bank core-replacement with system integrators
- Defence programmes with primes and subs
- Infrastructure programmes with EPC contracts

**Key concepts:** `governance`, `integration`, `multi-vendor`, `contract`, `accountability`

---

## Q144. How do you evaluate whether to insource or outsource work?

**Level:** Advanced

Compare total cost of ownership, including hidden management effort, against strategic control and capability building. Outsource commoditised work; keep core capabilities in-house. Revisit the balance every few years as the market and firm change.

**Real-life applications:**
- IT outsourcing decisions
- Manufacturing make vs buy
- Marketing agency vs in-house
- Logistics 3PL decisions

**Key concepts:** `make vs buy`, `core capability`, `TCO`, `outsourcing`, `strategy`

---

## Q145. How do you design an effective project recovery plan?

**Level:** Advanced

Start with a fearless audit of scope, schedule, cost, team, and stakeholders. Reset scope to a minimum viable outcome, rebaseline, and agree a three-stage rescue: stabilise, re-plan, deliver. Communicate honestly; credibility recovers with transparency.

**Real-life applications:**
- Rescuing a stalled ERP roll-out
- Recovering a troubled M&A integration
- Rebooting a failed digital transformation
- Fixing a delayed infrastructure build

**Key concepts:** `recovery`, `MVP`, `rebaseline`, `stabilise`, `credibility`

---

## Q146. How do you assess strategic fit of a project to corporate strategy?

**Level:** Advanced

Map each project to explicit strategic pillars and test the contribution of expected benefits. If the contribution is indirect or uncertain, challenge the investment. Strategic fit is the first filter; everything else comes after.

**Real-life applications:**
- Strategy-to-execution traceability matrix
- OKR-to-initiative mapping
- Value-stream mapping
- Portfolio strategic-fit scoring

**Key concepts:** `strategic fit`, `OKR`, `benefits`, `portfolio`, `traceability`

## Q147. How do you manage opposing cultures in a merger integration project?

**Level:** Advanced

Acknowledge both cultures, preserve what works in each, and define the target culture in behaviours, not slogans. Involve middle managers as change agents. Monitor culture signals like attrition and engagement more than org-chart progress.

**Real-life applications:**
- Post-acquisition integration offices
- Joint-venture start-ups
- Carve-out and merger programmes
- Cross-border integrations

**Key concepts:** `culture`, `integration`, `change`, `M&A`, `leadership`

## Q148. How do you measure project success beyond on-time, on-budget?

**Level:** Advanced

Define success as delivered benefits (revenue, savings, adoption), quality of outcome, team sustainability, and strategic contribution. On-time and on-budget are necessary but not sufficient. Track benefits 6-12 months after closure.

**Real-life applications:**
- Benefits tracking for a strategic programme
- Adoption scorecards for new systems
- Customer-outcome metrics post-launch
- Innovation impact reviews

**Key concepts:** `success`, `benefits`, `adoption`, `outcomes`, `closure`

## Q149. How do you balance standardisation and local autonomy in global rollouts?

**Level:** Advanced

Standardise the "what" (process and data) and flex the "how" (local execution). Define non-negotiables and local choices up front. Over-standardising kills local ownership; over-localising destroys scale benefits.

**Real-life applications:**
- Global ERP template with local legal extensions
- Brand guidelines with regional adaptation
- Global HR processes with country variation
- Standard CRM with regional workflows

**Key concepts:** `global rollout`, `standardisation`, `localisation`, `template`, `governance`

---

## Q150. How do you decide the right level of contingency?

**Level:** Advanced

Base it on Monte Carlo analysis of risk-weighted duration and cost. Different confidence levels (e.g. P50, P80) give different reserves; pick based on risk appetite. Avoid the flat 10% that ignores project-specific risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- Monte Carlo cost reserve for a megaproject
- Schedule contingency in pharma trials
- IT programme P80 budget
- Infrastructure risk-weighted reserve

**Key concepts:** `contingency`, `Monte Carlo`, `P80`, `risk appetite`, `reserve`

---

**Real-life applications:**

**Real-life applications:**

## Q153. How do you manage the leadership pipeline for delivery roles?

Identify critical delivery roles, map succession candidates, and rotate emerging leaders across projects of growing complexity. Pair them with experienced coaches. Invest early; good PMs are grown, not hired.

**Real-life applications:**
- PMO leadership development

## Q154. How do you choose between a scrum and kanban approach?

**Level:** Advanced

Scrum suits batch delivery with predictable cadences and cross-functional teams. Kanban suits continuous flow with varying demand and interrupt-driven work. Mix them (Scrumban) when cadence matters but interruptions are frequent.

**Real-life applications:**
- Scrum for feature teams
- Kanban for support teams
- Scrumban for platform teams
- Team-level framework selection

**Key concepts:** `scrum`, `kanban`, `scrumban`, `flow`, `cadence`

---

## Q155. How do you design a PMO that adds value rather than bureaucracy?

**Level:** Advanced

Start by listing the decisions the organisation needs to make; design PMO services around those decisions. Measure the PMO on cycle time of decisions and quality of portfolio outcomes, not the weight of its templates. Retire templates that no one uses.

**Real-life applications:**
- Lean enterprise PMO
- Delivery-assurance PMO
- Value-enabling PMO
- Portfolio PMO

**Key concepts:** `PMO`, `value`, `decisions`, `lean`, `governance`

---

## Q156. How do you evaluate whether to pause a programme during a financial downturn?

**Level:** Advanced

Map each programme's benefits to cash impact, strategic necessity, and optionality cost of delay. Pause programmes with back-loaded benefits and high burn. Accelerate those whose benefits shore up the downturn response.

**Real-life applications:**
- Freezing long-term capex in a recession
- Pausing brand investments in a cash crunch
- Accelerating cost-saving programmes
- Deferring innovation programmes

**Key concepts:** `downturn`, `cash`, `strategy`, `portfolio`, `optionality`

---

## Q157. How do you run a structured lessons-learned programme across projects?

**Level:** Advanced

Capture lessons consistently, tag them by theme, and require new projects to consult past lessons at kickoff. Assign an owner who updates the knowledge base and curates themes quarterly. Lessons without circulation are wasted effort.

**Real-life applications:**
- Enterprise lessons-learned platform
- Post-project review standard
- Cross-project retrospectives
- PMO-run knowledge base

**Key concepts:** `lessons learned`, `knowledge`, `curation`, `PMO`, `improvement`

## Q158. How do you decide between fixed-price and T&M for a complex project?

**Level:** Advanced

Fixed-price works when scope is well-understood and risk is mostly on the vendor; T&M works when scope will evolve and the buyer wants flexibility. Fixed price usually costs more because vendors price in risk. Choose based on scope maturity and control needs.

**Real-life applications:**
- Fixed-price construction contract
- T&M software engagement
- Hybrid contracts for large programmes
- Capped T&M for regulated builds

**Key concepts:** `fixed price`, `T&M`, `contract`, `risk`, `scope maturity`

---

## Q159. How do you handle escalations without losing team trust?

**Level:** Advanced

Be transparent with the team before escalating, focus on issues not individuals, and share the outcome back. Escalation should feel like a team tool, not a punishment. Protect the team from upstream politics while being honest internally.

**Real-life applications:**
- Technical decision escalated to CTO
- Vendor escalation to procurement
- Sponsor escalation on priorities
- Cross-team escalation to portfolio board

**Key concepts:** `escalation`, `trust`, `transparency`, `leadership`, `governance`

## Q160. How do you quantify the cost of delay?

**Level:** Advanced

Estimate revenue foregone, cost of carry, and competitive cost per unit time. Build cost-of-delay into prioritisation with the Weighted Shortest Job First (WSJF) method. A feature that earns $100k per week in delay is worth ten times one that earns $10k.

**Real-life applications:**
- WSJF prioritisation in SAFe
- Cost-of-delay analysis in product
- Launch-window opportunity costing
- Regulatory-deadline penalty modelling

**Key concepts:** `cost of delay`, `WSJF`, `prioritisation`, `value`, `economics`

---

## Q161. How do you evaluate a programme's real risk, versus perceived risk?

**Level:** Advanced

Test risks with quantitative analysis where possible, red-team the plan, and examine the track record of similar programmes. Perceived risk is shaped by biases and recent events. Executive fear often misses low-visibility but high-impact risks.

**Real-life applications:**
- Red-team risk workshops
- External assurance reviews
- Historical benchmarking of similar programmes
- Quantitative risk modelling

**Key concepts:** `risk`, `bias`, `red team`, `benchmark`, `quantitative`

---

## Q162. How do you design incentives that encourage honest reporting?

**Level:** Advanced

Reward early reporting of bad news and accurate forecasting, not the colour green. Avoid incentives that tie bonuses to "on-track" status alone. The PM culture mirrors what leadership rewards and tolerates.

**Real-life applications:**
- Incentive plans tied to forecast accuracy
- Psychological-safety initiatives in delivery
- Peer-review for status reports
- "Red is fine" communication culture

**Key concepts:** `incentives`, `honesty`, `culture`, `reporting`, `governance`

## Q163. How do you plan for benefits realisation post-closure?

**Level:** Advanced

Write a benefits-realisation plan at initiation, name an owner outside the project team, and schedule reviews 3, 6, and 12 months after go-live. Link benefits to KPIs already tracked by operations. Projects that do not measure benefits rarely deliver them.

**Real-life applications:**
- Twelve-month benefits review for ERP
- Revenue-tracking for a product launch
- Savings audit after automation
- Adoption tracking after a rollout

**Key concepts:** `benefits realisation`, `post-closure`, `KPI`, `ownership`, `accountability`

## Q164. How do you handle major scope change after a contract is signed?

**Level:** Advanced

Invoke formal change control, quantify impact, and renegotiate terms transparently with the customer. Keep evidence of original scope and decision points. Avoid quiet absorption of big changes; it is the fastest way to a loss-making project.

**Real-life applications:**
- Contract variations in construction
- Change orders in defence projects
- Customer-driven scope shifts in consulting
- Regulator-mandated changes mid-contract

**Key concepts:** `contract`, `variation`, `change control`, `commercial`, `governance`

## Q165. How do you run an effective programme assurance review?

**Level:** Advanced

Use independent reviewers with a structured set of questions, separate assurance from line management, and report findings directly to sponsors. Track actions and close them. Assurance should be useful, not ceremonial.

**Real-life applications:**
- Independent quality reviews
- Gate-zero health checks
- External PMO assurance engagements
- Gateway reviews in public projects

Define explicit appetites for financial loss, brand, safety, and compliance risk, and translate those into project-level thresholds. Aggregate exposure so the portfolio view is visible to the board. Adjust appetite as market conditions change.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q167. How do you lead a project through a crisis?

**Level:** Advanced

Stabilise first: communicate, secure safety, protect customers. Create a short-term command structure, decide on a clear priority, and re-plan once facts are known. Lead with visible calm; the team mirrors the PM's tone.

**Real-life applications:**
- Data-breach response
- Safety incident on site
- Major production outage
- Supply-chain disruption

**Key concepts:** `crisis`, `command`, `communication`, `priority`, `leadership`

## Q168. How do you build a culture of continuous improvement?

**Level:** Advanced

Embed improvement routines like retrospectives and kaizen events, require visible action from each cycle, and celebrate improvements publicly. Track metrics that show whether the organisation is learning. Protect the time for improvement work.

**Real-life applications:**
- Weekly kaizen on shop floor
- Sprint retros that lead to action
- Monthly improvement forums

## Q169. How do you manage technical debt during delivery?

**Level:** Advanced

Allocate a fixed share of each cycle to paying down debt, track debt like a risk, and require business justification before knowingly increasing it. Ignoring debt compounds slow-down over time. Make the cost of debt visible to the sponsor.

**Real-life applications:**
- 20% capacity reserved for tech debt
- Debt backlog tracked like product backlog
- Architecture runway in SAFe
- Explicit "pay later" logging for shortcuts

**Key concepts:** `technical debt`, `architecture`, `capacity`, `trade-off`, `sustainability`

---

## Q170. How do you decide when to stop a pilot and scale?

**Level:** Advanced

Set clear success criteria before the pilot starts, including scale-readiness signals like adoption and cost-per-unit. If criteria are met and the risks are understood, move quickly to scale. If not, extend or stop cleanly; do not drift.

**Real-life applications:**
- Retail format pilot to rollout
- SaaS pilot to full deployment
- Process pilot to enterprise adoption
- Product pilot to new market

**Key concepts:** `pilot`, `scale`, `criteria`, `decision`, `rollout`

Embed security reviews at each lifecycle stage, involve security specialists from kickoff, and build threat modelling into design. Treat security as a functional requirement with acceptance criteria. Late security fixes are far costlier than early design.

**Real-life applications:**
- Security architecture review gates

Define gate criteria that reflect real decisions (kill, continue, pivot), not box-ticking. Require evidence appropriate to the stage. A gate that never says no is theatre.

## Q176. How do you govern hybrid delivery (agile + waterfall)?

**Level:** Advanced

Clarify which boundaries are agile and which are waterfall, and design integration points (release trains, milestones) between them. Report in a way that respects both cadences. Conflicts usually show up at integration; plan for them.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q177. How do you design a handover that actually works?

**Level:** Advanced

Treat handover as a deliverable with acceptance criteria, schedule it weeks before go-live, and run parallel operations for a period. Embed operations staff in the project team beforehand. The best handover is invisible because nothing changes except the label.

**Real-life applications:**
- IT operational handover
- Facilities handover in construction
- Clinical-trial handover to operations
- Manufacturing ramp-up handover

**Key concepts:** `handover`, `acceptance`, `parallel run`, `operations`, `readiness`

**Real-life applications:**

## Q179. How do you balance short-term delivery and long-term capability building?

**Level:** Advanced

Ring-fence capability investment (training, tooling, platforms) in the plan and protect it when things tighten. Track long-term metrics like cycle time and engagement, not just delivery dates. Short-term-only thinking erodes the ability to deliver over years.

**Real-life applications:**
- Dedicated platform capacity
- Training budget protection
- Architecture runway maintenance
- Long-term engineering practices

**Key concepts:** `capability`, `long-term`, `platform`, `sustainability`, `strategy`

## Q180. How do you respond when a sponsor wants you to hide bad news?

**Level:** Advanced

Offer to present the bad news together with options and a clear plan, but insist the news cannot be hidden. If pushed, push back in writing. The PM's credibility is a career-long asset; hiding material information is a short-term choice that permanently damages it.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sponsor-PM disagreement before steerco
- Executive seeking to hide overspend
- Commercial pressure on vendor to mis-state status
- Pre-board-meeting pressure

**Key concepts:** `ethics`, `transparency`, `sponsor`, `governance`, `credibility`

## Q181. How do you evaluate alternative delivery models?

**Level:** Advanced

Compare owner-led, managed service, joint venture, and partner-led models against control, cost, capability, and speed. Weight the model against the project's strategic importance. There is no one best model; there is the best for a given project.

**Real-life applications:**
- BOT vs EPC contracts in infrastructure
- Managed service vs internal delivery in IT
- JV vs solo delivery in new markets
- Partner-led vs owner-led product build

**Key concepts:** `delivery model`, `control`, `capability`, `strategy`, `contract`

---

## Q182. How do you plan capacity for a long, uncertain programme?

**Level:** Advanced

Use a rolling-wave capacity plan: firm for the near term, indicative for the medium term, placeholder for the far horizon. Revisit quarterly. Over-committing capacity to fictional dates causes the worst disappointments.

**Real-life applications:**
- Multi-year digital transformation
- R&D long-horizon portfolio
- Capital programme over several years
- Global rollout across many waves

**Key concepts:** `capacity`, `rolling wave`, `long-term`, `forecast`, `portfolio`

---

## Q183. How do you manage a programme when its sponsor leaves?

**Level:** Advanced

Secure an interim sponsor quickly, re-confirm the business case with the new leadership, and re-affirm priorities. Many programmes unravel in the weeks after a sponsor change because no one reaffirms them. Make the transition explicit and fast.

**Real-life applications:**
- CEO change mid-programme
- Sponsor promotion or departure
- Re-org affecting sponsorship
- M&A that moves the sponsor

**Key concepts:** `sponsor`, `transition`, `re-affirmation`, `governance`, `continuity`

Measure both outcomes (delivery, quality) and collaboration, share results with vendors, and link a share of reward to scores. Use peer vendor comparison carefully. Without sharing and consequences, scorecards become paperwork.

**Real-life applications:**
- SaaS vendor quarterly scorecard

## Q185. How do you mitigate key-person dependency?

**Level:** Advanced

Document critical knowledge, cross-train, and rotate roles. Identify single points of failure in the team and plan for them like any other risk. A single irreplaceable person is a project risk, not a compliment.

**Real-life applications:**
- Knowledge-transfer programme for senior engineers
- Role rotation in critical teams
- Documentation sprint for tribal knowledge
- Contractor transition plan

**Key concepts:** `key person`, `single point of failure`, `knowledge`, `rotation`, `risk`

## Q186. How do you decide between phased and big-bang rollout?

**Level:** Advanced

Phased rollout reduces risk and allows learning but prolongs the "two systems" period and total cost. Big-bang shortens the pain but concentrates risk. Choose based on tolerance for dual-running cost and risk appetite.

**Real-life applications:**
- Big-bang ERP cutover
- Phased CRM by region
- Wave-based platform migrations
- Parallel-run banking migrations

**Key concepts:** `phased`, `big bang`, `risk`, `cost`, `rollout`

**Real-life applications:**

## Q191. How do you use Monte Carlo simulation for schedule risk?

**Level:** Advanced

Model task durations as distributions, run thousands of simulations, and read the confidence curve. Report dates at chosen confidence levels (e.g. P50, P80) rather than single-point estimates. It exposes the hidden optimism in deterministic plans.

**Real-life applications:**
- Megaproject schedule analysis
- Oil and gas project scheduling
- Complex IT programme risk
- Event logistics risk analysis

**Key concepts:** `Monte Carlo`, `simulation`, `P50`, `P80`, `schedule risk`

## Q192. How do you evaluate whether a project is beyond saving?

**Level:** Advanced

Test three things: is the business case still valid, can you deliver an acceptable outcome, and does the sponsor have the appetite to continue. If any one is clearly "no," stopping is usually the right answer. Closure is a valid outcome, not a failure.

**Real-life applications:**
- Stopping a failing digital transformation
- Closing an innovation bet that did not work
- Cancelling a merged-then-redundant initiative
- Closing a market-entry project

**Key concepts:** `cancellation`, `business case`, `sunk cost`, `decision`, `closure`

**Real-life applications:**
- Mapping software delivery KPIs to revenue

**Real-life applications:**
- Global enterprise product launches

## Q195. How do you plan for cut-over at high-stakes go-lives?

Create a minute-by-minute runbook, rehearse it at least twice, and define explicit go/no-go and roll-back decision points. Assign a cut-over director distinct from the PM. Over-prepare for the first two hours after launch.

**Real-life applications:**

**Real-life applications:**
- UK Major Projects Authority reviews
- Defence programme gateways
- Mega-infrastructure gateway reviews
- Bank transformation assurance

**Key concepts:** `assurance`, `gateway`, `audit`, `megaproject`, `governance`

## Q197. How do you manage the commercial interface between PM and finance?

**Level:** Advanced

Share a single source of truth for budget vs actuals, align on forecasting methodology, and meet regularly. Disagreements usually come from different data views. Resolve them once and early.

**Real-life applications:**
- Monthly PM-finance reconciliation
- Shared project-finance dashboards
- Joint forecast reviews
- Integrated cost-performance reporting

**Key concepts:** `finance`, `reconciliation`, `forecast`, `data`, `governance`

## Q198. How do you design a project intake process that prevents overload?

**Level:** Advanced

Set capacity limits, score requests by value and fit, and reject or defer those that exceed capacity. Make intake a visible, standard process. Saying yes to everything is the single biggest cause of portfolio failure.

**Real-life applications:**
- Enterprise demand-intake board
- Product backlog intake gate
- IT request triage process
- Platform team prioritisation

**Key concepts:** `intake`, `capacity`, `prioritisation`, `portfolio`, `governance`

---

## Q199. How do you report on a programme that is cross-functional and cross-geography?

**Level:** Advanced

Standardise the report template, aggregate regional inputs into one view, and highlight exceptions. Do not drown executives in detail. Use the same language and colour coding across regions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Global transformation status pack
- Multi-regional launch dashboard
- Cross-BU programme reporting
- Portfolio roll-up for the CEO

**Key concepts:** `reporting`, `global`, `aggregation`, `exception`, `standard`

---

## Q200. How do you design a project for graceful failure?

**Level:** Advanced

Build in early kill criteria, small committed batches, and clear rollback plans. Reward the team for stopping low-value work. A graceful failure captures learning and frees capacity; a dragged-out failure destroys trust.

**Real-life applications:**
- Innovation-project kill switches
- Staged investment commitments
- Feature flags for easy rollback
- Short commit horizons

**Key concepts:** `graceful failure`, `kill criteria`, `batches`, `rollback`, `learning`

---

## Q201. How do you mature an agile transformation beyond ceremonies?

**Level:** Advanced

Focus on technical practices (automation, architecture), flow metrics, and customer-outcome improvement, not just ceremonies. Use maturity frameworks sparingly. Ceremonies alone produce agile theatre; outcomes prove real change.

**Real-life applications:**
- DevOps capability investment
- Product-centric operating models
- Continuous-delivery pipelines
- Cross-team guilds and communities

**Key concepts:** `agile maturity`, `technical practices`, `flow`, `outcomes`, `DevOps`

## Q202. How do you assess the total cost of ownership for a major system?

**Level:** Advanced

Include build, run, change, and end-of-life costs over 5-10 years. Account for hidden costs like training, integration, and vendor lock-in. TCO often flips the "cheapest option up front" calculation.

**Real-life applications:**
- ERP TCO analysis
- Cloud vs on-prem TCO
- Build vs buy TCO
- Platform selection analysis

**Key concepts:** `TCO`, `lifecycle cost`, `lock-in`, `run cost`, `hidden cost`

---

## Q203. How do you balance discovery and delivery in product projects?

**Level:** Advanced

Run dual-track work: a continuous discovery stream (research, prototyping) feeding a delivery stream. Protect capacity for both. Pure delivery burns capacity on the wrong features; pure discovery ships nothing.

**Real-life applications:**
- Dual-track agile in product teams
- Discovery sprints feeding roadmaps
- Research guild feeding squad backlogs
- Rolling hypothesis-testing

**Key concepts:** `dual track`, `discovery`, `delivery`, `hypothesis`, `agile`

## Q204. How do you handle political capital during a project?

**Level:** Advanced

Invest political capital where it unlocks critical dependencies, and avoid spending it on low-stakes debates. Track favours asked and given. Protect your credibility; you only have so much with each stakeholder.

**Real-life applications:**
- Negotiating budget in tight years
- Winning critical vendor commitments
- Securing critical stakeholder time
- Getting cross-BU support

**Key concepts:** `political capital`, `influence`, `credibility`, `negotiation`, `stakeholder`

## Q205. How do you structure a programme around customer outcomes, not deliverables?

**Level:** Advanced

Define programme goals as measurable customer outcomes (e.g. reduce claim-time by 40%) and break deliverables into steps against those. Governance rewards outcome progress, not deliverable count. Outcome framing keeps decisions focused on value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Outcome-based transformation charters
- Customer-journey-centred programmes
- Mission-oriented teams
- Outcome-based contracts

**Key concepts:** `outcome`, `value`, `customer`, `mission`, `governance`

---

## Q206. How do you design an operating model post-project?

**Level:** Advanced

Define roles, processes, technology, and decision rights for steady state before go-live. Pilot the operating model in the final project stages. Handing over a product without a working operating model guarantees post-go-live pain.

**Real-life applications:**
- Post-ERP operating model
- Target operating model for a new business
- Support model for a new platform
- Post-merger operating model

**Key concepts:** `operating model`, `roles`, `processes`, `decision rights`, `handover`

---

## Q207. How do you measure and improve flow efficiency?

**Level:** Advanced

Measure lead time, cycle time, and the ratio of active work time to total time in a value stream. Attack the longest waits first; they usually dominate. Flow improvements are often larger than productivity gains.

**Real-life applications:**
- Value-stream mapping in operations
- Kanban cycle-time dashboards
- Incident-response flow metrics
- Order-to-cash cycle improvement

**Key concepts:** `flow`, `lead time`, `cycle time`, `value stream`, `waste`

---

## Q208. How do you evaluate a new PM methodology for adoption?

**Level:** Advanced

Pilot in one context, define measurable outcome targets, compare against a control team, and review honestly. Methodologies are tools, not religions. Adopt what genuinely improves outcomes and drop what does not.

**Real-life applications:**
- Piloting SAFe in one business unit
- Trying OKRs in a sub-team
- Evaluating a new PPM tool in one programme
- Testing Lean Portfolio Management

**Key concepts:** `methodology`, `pilot`, `outcome`, `evidence`, `adoption`

---

## Q209. How do you coach a struggling project manager?

**Level:** Advanced

Diagnose whether the gap is skill, will, or context, and coach differently for each. Pair them with a senior PM for specific situations. Give them safe, progressively harder stretch assignments. Invest early; recovered talent compounds over a career.

**Real-life applications:**
- PMO coaching programmes
- Pairing junior and senior PMs
- Stretch assignments in a safe environment
- Post-project structured reviews

**Key concepts:** `coaching`, `skill`, `will`, `stretch`, `development`

---

## Q210. How do you leave a lasting improvement to the organisation after a project?

**Level:** Advanced

Aim for two kinds of legacy: the delivered outcome, and improvements to people and processes. Document what worked, train a successor, and feed lessons to the PMO. A great PM leaves the organisation stronger for the next project, not just the current one.

**Real-life applications:**
- Updated PMO templates based on lessons
- Trained successor PM ready for next project
- Published playbook from the programme
- Strengthened supplier relationships

**Key concepts:** `legacy`, `lessons`, `succession`, `PMO`, `continuous improvement`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Portfolio Kill / Keep Gate
**Purpose:** Decide whether a project should continue, be paused, or be killed.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Strategic fit + Benefits + Confidence -> Continue / Re-scope / Kill
```
Components explained:
- Strategic fit: does the work still matter?
- Benefits: are the expected outcomes still worth it?
- Delivery confidence: can the team still deliver credibly?

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A digital transformation portfolio has 18 projects. Two show weak strategic fit and declining delivery confidence after vendor slippage. The gate kills them, freeing capacity for the three programmes that support the board's current priorities.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When portfolio capacity is tight and everything claims priority.

### Rolling-Wave Commit Horizon
**Purpose:** Separate firm commitments from directional forecasts.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Now -> committed horizon -> forecast horizon -> long-range direction
```
Components explained:
- Commit horizon: dates and scope are fixed.
- Forecast horizon: direction is visible but details can shift.
- Rolling wave: replan as learning improves.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A product programme locks the next 8 weeks but keeps the following quarter directional. That lets the team promise reliably without freezing the roadmap too early.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When executives want certainty that the team cannot honestly provide.

### Multi-Vendor Governance
**Purpose:** Keep multiple suppliers aligned to one outcome.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Vendor A + Vendor B + Vendor C -> one board -> one integration plan
```
Components explained:
- Shared KPIs: all vendors measured on the same outcome.
- Integration lead: one accountable person for the seams.
- Contract hooks: milestones tied to integration, not just output.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A bank core replacement fails when each vendor optimizes its own scope. A single programme board and milestone-linked contracts force the system view back into the room.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When blame can be pushed across vendor boundaries.

### Make vs Buy / Outsource vs Insource
**Purpose:** Decide which work belongs inside the firm.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Commoditised? -> yes -> buy
Core capability? -> yes -> keep in-house
```
Components explained:
- TCO: total cost including coordination and rework.
- Core capability: a strategic skill worth keeping.
- Control: how much oversight the business needs.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A company outsources help-desk commodity work but keeps architecture and customer analytics in-house because those shape competitive advantage.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When someone wants to outsource a capability that actually defines the business.

### Project Recovery and Rebaseline
**Purpose:** Rescue a programme without pretending the original plan still works.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Audit -> stabilise -> rebaseline -> deliver
```
Components explained:
- Fearless audit: scope, schedule, cost, team, stakeholders.
- MVP reset: cut to the minimum viable outcome.
- Rebaseline: reset the plan to something credible.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A delayed ERP roll-out is re-scoped to only the business-critical modules for the first release. That restores credibility and gives the team a delivery path instead of a fantasy schedule.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When a project is late enough that the first plan no longer matters.

### Strategic Fit Traceability
**Purpose:** Connect delivery work to strategy, benefits, and owners.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Strategic pillar -> initiative -> deliverable -> benefit -> KPI owner
```
Components explained:
- Traceability: every project should map back to a strategic pillar.
- Benefits owner: someone is accountable for value after delivery.
- OKR link: the work should support a measurable strategic objective.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** An internal automation project survives review because it directly supports the company's cost-out objective and has a named benefit owner in finance.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When a project sounds useful but its strategic reason is fuzzy.

### Culture / M&A Integration
**Purpose:** Manage the human side of integration after a merger or major change.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Current culture A + culture B -> target behaviours -> new norm
```
Components explained:
- Behaviours, not slogans: define what good looks like.
- Middle managers: convert the target culture into daily practice.
- Attrition / engagement: leading signals of integration health.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A consulting firm acquires a boutique but keeps losing top people because the target culture was not defined. Reframing the integration around specific behaviours and decision rights stabilises the team.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When a merger looks fine on paper but people are leaving.

### Contingency and Monte Carlo Risk
**Purpose:** Set reserves based on the shape of uncertainty.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
P50 baseline -> P80 reserve -> risk appetite decision
```
Components explained:
- P50: median estimate.
- P80: more conservative reserve for high uncertainty.
- Monte Carlo: simulate many possible outcomes.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A data platform programme uses Monte Carlo to show that the P80 schedule is six weeks longer than the P50 estimate. The board accepts the reserve because the go-live is mission-critical.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the team is using a flat contingency without evidence.

### PMO Value Model
**Purpose:** Make the PMO a decision enabler instead of a template factory.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Decision need -> PMO service -> faster / better decision
```
Components explained:
- Portfolio decisions: what to start, stop, or continue.
- Assurance: independent review and health checks.
- Lean governance: fewer forms, more decision value.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A PMO reduces reporting volume and instead runs monthly decision reviews on the top ten risks and dependencies. The result is faster portfolio decisions and less theatre.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the PMO is being criticized as bureaucracy.

### Agile / Hybrid Delivery Model
**Purpose:** Pick the right delivery cadence for the kind of work being done.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Scrum <-> Kanban <-> Scrumban <-> Hybrid governance
```
Components explained:
- Scrum: predictable cadence.
- Kanban: continuous flow.
- Hybrid: use both when work mixes discovery, delivery, and control.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A platform team uses Kanban for production support and Scrum for product increments, with a programme-level hybrid cadence for reporting and governance.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the same methodology is being forced on very different work.

### Benefits Realisation Loop
**Purpose:** Ensure projects create measurable value after closure.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Delivery -> adoption -> outcome KPI -> benefits owner -> closure review
```
Components explained:
- Adoption: are people using the output?
- Outcome KPI: did the business result move?
- Ownership: someone owns the post-closure value.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A customer service automation project is closed only after call handling time and customer satisfaction improve, not merely after launch.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When delivery is done but the value is not yet real.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| Don't | Do |
|-----|-----|
| Keep projects alive because they already consumed money | Kill or re-scope based on current strategic fit and benefit confidence |
| Report progress without stating the decision it supports | Tie every report to an owner, a risk, or a portfolio decision |
| Outsource a core capability just because it is cheaper this quarter | Compare TCO, control, and the value of keeping the capability in-house |
| Treat contingency as a flat rule of thumb | Use P50/P80 thinking or Monte Carlo to set reserves |
| Call a project successful at go-live and stop measuring | Track adoption and benefits realisation after closure |
| Force one delivery method on every kind of work | Match scrum, kanban, or hybrid governance to the work profile |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Portfolio Kill Review
**Situation:** A portfolio board reviews 14 initiatives. Three are green, six are amber, and five are red. Two red items still have senior sponsors, but their expected benefit scores fell from 8/10 to 4/10 after market changes.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Portfolio kill / keep gate + strategic fit.

**Analysis:** The key question is not how much has already been spent; it is whether the project still earns future attention. If the benefit score drops below the threshold and delivery confidence remains weak, the project should be paused or killed.

**Decision rule:** If strategic fit < 6/10 and delivery confidence < 6/10, kill or pause. If fit is 6-8/10, re-scope. If both are above 8/10, continue.

**Action:** Convert the board discussion into an explicit kill list, a re-scope list, and a protected-funding list.

### Scenario 2: Contingency and Schedule Risk
**Situation:** A platform cut-over is estimated at 20 weeks. Monte Carlo simulation shows P50 at 20 weeks, P80 at 27 weeks, and a 12% chance of a major go-live slip if contingency is set at only 5%.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Monte Carlo contingency + risk appetite.

**Analysis:** A flat 5% reserve is too weak if the P80 point is seven weeks above baseline. If the business can tolerate delay, use the P80 reserve. If the go-live is mission-critical, build a staged cutover with rollback.

**Decision rule:** If P80 - P50 > 10% of schedule, do not use a flat reserve. If the gap is 5-10%, keep a modest reserve and monitor weekly. If the gap is under 5%, a small reserve is acceptable.

**Action:** Present the board with a P50/P80 range, a contingency draw plan, and a go/no-go cut-over checklist.

### Scenario 3: Make vs Buy for a Delivery Capability
**Situation:** A business can outsource a reporting workstream for $450k per year or build an internal analytics pod for $620k per year. The outsourced option is cheaper, but the capability touches strategic customer segmentation and needs tight control.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Make vs buy + core capability + TCO.

**Analysis:** If the reporting layer is commoditised, outsourcing makes sense. But if the capability drives strategic decisions, hidden coordination cost and control risk may outweigh the price gap. The right answer depends on whether the work is a commodity or a differentiator.

**Decision rule:** If core capability score > 7/10, keep in-house. If core capability score < 4/10 and TCO is materially lower, outsource. If in the middle, use a hybrid model.

**Action:** Split the work into commodity reporting and strategic analytics, outsource the former, and keep the latter in-house.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build a portfolio register that records strategic fit, benefit score, confidence, owner, and next gate for every initiative.
2. Define kill criteria and stage gates before the project starts, then publish them to sponsors.
3. Create a decision tree for make-vs-buy that includes TCO, control, and core capability score.
4. Set a rebaseline trigger and a rescue playbook for any project that slips beyond tolerance.
5. Establish a benefits owner and post-closure KPI review for every major project.
6. Use Monte Carlo or P50/P80 thinking for major schedule or cost risks instead of flat contingency.
7. Run quarterly lessons-learned reviews and turn the outputs into process changes, not just a slide deck.
