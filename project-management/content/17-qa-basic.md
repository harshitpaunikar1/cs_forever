# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and concepts covering Q1 to Q70.

## Q1. What is a project?

**Level:** Basic

A project is a temporary piece of work that creates a unique product, service, or result. It has a clear start and end, and once the goal is reached the project closes. Projects are different from ongoing operations like running a factory or processing payroll.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building a new office tower
- Launching a mobile app
- Running a three-month marketing campaign
- Organising a product trade show

**Key concepts:** `project`, `temporary`, `unique output`, `scope`, `lifecycle`

---

## Q2. What is project management?

**Level:** Basic

Project management is the practice of planning, organising, and guiding work so a project delivers on time, on budget, and at the right quality. It uses repeatable tools like plans, status reports, and risk registers. A project manager coordinates people, money, and information toward a shared goal.

**Real-life applications:**
- Coordinating a software release across five teams
- Managing a warehouse relocation
- Overseeing a product launch
- Running a hospital wing opening

**Key concepts:** `planning`, `coordination`, `scope`, `schedule`, `quality`

---

## Q3. What is the triple constraint?

**Level:** Basic

The triple constraint is the balance between scope, schedule, and cost. Changing one usually forces a change in at least one other. Quality is often shown in the centre because it is affected by all three.

**Real-life applications:**
- Cutting scope when a launch date is fixed
- Adding people to hit a tight deadline
- Negotiating more budget for extra features
- Choosing which features to drop under a budget cap

**Key concepts:** `scope`, `schedule`, `cost`, `quality`, `trade-off`

---

## Q4. What is a project charter?

**Level:** Basic

A project charter is a short document that formally authorises the project and names the project manager. It captures the goal, high-level scope, sponsor, and success criteria. Without a charter, teams often debate basics for weeks.

**Real-life applications:**
- Starting a new product initiative
- Kicking off a regulatory compliance project
- Launching an internal restructuring programme
- Authorising a capital investment project

**Key concepts:** `charter`, `sponsor`, `authorisation`, `success criteria`, `initiation`

---

## Q5. Who is a project sponsor?

**Level:** Basic

The sponsor is the senior leader who funds the project and owns its business outcome. They approve major decisions, unblock issues the PM cannot solve alone, and champion the project with peers. Every project needs exactly one accountable sponsor.

**Real-life applications:**
- A VP sponsoring a CRM rollout
- A CFO sponsoring a finance-system upgrade
- A plant manager sponsoring a safety programme
- A head of marketing sponsoring a brand refresh

**Key concepts:** `sponsor`, `accountability`, `funding`, `escalation`, `governance`

---

## Q6. What is scope?

**Level:** Basic

Scope is the sum of everything the project will deliver. It includes features, outputs, work, and excludes anything outside those boundaries. Clear scope is the basis for estimating time and cost.

**Real-life applications:**
- Defining app features for version 1
- Listing rooms included in an office renovation
- Specifying systems covered in an audit
- Agreeing deliverables for a consulting engagement

**Key concepts:** `scope`, `deliverable`, `boundary`, `inclusion`, `exclusion`

---

## Q7. What is scope creep?

**Level:** Basic

Scope creep is the slow addition of extra work to a project without adjusting time or budget. It usually happens through many small requests that individually seem harmless. Over time these additions can break the schedule.

**Real-life applications:**
- "Just one more" dashboard chart each week
- Extra rooms added to a renovation
- Additional reports requested during testing
- Unplanned integrations added mid-project

**Key concepts:** `scope creep`, `change control`, `baseline`, `trade-off`, `risk`

---

## Q8. What is a work breakdown structure (WBS)?

**Level:** Basic

A WBS is a tree diagram that breaks the project scope into progressively smaller pieces until each piece is small enough to estimate and assign. It makes the whole project visible on one page. Each box is a deliverable, not an activity.

**Real-life applications:**
- Breaking a software release into modules
- Splitting a building into floors and rooms
- Decomposing an event into venue, speakers, catering
- Structuring a research project into phases

**Key concepts:** `WBS`, `decomposition`, `deliverable`, `planning`, `work package`

---

## Q9. What is a milestone?

**Level:** Basic

A milestone is a named point in the schedule that marks a significant achievement, such as "design approved" or "go-live." Milestones have zero duration and are used to communicate progress to stakeholders. They are the most-watched dates in a plan.

**Real-life applications:**
- "Beta launch" in a software project
- "Foundation poured" in construction
- "Regulatory filing complete" in pharma
- "Contract signed" in procurement

**Key concepts:** `milestone`, `schedule`, `progress`, `reporting`, `gate`

---

## Q10. What is a deliverable?

**Level:** Basic

A deliverable is a tangible output produced by the project, such as a report, a piece of software, or a building. Each deliverable has acceptance criteria so the team knows when it is truly done. Projects are judged on the quality of their deliverables.

**Real-life applications:**
- A trained call-centre team
- A working mobile app
- A signed vendor contract
- A published research paper

**Key concepts:** `deliverable`, `output`, `acceptance criteria`, `scope`, `completion`

---

## Q11. What is a stakeholder?

**Level:** Basic

A stakeholder is any person or group that is affected by or can influence the project. They include customers, sponsors, team members, regulators, and neighbours of a construction site. Ignoring even one powerful stakeholder can derail a project.

**Real-life applications:**
- Residents near a new airport runway
- Compliance officers on a fintech project
- End users of a new internal tool
- Suppliers to a factory upgrade

**Key concepts:** `stakeholder`, `influence`, `interest`, `communication`, `engagement`

---

## Q12. What is a risk?

**Level:** Basic

A risk is an uncertain event that, if it happens, would affect the project for better or worse. Most risks discussed in projects are negative, like a vendor failing to deliver. Good projects name risks early and plan how to handle them.

**Real-life applications:**
- Key developer leaving the company
- Permit approval delayed
- Currency rate swing affecting imports
- New regulation released mid-project

**Key concepts:** `risk`, `probability`, `impact`, `uncertainty`, `mitigation`

---

## Q13. What is a risk register?

**Level:** Basic

A risk register is a living list of known risks with owners, scores, and planned responses. It is reviewed regularly, usually weekly. Risks that go unrecorded tend to hit hardest.

**Real-life applications:**
- Bank compliance project risk log
- Construction project risk matrix
- IT migration risk tracker
- Clinical trial risk dossier

**Key concepts:** `risk register`, `owner`, `probability`, `impact`, `response`

---

## Q14. What is mitigation?

**Level:** Basic

Mitigation is any action taken to reduce the chance or impact of a risk. It is different from contingency, which kicks in only if the risk actually happens. Mitigation is about prevention.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running load tests before launch
- Training a backup person for a key role
- Signing a secondary supplier
- Adding fire detection to a warehouse

**Key concepts:** `mitigation`, `prevention`, `risk response`, `control`, `planning`

---

## Q15. What is contingency?

**Level:** Basic

Contingency is a reserve, usually of time or money, set aside to deal with known but uncertain risks. It is used only when the risk actually materialises. Contingency is different from mitigation which tries to prevent the risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- 10% budget reserve for overruns
- Two weeks of schedule buffer
- Backup inventory in case of supplier outage
- Standby generator for power cuts

**Key concepts:** `contingency`, `reserve`, `buffer`, `risk`, `response`

---

## Q16. What is a Gantt chart?

**Level:** Basic

A Gantt chart is a bar chart showing each task as a horizontal bar across a timeline. It shows when tasks start, finish, and how they overlap. It is the most common way to visualise a project schedule.

**Real-life applications:**
- Planning a product launch over 6 months
- Tracking a construction project
- Scheduling an event setup
- Managing a research programme

**Key concepts:** `Gantt chart`, `schedule`, `task`, `duration`, `dependency`

---

## Q17. What is a dependency?

**Level:** Basic

A dependency is a relationship between two tasks where one cannot start or finish until the other is done. The most common type is finish-to-start. Missing a dependency in planning is a classic cause of delays.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pouring concrete before framing
- Finishing design before coding
- Getting approval before spending
- Booking venue before sending invites

**Key concepts:** `dependency`, `predecessor`, `successor`, `sequence`, `schedule`

---

## Q18. What is slack or float?

**Level:** Basic

Slack is the amount of time a task can slip without delaying the overall project. Tasks with zero slack are on the critical path. Knowing slack helps a PM decide where a delay is tolerable.

**Real-life applications:**
- Delaying a non-critical report by a day
- Absorbing a minor design rework
- Shifting non-blocking training tasks
- Rescheduling a stakeholder demo

**Key concepts:** `slack`, `float`, `critical path`, `schedule`, `buffer`

---

## Q19. What is the critical path?

**Level:** Basic

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks in a project. Its length equals the shortest possible project duration. Any delay on this chain delays the project.

**Real-life applications:**
- Identifying long-lead equipment in construction
- Finding the slowest test cycle in software
- Spotting permit delays in a real-estate project
- Managing drug approval steps in pharma

**Key concepts:** `critical path`, `duration`, `dependency`, `schedule`, `planning`

---

## Q20. What is a baseline?

**Level:** Basic

A baseline is the approved version of a plan, held as the reference against which actual progress is measured. Teams usually baseline scope, schedule, and cost at the end of planning. A baseline only changes through formal approval.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing actual cost to original budget
- Measuring slippage against planned dates
- Reporting scope changes vs baseline
- Auditing a finished project vs plan

**Key concepts:** `baseline`, `reference`, `variance`, `change control`, `plan`

---

## Q21. What is variance?

**Level:** Basic

Variance is the difference between planned and actual performance. Cost variance compares planned spend to actual spend, and schedule variance compares planned progress to actual. Tracking variance is the heart of project control.

**Real-life applications:**
- Cost variance of $20,000 over budget
- Schedule variance of two weeks late
- Scope variance after extra requests
- Quality variance via defect counts

**Key concepts:** `variance`, `cost`, `schedule`, `baseline`, `control`

---

## Q22. What is a kickoff meeting?

**Level:** Basic

A kickoff is the first team meeting where the PM aligns everyone on goals, roles, and the plan. It sets tone and expectations for the rest of the project. Good kickoffs are short, crisp, and action-oriented.

**Real-life applications:**
- Starting a 6-month ERP rollout
- Opening a new client engagement
- Beginning a product launch project
- Launching a compliance programme

**Key concepts:** `kickoff`, `alignment`, `roles`, `initiation`, `team`

---

## Q23. What is a status report?

**Level:** Basic

A status report is a short, regular summary of progress, risks, and issues. Weekly is the most common cadence. It keeps stakeholders informed without another meeting.

**Real-life applications:**
- Weekly RAG reports to an IT steering board
- Monthly progress note to a client sponsor
- Daily stand-up notes in a sprint
- Quarterly report to the board

**Key concepts:** `status`, `reporting`, `RAG`, `communication`, `cadence`

---

## Q24. What are RAG statuses?

**Level:** Basic

RAG stands for Red, Amber, Green, a colour code for project health. Green means on track, amber means at risk, and red means off track. RAG makes status scannable at a glance.

**Real-life applications:**
- Portfolio dashboards for executives
- Weekly project scorecards
- Risk matrix colouring
- Steering committee decks

**Key concepts:** `RAG`, `status`, `health`, `reporting`, `dashboard`

---

## Q25. What is quality?

**Level:** Basic

In projects, quality means the deliverable meets its agreed acceptance criteria and is fit for its intended use. Quality is not "the best possible"; it is "good enough for the stated purpose." Clear criteria up front avoid arguments at handover.

**Real-life applications:**
- Meeting medical safety thresholds
- Hitting automotive tolerance specs
- Satisfying regulator-mandated checks
- Passing user acceptance testing

**Key concepts:** `quality`, `acceptance criteria`, `fit for purpose`, `standards`, `testing`

---

## Q26. What is a defect?

**Level:** Basic

A defect is any flaw in a deliverable that fails to meet acceptance criteria. Defects found late cost far more than defects found early. Tracking defect counts over time shows quality trends.

**Real-life applications:**
- Bugs logged in a software release
- Cracks in concrete found at inspection
- Missing fields in a report
- Typos in printed marketing material

**Key concepts:** `defect`, `bug`, `quality`, `rework`, `inspection`

---

## Q27. What is rework?

**Level:** Basic

Rework is effort spent redoing work that was already thought to be finished, usually because of defects or missed requirements. Rework destroys budget and morale. Minimising rework is a core quality goal.

**Real-life applications:**
- Reprinting brochures after a typo
- Rewriting code that failed testing
- Repouring a concrete slab
- Reshooting a damaged marketing video

**Key concepts:** `rework`, `defect`, `cost`, `quality`, `waste`

---

## Q28. What is an agile sprint?

**Level:** Basic

A sprint is a fixed-length time-box, usually one to four weeks, during which a team completes a small slice of working product. At the end, the team demos and reprioritises. Sprints deliver frequent feedback.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two-week sprints in a fintech team
- One-week design sprints in a startup
- Four-week iterations in hardware prototyping
- Monthly release trains in enterprise IT

**Key concepts:** `sprint`, `iteration`, `agile`, `time-box`, `increment`

---

## Q29. What is a backlog?

**Level:** Basic

A backlog is an ordered list of work items waiting to be done, with the most valuable item on top. The team picks from the top each sprint. A well-kept backlog is the single source of truth for upcoming work.

**Real-life applications:**
- Product backlog in a SaaS team
- Support ticket queue
- Content publishing pipeline
- Maintenance task list

**Key concepts:** `backlog`, `priority`, `user story`, `agile`, `product`

---

## Q30. What is a user story?

**Level:** Basic

A user story is a small unit of work written from the user's point of view, usually "As a [user], I want [goal], so that [benefit]." It focuses the team on value, not tasks. Each story is small enough to finish in one sprint.

**Real-life applications:**
- "As a shopper, I want to save items for later..."
- "As an admin, I want to reset passwords..."
- "As a driver, I want to see route ETA..."
- "As a patient, I want to book an appointment online..."

**Key concepts:** `user story`, `value`, `agile`, `backlog`, `requirement`

---

## Q31. What is a stand-up?

**Level:** Basic

A stand-up is a short daily team meeting, usually 15 minutes, where each person shares what they did, what they'll do next, and any blockers. Standing up keeps the meeting short. Its goal is quick sync, not detailed problem solving.

**Real-life applications:**
- Daily dev team sync
- Morning toolbox meeting on a site
- Sales team daily huddle
- Support team handoff meeting

**Key concepts:** `stand-up`, `daily`, `sync`, `agile`, `blocker`

---

## Q32. What is a retrospective?

**Level:** Basic

A retrospective is a team meeting held at the end of a sprint or phase to reflect on what went well, what didn't, and what to change. It focuses on process, not individuals. Retrospectives drive continuous improvement.

**Real-life applications:**
- End-of-sprint reflection
- Post-launch review
- Monthly team improvement session
- Quarterly programme retro

**Key concepts:** `retrospective`, `improvement`, `agile`, `process`, `learning`

---

## Q33. What is a RACI matrix?

**Level:** Basic

A RACI matrix is a table that lists tasks or decisions against people, marking each cell R (Responsible), A (Accountable), C (Consulted), or I (Informed). It removes ambiguity about who does what. One person should be Accountable per row.

**Real-life applications:**
- Clarifying design sign-off authority
- Assigning module owners in IT projects
- Defining procurement roles
- Mapping marketing campaign duties

**Key concepts:** `RACI`, `roles`, `accountability`, `clarity`, `governance`

---

## Q34. What is change control?

**Level:** Basic

Change control is the formal process of evaluating, approving, and recording any change to scope, schedule, or cost. It prevents quiet scope creep. Every significant change should leave a paper trail.

**Real-life applications:**
- Approving a new feature mid-project
- Handling a design variation request
- Adjusting a signed contract
- Managing a client-requested timeline shift

**Key concepts:** `change control`, `CCB`, `change request`, `baseline`, `approval`

---

## Q35. What is a change request?

**Level:** Basic

A change request is a written proposal to modify the approved plan. It includes the reason, impact, and cost of the change. No change should go ahead without one.

**Real-life applications:**
- Request for an extra module
- Request to shift go-live date
- Request to swap a supplier
- Request for extended scope of work

**Key concepts:** `change request`, `impact`, `approval`, `baseline`, `control`

---

## Q36. What is a sponsor approval?

**Level:** Basic

Sponsor approval is the sign-off from the senior leader who funds the project, required for major changes or milestones. Without it, the PM lacks authority for big decisions. Good PMs keep approvals documented.

**Real-life applications:**
- Approving phase-gate exit
- Signing off budget top-up
- Authorising go-live
- Accepting project closure

**Key concepts:** `sponsor`, `approval`, `governance`, `milestone`, `authority`

---

## Q37. What is go-live?

**Level:** Basic

Go-live is the moment a new product or system is made available to real users. It is one of the most watched milestones in a project. Successful go-lives are carefully rehearsed.

**Real-life applications:**
- Launching a new banking system
- Opening a new store
- Releasing a mobile app to the app store
- Cutover to a new ERP module

**Key concepts:** `go-live`, `cutover`, `launch`, `readiness`, `milestone`

---

## Q38. What is user acceptance testing (UAT)?

**Level:** Basic

UAT is the final round of testing where end users check that a deliverable meets their real-world needs. Only after UAT passes does the team go live. UAT is about fitness for use, not bug-hunting.

**Real-life applications:**
- Bank staff testing a new claims system
- Doctors trialling a new records app
- Logistics team testing a route planner
- HR team validating a payroll upgrade

**Key concepts:** `UAT`, `acceptance`, `testing`, `user`, `sign-off`

---

## Q39. What is a feasibility study?

**Level:** Basic

A feasibility study is a pre-project analysis that checks whether a proposed project is worth doing. It looks at costs, benefits, risks, and alternatives. It is usually short and helps decide go or no-go.

**Real-life applications:**
- Assessing a new factory location
- Evaluating a SaaS platform adoption
- Exploring a new product line
- Studying a potential market entry

**Key concepts:** `feasibility`, `business case`, `evaluation`, `initiation`, `decision`

---

## Q40. What is a business case?

**Level:** Basic

A business case is a written justification showing why the project is worth the investment. It compares costs, benefits, and risks. Sponsors use it to decide whether to fund the project.

**Real-life applications:**
- Case for a new CRM investment
- Case for a new building
- Case for a marketing campaign
- Case for a process improvement programme

**Key concepts:** `business case`, `ROI`, `cost-benefit`, `justification`, `approval`

---

## Q41. What is ROI?

**Level:** Basic

ROI, or Return on Investment, is a measure of the financial benefit of a project relative to its cost. A simple formula is (benefit - cost) / cost. Sponsors often demand a minimum ROI to approve a project.

**Real-life applications:**
- Evaluating software automation savings
- Assessing ad campaign payback
- Justifying an equipment purchase
- Comparing two project options

**Key concepts:** `ROI`, `payback`, `business case`, `value`, `finance`

---

## Q42. What is earned value?

**Level:** Basic

Earned value is the budgeted cost of the work actually completed at a given point. It lets the PM compare planned progress, actual progress, and actual cost in one framework. It gives early warning when a project is off track.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking a construction project's progress in dollars
- Measuring a software project against budget
- Reporting to government funders
- Managing a large contract with penalty clauses

**Key concepts:** `earned value`, `EVM`, `PV`, `AC`, `variance`

---

## Q43. What is a Kanban board?

**Level:** Basic

A Kanban board is a visual tool that shows tasks as cards moving across columns like "To Do," "In Progress," and "Done." It limits work in progress to avoid overload. It is a lightweight way to see team flow.

**Real-life applications:**
- Software development flow
- Content publication pipeline
- Support ticket tracking
- Marketing campaign task board

**Key concepts:** `Kanban`, `board`, `flow`, `WIP`, `visual management`

---

## Q44. What is work in progress (WIP)?

**Level:** Basic

WIP is the number of tasks a team is actively working on at one time. High WIP usually hides problems and slows overall delivery. Good teams limit WIP to finish things before starting new ones.

**Real-life applications:**
- Capping dev tickets in a sprint
- Limiting active design drafts
- Throttling open sales opportunities
- Controlling active production runs

**Key concepts:** `WIP`, `limit`, `flow`, `Kanban`, `focus`

---

## Q45. What is a project lifecycle?

**Level:** Basic

The project lifecycle is the series of phases a project goes through from idea to closure. Common phases are initiation, planning, execution, monitoring, and closure. Each phase has its own deliverables and decision points.

**Real-life applications:**
- IT project lifecycle stage gates
- Construction lifecycle phases
- Product development funnel
- Research grant lifecycle

**Key concepts:** `lifecycle`, `phase`, `gate`, `initiation`, `closure`

---

## Q46. What is a stage gate?

**Level:** Basic

A stage gate is a checkpoint between project phases where leadership reviews progress and decides to continue, stop, or change direction. It prevents bad projects from running too long. Passing a gate usually requires specific evidence.

**Real-life applications:**
- Gate review before pilot launch
- Gate check before full rollout
- Research-to-development gate
- Concept-to-design gate

**Key concepts:** `stage gate`, `review`, `decision`, `phase`, `governance`

---

## Q47. What is an assumption?

**Level:** Basic

An assumption is something the plan treats as true without proof. All projects make assumptions; the issue is when they are not written down and turn out false. Good PMs log assumptions and review them regularly.

**Real-life applications:**
- Assuming a supplier can deliver in four weeks
- Assuming permit approval in 30 days
- Assuming stable currency rates
- Assuming team availability stays constant

**Key concepts:** `assumption`, `constraint`, `planning`, `risk`, `baseline`

---

## Q48. What is a constraint?

**Level:** Basic

A constraint is a limit placed on the project, such as a fixed budget, a legal deadline, or specific technology. Constraints shape the possible solutions. They are noted in the charter alongside assumptions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Fixed tax-year deadline
- Capped budget from a grant
- Mandatory technology stack
- Regulatory environmental limits

**Key concepts:** `constraint`, `limit`, `scope`, `planning`, `trade-off`

---

## Q49. What is scope baseline?

**Level:** Basic

Scope baseline is the approved scope statement plus WBS used as the reference for measuring scope changes. Once baselined, scope only moves via change control. Without a baseline, scope creep is unavoidable.

**Real-life applications:**
- Baselining app v1 feature list
- Freezing construction drawing set
- Baselining a grant deliverables list
- Locking campaign creative set

**Key concepts:** `scope baseline`, `WBS`, `reference`, `change control`, `plan`

---

## Q50. What is a pilot?

**Level:** Basic

A pilot is a small-scale test of a new product, service, or process before full rollout. It limits risk by letting the team learn in a controlled setting. Lessons from the pilot shape the main launch.

**Real-life applications:**
- Rolling out a new app to one region first
- Testing a new process in one warehouse
- Trialling a training programme with one team
- Piloting a new store format in one city

**Key concepts:** `pilot`, `rollout`, `learning`, `risk`, `phased launch`

---

## Q51. What is a rollout?

**Level:** Basic

A rollout is the process of deploying a project's output to all intended users, sites, or regions. It often happens in waves after a pilot succeeds. Planning and communication are key to smooth rollouts.

**Real-life applications:**
- Rolling out an ERP to 20 factories
- Launching a new app in 50 markets
- Deploying a new process across regions
- Rolling out a brand refresh to all stores

**Key concepts:** `rollout`, `deployment`, `phased`, `change management`, `scale`

---

## Q52. What is a capacity plan?

**Level:** Basic

A capacity plan shows how much work a team can realistically do over a period, given people and other constraints. It is compared to demand to find gaps. It helps say "no" with facts.

**Real-life applications:**
- Planning annual IT capacity across projects
- Agency staff planning across clients
- Factory line capacity forecasting
- Call-centre staffing plans

**Key concepts:** `capacity`, `demand`, `utilisation`, `planning`, `resource`

---

## Q53. What is utilisation?

**Level:** Basic

Utilisation is the percentage of available time a resource is actively working on project tasks. Very high utilisation usually means no slack for unexpected work. Typical healthy utilisation is around 70–80%.

**Real-life applications:**
- Developer time tracking reports
- Consultant billable-hour ratios
- Equipment usage rates
- Meeting-room occupancy

**Key concepts:** `utilisation`, `capacity`, `slack`, `resource`, `efficiency`

---

## Q54. What is a vendor?

**Level:** Basic

A vendor is an external company or person that supplies goods or services to the project. Vendors are managed through contracts and service levels. Poor vendor management is a common risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- Cloud hosting providers
- Equipment suppliers in construction
- Consulting firms in IT projects
- Logistics carriers in supply chain projects

**Key concepts:** `vendor`, `supplier`, `contract`, `SLA`, `procurement`

---

## Q55. What is procurement?

**Level:** Basic

Procurement is the process of buying goods and services needed by the project. It covers sourcing, negotiation, contracting, and payment. Good procurement protects budget and quality.

**Real-life applications:**
- Purchasing servers for an IT build-out
- Contracting a general contractor for a building
- Hiring a training firm for a rollout
- Buying materials for a manufacturing run

**Key concepts:** `procurement`, `sourcing`, `contract`, `vendor`, `cost`

---

## Q56. What is a contract?

**Level:** Basic

A contract is a legally binding agreement between two parties that sets out obligations, deliverables, and payment. In projects, contracts are used with vendors and customers. Changes usually require formal variation orders.

**Real-life applications:**
- Construction main contract
- SaaS subscription agreement
- Consulting statement of work
- Supply agreement with a manufacturer

**Key concepts:** `contract`, `obligations`, `terms`, `variation`, `legal`

---

## Q57. What is an SLA?

**Level:** Basic

SLA stands for Service Level Agreement, a formal statement of the service levels a vendor will meet, such as uptime or response time. Breaches trigger penalties or remedies. SLAs make expectations measurable.

**Real-life applications:**
- 99.9% uptime for a cloud host
- 4-hour response for IT support
- 24-hour ticket resolution for a helpdesk
- Delivery within 48 hours for logistics

**Key concepts:** `SLA`, `service level`, `vendor`, `penalty`, `measurement`

---

## Q58. What is a handover?

**Level:** Basic

A handover is the formal transfer of a project's output from the delivery team to operations or the customer. It includes documentation, training, and support arrangements. Clean handovers prevent long tails of support burden on the project team.

**Real-life applications:**
- Handing a new app to support
- Passing a building to facilities
- Transferring a campaign to BAU marketing
- Moving a system from vendor to internal IT

**Key concepts:** `handover`, `transition`, `operations`, `documentation`, `support`

---

## Q59. What is a closure report?

**Level:** Basic

A closure report is a short final document written at the end of a project summarising outcomes, actual cost, schedule, and key lessons. It gives leadership a clear view of what the project delivered. It is the PM's last major deliverable.

**Real-life applications:**
- End-of-ERP-rollout report
- Campaign wrap-up document
- Clinical trial final report
- Infrastructure project closeout

**Key concepts:** `closure report`, `outcomes`, `lessons`, `governance`, `closure`

---

## Q60. What is benefits realisation?

**Level:** Basic

Benefits realisation is the tracking of whether the promised business benefits of a project actually materialise after delivery. It usually happens 3 to 12 months after go-live. Without it, projects claim success without proof.

**Real-life applications:**
- Measuring actual savings from automation
- Tracking user adoption of a new tool

**Key concepts:** `benefits realisation`, `outcomes`, `value`, `measurement`, `closure`

## Q61. What is a programme?

**Level:** Basic

A programme is a group of related projects managed together to deliver a larger strategic outcome. Projects inside a programme share goals, risks, and governance. A programme manager oversees the whole set.

**Real-life applications:**
- Digital transformation programme
- Merger integration programme
- Smart-city infrastructure programme
- Regulatory overhaul programme

**Key concepts:** `programme`, `portfolio`, `coordination`, `strategy`, `governance`

## Q62. What is a portfolio?

**Level:** Basic

A portfolio is the full set of projects and programmes an organisation is running. Portfolio management decides which initiatives to fund and in what order. It links projects to corporate strategy.

**Real-life applications:**
- IT project portfolio
- R&D innovation portfolio
- Capital investment portfolio
- Marketing campaign portfolio

**Key concepts:** `portfolio`, `prioritisation`, `strategy`, `investment`, `governance`

## Q63. What is a PMO?

**Level:** Basic

PMO stands for Project Management Office, a team that sets standards, tracks projects, and supports PMs across the organisation. PMOs can be supportive, controlling, or directive. They aim to raise delivery consistency.

**Real-life applications:**
- Enterprise PMO tracking 100 projects

**Key concepts:** `PMO`, `standards`, `governance`, `oversight`, `portfolio`

## Q64. What is waterfall?

**Level:** Basic

Waterfall is a traditional project approach where phases run in sequence: requirements, design, build, test, deploy. Each phase usually finishes before the next starts. It suits projects with stable requirements.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building a bridge
- Regulatory compliance projects
- Hardware manufacturing projects
- Construction and infrastructure

**Key concepts:** `waterfall`, `sequential`, `lifecycle`, `planning`, `phase`

---

## Q65. What is agile?

**Level:** Basic

Agile is a family of iterative project methods that deliver working product in short cycles, with frequent customer feedback. It suits projects where requirements can change. Scrum and Kanban are common agile frameworks.

**Real-life applications:**
- SaaS product development
- Digital marketing campaigns
- Mobile app delivery
- Internal tool development

**Key concepts:** `agile`, `iterative`, `scrum`, `sprint`, `feedback`

## Q66. What is scrum?

**Level:** Basic

Scrum is an agile framework built around fixed-length sprints, defined roles (product owner, scrum master, team), and ceremonies like stand-ups, reviews, and retrospectives. It is the most widely used agile approach. Scrum works best with small, cross-functional teams.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two-week sprints in a dev team
- Cross-functional product squad
- Digital transformation teams
- Analytics team iterations

**Key concepts:** `scrum`, `sprint`, `product owner`, `scrum master`, `agile`

---

## Q67. What is a product owner?

**Level:** Basic

A product owner is the person responsible for maximising the value of the product by managing the backlog. They decide priority and accept completed work. In scrum, this role sits between customer and team.

**Real-life applications:**
- SaaS feature prioritisation
- Internal platform product owner
- Mobile app content prioritisation
- Analytics product roadmap owner

**Key concepts:** `product owner`, `backlog`, `priority`, `value`, `scrum`

---

## Q68. What is a scrum master?

**Level:** Basic

A scrum master is a servant-leader who helps the team follow scrum practices, removes blockers, and shields the team from interruptions. They do not manage people or priorities. Their success is the team's effectiveness.

**Real-life applications:**
- Facilitating sprint ceremonies
- Removing cross-team blockers
- Coaching on agile practices
- Protecting sprint focus

**Key concepts:** `scrum master`, `servant leader`, `facilitator`, `scrum`, `blocker`

---

## Q69. What is definition of done?

**Level:** Basic

Definition of Done is a shared checklist the team agrees describes when a piece of work is truly finished, including tests, reviews, and documentation. It prevents "almost done" debt. It usually applies to every user story.

**Real-life applications:**
- Code-reviewed and tested before merge
- Designs reviewed and approved
- Marketing asset legally cleared
- Hardware unit passed inspection

**Key concepts:** `definition of done`, `quality`, `criteria`, `agile`, `completion`

---

## Q70. What is velocity?

**Level:** Basic

Velocity is the amount of work an agile team completes in a sprint, usually measured in story points. Over several sprints, it becomes a forecasting tool. It is used for planning, not for comparing teams.

**Real-life applications:**
- Forecasting release dates
- Setting realistic sprint goals
- Spotting team overcommitment trends
- Planning feature roadmaps

**Key concepts:** `velocity`, `story points`, `forecasting`, `agile`, `sprint`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Framework 1: Charter-to-Closure Lifecycle
**Purpose:** Put the whole project on one timeline, from initiation to benefits realization.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Charter -> Scope -> Plan -> Execute -> Control -> Go-live -> Close -> Benefits
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: initiation - charter, sponsor, success criteria.
Component 2: planning - scope baseline, WBS, schedule, risk, budget.
Component 3: execution - deliverables, status, change control, quality.
Component 4: closure - handover, lessons, outcomes, benefits realization.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A hospital wing opening project needs a signed charter, a clear scope, staged rollout, UAT, and a closeout report that confirms safety and operational readiness. The lifecycle makes each gate explicit.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the project is starting to drift or the team is arguing over what happens after go-live.

### Framework 2: Dependency and Critical Path Map
**Purpose:** Show which tasks drive the finish date and where slack exists.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Task A -> Task B -> Task C -> Finish
           |          |
         slack       critical path
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: predecessor/successor logic - what must happen first.
Component 2: critical path - the longest zero-slack chain.
Component 3: float/slack - time available before delay matters.
Component 4: baseline/variance - how actual progress compares with plan.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A software release waits on security testing, data migration, and legal sign-off. The critical path tells the PM where to protect time and where extra effort will not move the date.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When dates are slipping and everyone claims their task is the bottleneck.

### Framework 3: Risk-to-Response Pipeline
**Purpose:** Convert unknowns into named risks with owners, scores, and responses.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Identify -> Score -> Mitigate -> Contingency -> Review
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: probability and impact - how likely and how bad.
Component 2: mitigation - actions that reduce likelihood or impact.
Component 3: contingency - reserve or fallback if the risk happens.
Component 4: owner - the person accountable for action.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A vendor might miss a compliance deadline. The pipeline gives the PM a mitigation path (early testing, alternate supplier) and a contingency (schedule buffer, reserve budget).

When to pull this out in a meeting: When risks are being discussed as vague possibilities instead of controlled actions.

### Framework 4: Control Cadence Stack
**Purpose:** Keep project governance, status reporting, and change control aligned.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
RACI -> CCB -> Status / RAG -> Dashboard -> Escalation
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: RACI - who is responsible, accountable, consulted, informed.
Component 2: CCB/change control - who approves scope changes.
Component 3: status rhythm - the review cadence and RAG system.
Component 4: escalation path - how blockers move upward quickly.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product launch team uses a weekly CCB to approve change requests, a daily stand-up for blockers, and a red/amber/green dashboard for leadership. That prevents hidden scope creep and confusion.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is moving, but nobody can explain who can change what.

### Framework 5: Delivery-Mode Selector
**Purpose:** Choose waterfall, iterative, agile, or scrum based on uncertainty, change rate, and coordination needs.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
High uncertainty -> agile / iterative / scrum
Low uncertainty  -> waterfall / sequential
High WIP pressure -> kanban
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: uncertainty - how much is unknown at the start.
Component 2: change rate - how often requirements will move.
Component 3: feedback speed - how quickly the team can learn.
Component 4: governance fit - how formal the controls must be.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A mobile app feature with unclear user demand should use agile or scrum; a facilities relocation with fixed permits can be run waterfall. The wrong mode creates either bureaucracy or chaos.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When people argue over methodology instead of the actual work conditions.

## 4. Formulas
No explicit numeric formulas appear in the source. The formulas below are decision heuristics synthesized from the source themes and marked [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Schedule Performance Index
Formula: `SPI = EV / PV`
Variables:
EV = earned value
PV = planned value
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the project is ahead or behind schedule in value terms.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 1 -> behind schedule
Value = 1 -> on schedule
Value > 1 -> ahead of schedule
Worked example with numbers: EV 80 and PV 100 -> SPI = 0.8. Decision: add focus to the critical path.

### Formula 2: Cost Performance Index
Formula: `CPI = EV / AC`
Variables:
EV = earned value
AC = actual cost
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the project is spending efficiently.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 1 -> over budget
Value = 1 -> on budget
Value > 1 -> under budget
Worked example with numbers: EV 80 and AC 90 -> CPI = 0.89. Decision: slow scope growth and review rework.

### Formula 3: Schedule Variance
Formula: `SV = EV - PV`
Variables:
EV = earned value
PV = planned value
Why this formula exists: It shows the schedule gap in absolute value terms.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> behind schedule
Value = 0 -> on track
Value > 0 -> ahead of plan
Worked example with numbers: EV 80, PV 100 -> SV = -20. Decision: investigate blockers on the critical path.

### Formula 4: Cost Variance
Formula: `CV = EV - AC`
Variables:
EV = earned value
AC = actual cost
Why this formula exists: It shows the cost gap in absolute value terms.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> over budget
Value = 0 -> on budget
Value > 0 -> under budget
Worked example with numbers: EV 80, AC 90 -> CV = -10. Decision: check procurement, labor, and rework.

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Software Release Slipping
Situation: A software release has 42 tasks. Testing, legal review, and data migration sit on the critical path, but the team is spending most of its extra time on non-critical UI polish.
Applicable framework/metric: Dependency and Critical Path Map.
Analysis: The critical path is being ignored, so schedule variance will keep widening even if total effort goes up. Slack on the UI work does not help the finish date.
Decision rule: If a task is not on or near the critical path, do not prioritize it ahead of gating work. If critical-path tasks are red, move resources immediately.
Action: Reassign engineers to the blocking tasks, freeze new scope, and publish a milestone-focused dashboard.

### Scenario 2: Budget Overrun During Office Relocation
Situation: A relocation project is 12% over budget, but the team says the move is still "on track" because the new office is half furnished.
Applicable framework/metric: EVM and Risk-to-Response Pipeline.
Analysis: The project may look active, but cost variance and CPI will tell the truth. If the team keeps spending without a mitigation plan, the overrun will continue.
Decision rule: If CPI < 1 for two reporting cycles, freeze discretionary spend and review the estimate-to-complete. If SV is also negative, protect the critical path.
Action: Update the risk register, negotiate with suppliers, and rebaseline the budget if the scope really changed.

### Scenario 3: Scope Creep in a Product Launch
Situation: Stakeholders keep adding "one small request" to a launch that is already late. The PMO is using a dashboard, but change requests are being approved informally.
Applicable framework/metric: Control Cadence Stack and Scope Baseline.
Analysis: The project has weak governance, not just weak execution. If change requests are not controlled, the baseline becomes meaningless.
Decision rule: If the change request count rises but the CCB approval rate is opaque, stop accepting informal scope changes. If RAG turns red, escalate.
Action: Reinstate the CCB, log every change request, and tie any new request to a time/cost trade-off.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Write the charter, sponsor, and success criteria before the team starts.
2. Baseline the scope, build the WBS, and map the critical path.
3. Create the risk register with owners, mitigation, and contingency.
4. Set the RACI, CCB, and reporting cadence before execution begins.
5. Choose the right delivery mode - waterfall, agile, scrum, or kanban.
6. Track quality, cost, and schedule with a dashboard that leadership actually uses.
7. Close with handover, lessons learned, and benefits realization instead of treating go-live as the end.
