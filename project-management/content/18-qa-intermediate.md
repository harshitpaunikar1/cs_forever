# Practice Q&A — Intermediate

Analysis, comparison, and applied reasoning covering Q71 to Q140.

## Q71. How do scope, schedule, and cost trade off in practice?

**Level:** Intermediate

Tightening one of the three usually stretches the other two. If a sponsor demands an earlier date, the PM typically cuts scope or adds cost to maintain quality. A clear conversation about this trade-off stops unrealistic promises and forces real decisions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Cutting features to hit a fixed launch date
- Hiring contractors to meet a client deadline
- Deferring modules when budget is capped
- Re-scoping a fixed-fee consulting deal

**Key concepts:** `triple constraint`, `trade-off`, `negotiation`, `scope`, `schedule`

---

## Q72. When should you re-baseline a plan?

**Level:** Intermediate

Re-baseline only when approved changes accumulate to the point that the original plan is no longer useful for tracking. Small variances are handled through normal variance reporting. Re-baselining too often destroys the ability to see real trends.

**Real-life applications:**
- Major scope addition approved by CCB
- Multiple critical-path tasks re-estimated
- A vendor change affecting schedule
- Regulatory change forcing new deliverables

**Key concepts:** `baseline`, `variance`, `change control`, `tracking`, `governance`

---

## Q73. How do you prioritise stakeholders?

**Level:** Intermediate

Plot each stakeholder on a power-interest grid and tailor engagement: manage closely those with high power and high interest, keep informed those with high power but low interest, and keep satisfied those with low power but high interest. The PM spends most time on the top-right quadrant. Tactics depend on quadrant.

**Real-life applications:**
- Ranking regulators, customers, and staff in a pharma trial
- Grouping community, council, and contractors in a build
- Splitting exec sponsors and end users in an IT rollout
- Sorting investors and team in a startup launch

**Key concepts:** `stakeholder`, `power-interest`, `engagement`, `prioritisation`, `communication`

---

## Q74. How do you break down a complex project?

**Level:** Intermediate

Start by listing high-level deliverables, then decompose each one into smaller work packages until each package can be assigned, estimated, and tracked. This creates a work breakdown structure. Stop decomposing when packages are small enough to complete in one reporting cycle.

**Real-life applications:**
- WBS for a new product development
- Breaking a data-centre migration into systems
- Decomposing a research programme into studies
- Structuring a multi-region rollout

**Key concepts:** `WBS`, `decomposition`, `work package`, `planning`, `deliverable`

---

## Q75. How do you estimate task duration?

**Level:** Intermediate

Use a mix of analogous estimates (based on past projects), parametric estimates (based on a rate like hours per page), and expert judgement. Three-point estimation (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) helps capture uncertainty. The people doing the work should always estimate.

**Real-life applications:**
- Estimating code features from velocity
- Pricing a build from past job data
- Estimating report pages per day
- Scoping training sessions per region

**Key concepts:** `estimation`, `three-point`, `analogous`, `parametric`, `expert judgement`

---

## Q76. How do you compare waterfall and agile?

**Level:** Intermediate

Waterfall plans everything up front and delivers once; it fits stable, well-understood projects. Agile plans lightly and delivers in short cycles; it fits projects where requirements evolve. Many organisations use a hybrid mix, waterfall for hardware and agile for software parts.

**Real-life applications:**
- Waterfall for a bridge, agile for an app
- Hybrid delivery for a smart-meter rollout
- Agile pilot inside a waterfall enterprise
- Staged waterfall with agile builds for an ERP

**Key concepts:** `waterfall`, `agile`, `hybrid`, `lifecycle`, `requirements`

---

## Q77. How do you handle a disengaged sponsor?

**Level:** Intermediate

Schedule a direct conversation to re-establish shared goals and agree the minimum engagement needed. If that fails, escalate via a peer, pause the project, or formally request a new sponsor. A project without an engaged sponsor rarely survives difficult decisions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Re-engaging a VP pulled into another initiative
- Requesting a replacement sponsor after reorg
- Formal escalation when sponsor unreachable
- Using a steering committee to proxy sponsor

**Key concepts:** `sponsor`, `escalation`, `governance`, `engagement`, `risk`

## Q78. How do you write a strong project charter?

**Level:** Intermediate

Keep it to one page and cover: goal, scope highlights, success criteria, sponsor, budget, high-level milestones, and key risks. Avoid jargon and get it signed before major spending. A charter you can hand to anyone in five minutes is a good charter.

**Real-life applications:**
- One-pager charter for CRM rollout
- Charter for a regulatory change programme
- Charter for a capital refurbishment
- Charter for a marketing launch

**Key concepts:** `charter`, `authorisation`, `success criteria`, `sponsor`, `initiation`

---

## Q79. How do you manage conflicts between stakeholders?

**Level:** Intermediate

Separate facts from positions, find the shared interest, and frame the decision in terms of project objectives. Document the decision and who made it. Escalate to the sponsor only when the PM has no authority to decide.

**Real-life applications:**
- Marketing vs legal on campaign claims
- Sales vs product on roadmap items
- Operations vs IT on deployment timing
- Finance vs engineering on cost vs features

**Key concepts:** `conflict`, `facilitation`, `decision`, `escalation`, `governance`

## Q80. How do you apply a risk response?

**Level:** Intermediate

Pick from four responses: avoid (change the plan to remove the risk), reduce (lower probability or impact), transfer (shift it via insurance or contract), or accept (live with it). Each risk in the register gets one response and one owner. Response choice depends on cost vs expected loss.

**Real-life applications:**
- Avoiding a risky vendor by dual-sourcing
- Reducing downtime with redundant servers
- Transferring flood risk via insurance
- Accepting minor cosmetic risks in prototypes

**Key concepts:** `risk response`, `avoid`, `reduce`, `transfer`, `accept`

---

## Q81. How do you measure project progress objectively?

**Level:** Intermediate

Avoid the "90% done" trap by using measurable completion rules: pages written, tests passed, modules deployed. Earned value is the most rigorous approach, comparing planned value, earned value, and actual cost. Objective measures keep reports honest.

**Real-life applications:**
- Lines of concrete poured vs plan
- Features in production vs planned
- Test cases passed vs written
- Users trained vs target

**Key concepts:** `earned value`, `objective`, `progress`, `measurement`, `reporting`

---

## Q82. How do you reduce scope creep?

**Level:** Intermediate

Baseline scope early, require a written change request for every addition, and show the schedule and cost impact of each request. Use a change control board to decide on non-trivial changes. Make the cost of "just one more thing" visible every time.

**Real-life applications:**
- Software feature change register
- Construction variation-order log
- Consulting out-of-scope notices
- Campaign creative change tracker

**Key concepts:** `scope creep`, `change control`, `baseline`, `impact`, `CCB`

---

## Q83. How do you build a realistic schedule?

**Level:** Intermediate

Start with a WBS, estimate each work package, map dependencies, and identify the critical path. Add contingency for known risks but not so much that everything becomes padded. Get team buy-in so owners commit to their dates.

**Real-life applications:**
- Gantt for a product launch
- Network diagram for infrastructure
- Release plan for a software product
- Build schedule for a factory line

**Key concepts:** `schedule`, `WBS`, `critical path`, `contingency`, `dependencies`

---

## Q84. What are signs a project is off track?

**Level:** Intermediate

Watch for slipping milestones, rising defect counts, burn rate exceeding progress, team overtime as a habit, and dropping stakeholder attendance. One signal alone can be noise, but two together is usually a red flag. Early action beats late heroics.

**Real-life applications:**
- Weekly status turning amber
- QA backlog growing sprint on sprint
- Costs at 80% when progress is 50%
- Key attendees skipping steerco

**Key concepts:** `signals`, `tracking`, `risk`, `variance`, `early warning`

---

## Q85. How do you allocate limited resources across projects?

**Level:** Intermediate

Rank projects by strategic value, consider dependencies, and publish a shared capacity view. When two projects need the same person, either the portfolio leader prioritises or the PMs negotiate. Avoid quietly double-booking; it delays both projects.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sharing senior architects across programmes
- Scheduling CNC machines across product lines
- Booking film editors across campaigns
- Allocating testers across release trains

**Key concepts:** `resource allocation`, `capacity`, `portfolio`, `prioritisation`, `negotiation`

## Q86. How do you run an effective stand-up?

**Level:** Intermediate

Keep it to 15 minutes, held at the same time and place each day, standing up. Each person answers three questions: yesterday, today, blockers. Detailed discussions move to after the stand-up with only the relevant people.

**Real-life applications:**
- Daily dev team sync
- Morning safety huddle on site
- Sales floor daily huddle
- Factory shift-change meeting

**Key concepts:** `stand-up`, `agile`, `sync`, `blocker`, `cadence`

## Q87. How do you handle under-performance in a team member?

**Level:** Intermediate

Address it early with a private 1:1 focused on specific behaviour, not personality. Agree clear goals and a short review period, and document the conversation. Involve HR when the issue persists or affects the team.

**Real-life applications:**
- Missed deadlines from a senior engineer
- Quality drop in a designer's output
- Disengagement in a team lead
- Consistent communication failures

**Key concepts:** `feedback`, `performance`, `1:1`, `HR`, `leadership`

## Q88. How do you hold a useful retrospective?

**Level:** Intermediate

Keep the focus on the process, not individuals, using a simple structure like "start, stop, continue." Limit to 60 minutes, pick two or three specific actions, and assign owners. Track whether last retro's actions were done before discussing new items.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sprint retros in scrum teams
- Post-campaign marketing retros
- Post-release engineering retros
- Quarterly programme retros

**Key concepts:** `retrospective`, `actions`, `improvement`, `facilitation`, `follow-up`

---

## Q89. How do you choose between agile and waterfall?

**Level:** Intermediate

Pick agile when requirements are uncertain, the customer can engage often, and small releases add value. Pick waterfall when requirements are fixed, the delivery is one-shot, and regulations require heavy up-front documentation. Consider hybrid for mixed deliveries.

**Real-life applications:**
- SaaS product: agile
- Bridge: waterfall
- Medical device: waterfall core with agile software
- Marketing site: agile

**Key concepts:** `agile`, `waterfall`, `lifecycle`, `requirements`, `fit`

---

## Q90. How do you handle a vendor who is slipping?

**Level:** Intermediate

Meet early, understand root cause, and review contractual obligations and SLAs. Work together on a recovery plan and document it. If slippage continues, escalate within the vendor's organisation or activate contract remedies.

**Real-life applications:**
- SaaS vendor missing feature date
- Construction subcontractor behind schedule
- Logistics carrier missing SLAs
- Consultant missing deliverables

**Key concepts:** `vendor`, `SLA`, `recovery`, `escalation`, `contract`

## Q91. How do you onboard a new team member mid-project?

**Level:** Intermediate

Pair them with a buddy, give access to documents, tools, and the current backlog, and set a two-week ramp plan with small deliverables. Block time for them to shadow key meetings. Expect lower output for the first sprint.

**Real-life applications:**
- New dev in a sprint mid-way
- Contractor joining a build
- Consultant joining a programme
- New support staff in operations

**Key concepts:** `onboarding`, `ramp`, `buddy`, `productivity`, `team`

## Q92. How do you use earned value to forecast completion?

**Level:** Intermediate

Use the cost performance index (CPI = EV / AC) and schedule performance index (SPI = EV / PV). A CPI of 0.8 means every dollar buys 80 cents of work; divide remaining budget accordingly. The forecast assumes future performance matches past.

**Real-life applications:**
- Construction cost forecasting
- Large IT programme financials
- Government contract reporting
- R&D grant spend forecasting

**Key concepts:** `earned value`, `CPI`, `SPI`, `forecast`, `variance`

---

## Q93. How do you handle competing priorities from multiple sponsors?

**Level:** Intermediate

Get the sponsors together, present the trade-offs in a simple table, and ask them to rank. Document the agreed ranking and share it widely. If they cannot agree, escalate to the level above both.

**Real-life applications:**
- Marketing and sales competing for platform features
- Operations and finance competing for tool budget
- Commercial and technical leads on roadmap
- Regional sponsors wanting different rollout orders

## Q94. How do you prepare for go-live?

**Real-life applications:**

## Q95. How do you define acceptance criteria?

**Level:** Intermediate

Write them in plain, testable language, one criterion per line. Each must be verifiable by inspection or test. Involve the customer in drafting them so there is no surprise at handover.

**Real-life applications:**
- "Login page loads under 2 seconds"
- "Concrete strength meets ASTM C39"
- "Report contains all required fields"
- "System supports 1,000 concurrent users"

**Key concepts:** `acceptance criteria`, `testable`, `verification`, `scope`, `quality`

## Q96. How do you manage a distributed team?

**Level:** Intermediate

Pick a small set of shared collaboration tools, define overlap hours, and standardise documentation. Rotate meeting times so no one region always has the worst slot. Visit in person when you can; one trip can pay back for a year.

**Real-life applications:**
- A dev team across India, Europe, US
- A design team across three cities
- Consulting team split across clients
- Global marketing squad

**Key concepts:** `distributed team`, `collaboration`, `tools`, `timezone`, `trust`

## Q97. How do you keep a risk register useful?

**Level:** Intermediate

Review it weekly, close risks that are resolved, and retire risks that have passed. Focus discussion on top-five risks only. A long, stale register is worse than a short, live one.

**Real-life applications:**
- Weekly PM risk review meeting
- Board-level top-risk report
- Site safety risk review
- Programme-level risk council

**Key concepts:** `risk register`, `review`, `cadence`, `prioritisation`, `governance`

## Q98. How do you estimate uncertain tasks in agile?

**Level:** Intermediate

Use relative sizing with story points or T-shirt sizes instead of absolute hours. Planning poker surfaces differing views and forces discussion. Calibrate using past stories the team has actually completed.

**Real-life applications:**
- Planning poker in a scrum team
- T-shirt sizing for new features
- Relative sizing in product roadmap
- Effort buckets in early discovery

**Key concepts:** `story points`, `planning poker`, `relative sizing`, `agile`, `estimation`

## Q99. How do you use the power-interest grid?

**Level:** Intermediate

Plot each stakeholder on two axes: power to affect the project and interest in it. High power + high interest means manage closely; high power + low interest means keep satisfied; low power + high interest means keep informed; low on both means monitor. The grid drives the communication plan.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sorting executives vs staff in a rollout
- Grouping investors vs contractors in a startup
- Categorising regulators vs neighbours for a factory
- Ranking unions vs suppliers in a reorg

**Key concepts:** `stakeholder`, `power-interest`, `grid`, `engagement`, `communication`

---

## Q100. How do you say "no" to a stakeholder request?

**Level:** Intermediate

Acknowledge the request, explain the trade-off in time or cost, and offer options: defer, accept with slip, or replace something else. Let data do the talking, not personal preference. Document the decision.

**Real-life applications:**
- Declining a late feature addition
- Deferring a non-critical reporting request
- Trading a new feature for an existing one
- Rejecting a scope add against fixed price

**Key concepts:** `trade-off`, `scope`, `communication`, `change control`, `negotiation`

## Q101. How do you measure stakeholder engagement?

**Level:** Intermediate

Track attendance at key meetings, response times to decisions, sentiment from one-on-ones, and the frequency of escalations from that stakeholder. Sudden drops in any of these suggest disengagement. Follow up quickly before it becomes a blocker.

**Real-life applications:**
- Steering committee attendance trends
- Sponsor decision turnaround time
- Survey pulses after milestones
- Frequency of ad hoc sponsor emails

**Key concepts:** `engagement`, `metrics`, `communication`, `stakeholder`, `early warning`

Build a matrix listing each audience, the message, channel, frequency, and owner. Match effort to stakeholder power and interest. Include a simple escalation path for urgent issues.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comms plan for a 50-site rollout

## Q103. How do you rescue a struggling project?

**Level:** Intermediate

Pause to assess honestly: scope, schedule, cost, team, sponsor. Shrink scope to the minimum viable outcome, re-baseline, and reset expectations with leadership. Communicate the reset plainly; stakeholders respect honesty over optimism.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pulling a delayed ERP back on track
- Recovering a stalled app launch
- Resetting a troubled construction phase
- Rebooting a cancelled-then-revived initiative

**Key concepts:** `recovery`, `reset`, `scope`, `communication`, `honesty`

---

## Q104. How do you negotiate scope with a customer?

**Level:** Intermediate

Frame choices in terms of value, cost, and schedule. Show which items fit within the agreed constraints and which do not. Offer two or three packages instead of one take-it-or-leave-it answer.

**Real-life applications:**
- Consulting SOW negotiation
- SaaS customer feature requests
- Custom build scope discussions
- Campaign deliverable negotiation

**Key concepts:** `negotiation`, `scope`, `value`, `options`, `trade-off`

## Q105. How do you allocate contingency?

**Level:** Intermediate

Base contingency on a risk-weighted view of uncertainty rather than a flat percentage. Common risks go into the register with named mitigations; the PM controls a smaller management reserve. Release contingency only with written justification.

**Real-life applications:**
- Construction 10% risk reserve
- IT project management reserve
- Pharma trial contingency pots
- Marketing campaign float

**Key concepts:** `contingency`, `risk`, `reserve`, `budget`, `governance`

## Q106. How do you track cost on a complex project?

**Level:** Intermediate

Split the budget into work packages, track commitments (PO raised) as well as actuals, and forecast weekly. Use a simple traffic-light dashboard. Finance and PM should reconcile numbers at least monthly.

**Real-life applications:**
- Cost control for an infrastructure build
- SaaS implementation cost tracker
- R&D grant expenditure reporting
- Multi-site rollout cost dashboard

## Q107. How do you handle a sudden requirement change from the sponsor?

**Level:** Intermediate

Log it as a change request, show impact on scope, schedule, and cost, and present options. Do not start work before approval; good intentions become bad over-runs. Keep the paper trail for audit.

**Real-life applications:**
- CEO asking for new reporting mid-project
- Regulator new mandate mid-build
- Customer escalating late feature
- Sponsor pivoting target market

**Key concepts:** `change request`, `impact`, `governance`, `baseline`, `scope`

## Q108. How do you plan for quality in a project?

**Level:** Intermediate

Write a simple quality plan: what standards apply, what checks happen when, who signs off what. Build inspections into the schedule, not at the very end. Set acceptance criteria with the customer before work begins.

**Real-life applications:**
- QA plan for a software release

- Construction inspection plan

## Q109. How do you balance thoroughness and speed?

**Level:** Intermediate

Identify which steps directly affect safety, money, or reputation; invest in those. For lower-stakes work, accept a minimum viable process. Revisit the balance every phase as the project matures.

**Real-life applications:**
- Light process for prototypes, heavy for production
- Extensive review of financial changes
- Faster checks on internal-only releases
- Tight controls for regulated parts

**Key concepts:** `rigour`, `speed`, `risk-based`, `trade-off`, `process`

## Q110. How do you document a project?

## Q112. How do you manage a rollout across many sites?

**Level:** Intermediate

Pilot in one site, capture lessons, and then roll out in waves. Maintain a central playbook and site-readiness checklist. Have a small hypercare team ready to support the first few days after each site goes live.

**Real-life applications:**
- 20-factory ERP deployment
- Multi-country SaaS rollout
- Nationwide retail refresh

## Q113. How do you run a change-control board?

**Level:** Intermediate

Schedule it on a fixed cadence, set a minimum quorum including sponsor, and require a completed change request for every item. Time-box discussion per request. Publish decisions promptly.

**Real-life applications:**
- Weekly CCB in a software programme
- Monthly change board in an operations project

## Q114. How do you handle a blocked decision?

**Level:** Intermediate

Name the decision needed, the options, and the impact of delay, then present to the next level of authority. Set a decision deadline. If the deadline passes, make the best reversible choice and continue.

**Real-life applications:**
- Stalled architecture decision
- Pending regulatory interpretation
- Unresolved vendor choice
- Deferred feature prioritisation

**Key concepts:** `decision`, `escalation`, `deadline`, `governance`, `progress`

Lock scope tightly in the statement of work and price every change formally. Track effort against each deliverable. Be clear that "helpful" unpaid work erodes margin and sets bad expectations.

## Q117. How do you estimate cost on an agile project?

**Level:** Intermediate

Estimate capacity per sprint in team-weeks and multiply by loaded cost, plus a contingency for uncertainty. Scope is the variable, so the customer chooses how many sprints to buy. Re-forecast every two or three sprints.

**Real-life applications:**
- Budgeting a six-month product build
- Funding an internal platform squad
- Cost planning for digital transformation
- Rolling-wave cost forecasts

**Key concepts:** `agile cost`, `capacity`, `contingency`, `forecast`, `budget`

## Q119. How do you select the right KPIs?

**Level:** Intermediate

Pick fewer than 10, and make sure each one drives a decision. Balance leading indicators (early signals) with lagging indicators (actual outcomes). Test them for gameability before using them.

**Real-life applications:**
- SaaS delivery dashboard

## Q120. How do you improve team velocity without burnout?

**Level:** Intermediate

Remove blockers, limit WIP, reduce meetings, and protect focused time. Measure outcomes, not hours worked. Velocity improvements from better flow are sustainable; from overtime, they are not.

**Real-life applications:**
- Cutting recurring meetings by half
- Introducing focus time blocks
- Reducing WIP in Kanban
- Removing a toxic dependency team

**Key concepts:** `velocity`, `flow`, `WIP`, `focus`, `sustainable pace`

**Real-life applications:**

## Q122. How do you handle a scope disagreement between the sponsor and users?

**Level:** Intermediate

Surface the disagreement, frame each option in terms of user value and business value, and propose a compromise where possible. Escalate early if it cannot be resolved. Do not let the team work to two conflicting definitions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sponsor wants cost savings, users want features
- Sponsor rushes launch, users need training time
- Sponsor prioritises reporting, users prioritise usability
- Sponsor wants new market, users want fixes

**Key concepts:** `scope`, `stakeholder`, `conflict`, `value`, `governance`

**Real-life applications:**

## Q127. How do you set up a war room?

**Level:** Intermediate

Bring decision-makers and subject experts to one physical or virtual space, define a clear goal and time-box, and hold daily or hourly syncs. Track actions on a visible board. Dissolve it as soon as the crisis passes.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Triple Constraint
**Purpose:** Make the core project trade-off visible: scope, schedule, and cost cannot all be fixed at once.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
          Scope
         /    \
    Cost ------ Schedule
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Scope - what deliverables are included.
Component 2: Schedule - when the deliverables must land.
Component 3: Cost - the budget or burn available.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting rollout wants to add two more modules without moving the deadline or budget; the PM can either cut scope, add cost, or negotiate the date.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When every stakeholder says their ask is non-negotiable.

### Work Breakdown Structure
**Purpose:** Decompose a project until it can be estimated, owned, and tracked.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Project
  -> Deliverable
    -> Work Package
      -> Task
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Deliverables - major outputs.
Component 2: Work packages - assignable chunks of work.
Component 3: Tasks - the smallest trackable pieces.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An ERP implementation is broken into process design, data migration, testing, training, and cutover work packages so each team has a named owner and estimate.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a project feels too big to estimate or staff credibly.

### Critical Path Method
**Purpose:** Identify the sequence that determines the finish date.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
A -> B -> D
 \-> C -/
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Critical path - tasks with zero float that drive the end date.
Component 2: Float - schedule slack on non-critical tasks.
Component 3: Dependencies - the links that constrain sequencing.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: If data conversion delays the UAT window, the critical path moves from build work to migration work, and the PM must protect that chain first.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When date risk matters more than feature detail.

### Earned Value Management
**Purpose:** Compare planned work, completed work, and actual spend in one model.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Planned Value -> Earned Value -> Actual Cost
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Planned Value - what should have been done.
Component 2: Earned Value - what has actually been completed.
Component 3: Actual Cost - what it really cost.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A transformation project may be on budget but behind schedule if actual cost is low and earned value lags planned value.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When leaders want objective evidence instead of status adjectives.

### Power-Interest Grid
**Purpose:** Decide how much attention each stakeholder needs.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
High Power / High Interest -> Manage closely
High Power / Low Interest   -> Keep satisfied
Low Power / High Interest   -> Keep informed
Low Power / Low Interest    -> Monitor
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Power - ability to block or accelerate the project.
Component 2: Interest - how much they care about the outcome.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: In an IT rollout, the CFO may be high power/low interest, the operations lead may be high power/high interest, and the end users may be low power/high interest.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When stakeholder management is becoming political.

### Risk Response Matrix
**Purpose:** Force a decision on what to do about each material risk.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Avoid | Reduce | Transfer | Accept
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Avoid - remove the risky work.
Component 2: Reduce - lower likelihood or impact.
Component 3: Transfer - shift the risk to another party.
Component 4: Accept - monitor but do not over-engineer.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A team may avoid an unproven dependency, reduce data-migration risk with a pilot, transfer SLA risk to the vendor contract, and accept a small cosmetic defect.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When risks are being listed but nobody is deciding.

### Agile / Hybrid Delivery Model
**Purpose:** Match delivery style to uncertainty and integration complexity.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Stable requirements -> Waterfall
Changing requirements -> Agile
Mixed environment -> Hybrid
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Waterfall - upfront planning and sequential delivery.
Component 2: Agile - short cycles and frequent feedback.
Component 3: Hybrid - waterfall for hard dependencies, agile for uncertain parts.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A hospital tech upgrade may use waterfall for infrastructure cutover and agile for user-facing workflow configuration.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is arguing about process style instead of delivery risk.

## 4. Formulas
The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source]; the source names the concepts but does not state the math explicitly.

Formula: CPI = Earned Value / Actual Cost
Variables:
Earned Value = value of completed work.
Actual Cost = money spent so far.
Why this formula exists: It tells you whether the project is getting enough value for the money burned.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 1 -> over budget -> tighten spend or reduce scope.
Value = 1 -> on budget -> continue monitoring.
Value > 1 -> cost-efficient -> preserve the delivery pattern.
Worked example with numbers: If earned value is $80k and actual cost is $100k, CPI = 0.8, which means the project is over budget.

Formula: SPI = Earned Value / Planned Value
Variables:
Earned Value = completed work value.
Planned Value = work value that should have been completed.
Why this formula exists: It tells you whether the schedule is ahead or behind.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 1 -> behind schedule -> review critical path and blockers.
Value = 1 -> on schedule -> keep cadence.
Value > 1 -> ahead of plan -> consider whether scope or quality is slipping.
Worked example with numbers: If earned value is $80k and planned value is $100k, SPI = 0.8, which means the project is behind schedule.

Formula: Schedule variance = Earned Value - Planned Value
Variables:
Earned Value = completed work value.
Planned Value = planned work value.
Why this formula exists: It shows the schedule gap in dollars or work units.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> behind -> re-plan the dependency chain.
Value = 0 -> on track -> hold the current plan.
Value > 0 -> ahead -> verify that the quality gate is still intact.
Worked example with numbers: If earned value is $80k and planned value is $100k, schedule variance is -$20k.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat the date as fixed when scope is still moving. | Re-negotiate scope, schedule, or cost explicitly through governance. |
| Let every stakeholder drive the same level of attention. | Use a power-interest grid and tailor engagement by quadrant. |
| Estimate a giant project at task-level on day one. | Decompose into work packages and use rolling-wave planning. |
| Report status in adjectives only. | Report CPI, SPI, variances, milestones, and risks. |
| Hide bad news until the next meeting. | Use escalation, decision logs, and early-warning signals immediately. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: ERP rollout slipping
Situation: An ERP rollout is 60% complete by planned value, but only 42% complete by earned value, and actual cost has already reached 70% of budget.
Applicable framework/metric: Earned value, critical path, and triple constraint.
Analysis: CPI is 0.6 and SPI is 0.7, which means the project is both over budget and behind schedule. The PM should not promise the original date without reducing scope or adding resources.
Decision rule: If CPI or SPI drops below 0.8, trigger a replan; if both stay between 0.8 and 1.0, monitor closely; if both exceed 1.0, maintain the current approach.
Action: Rework the critical path, freeze nonessential scope, and escalate the delta to the steering committee.

Scenario 2: Vendor-managed integration
Situation: A company outsources a data integration layer under a fixed-price SOW. The vendor's SLA misses are rising, and the internal team is seeing a growing blocker queue.
Applicable framework/metric: Vendor/SLA governance and risk response.
Analysis: If SLA attainment falls below 95% and blocker age rises above two weeks, the contract is not controlling the risk. The team needs stronger recovery terms or a different delivery model.
Decision rule: If SLA adherence is above 98%, keep the contract; if 95%-98%, tighten governance; if below 95%, renegotiate or transfer the work.
Action: Reset the weekly vendor review, add recovery milestones, and document every change request.

Scenario 3: Distributed agile release
Situation: A distributed team across three time zones is shipping a customer-facing feature. Velocity is stable, but defect escape has risen from 2% to 6% after the team switched to async coordination.
Applicable framework/metric: Agile delivery model and quality gate.
Analysis: Stable velocity does not mean healthy delivery if defects are leaking after release. The issue is probably collaboration, acceptance criteria, or review discipline rather than raw speed.
Decision rule: If defect escape exceeds 5%, slow the release cadence and tighten quality; if it stays under 2%, scale rollout; if it sits between 2% and 5%, keep the cadence but add checks.
Action: Add a clearer definition of done, restore synchronous design reviews where needed, and use hypercare until the defect curve stabilizes.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Write a one-page charter with scope, schedule, budget, sponsor, and success criteria.
2. Build a WBS and dependency map before assigning dates or vendors.
3. Run a risk workshop and convert the top risks into named response owners.
4. Publish a stakeholder map and communication plan for execs, users, vendors, and the PMO.
5. Create a dashboard with CPI, SPI, milestone variance, defect escape, and readiness.
6. Hold regular steerco reviews with explicit decisions, not just updates.
7. Capture lessons, versions, and sign-offs so the next rollout does not relearn the same mistakes.
