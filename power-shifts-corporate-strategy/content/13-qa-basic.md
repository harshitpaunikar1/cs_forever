# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational questions covering definitions, core concepts, and introductory reasoning across all 12 topics.

---

## Q1. What is power dynamics in an organizational context?

**Level:** Basic

Power dynamics refers to how influence, authority, and control flow between individuals, groups, and institutions within an organization. It encompasses both formal authority granted by titles and informal influence earned through relationships, expertise, or access to resources.

**Real-life applications:**
- Understanding who really makes decisions in a company beyond the org chart
- Identifying which team members can champion or block a new initiative
- Navigating office politics when proposing changes to established processes
- Recognizing when a junior employee holds outsized influence due to specialized knowledge

**Key concepts:** `formal authority`, `informal influence`, `power asymmetry`, `organizational hierarchy`, `decision-making`

---

## Q2. What is the difference between formal authority and informal influence?

**Level:** Basic

Formal authority comes from an official role or title — a VP can approve budgets because the org chart says so. Informal influence comes from relationships, expertise, or reputation — a senior engineer may shape technical direction without any management title. Both types of power coexist in every organization, and they frequently diverge.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project manager with formal authority but no technical credibility struggles to lead engineers
- A long-tenured employee informally mentors new hires and shapes team culture
- A board member uses personal relationships to influence decisions outside formal meetings
- A salesperson with deep customer relationships drives product roadmap priorities informally

**Key concepts:** `formal authority`, `informal influence`, `organizational hierarchy`, `power dynamics`, `leadership`

---

## Q3. What is power asymmetry?

**Level:** Basic

Power asymmetry exists when one party in a relationship or negotiation has significantly more influence, resources, or information than the other. This imbalance affects how decisions are made, how value is distributed, and whether weaker parties can protect their interests.

**Real-life applications:**
- A large retailer dictating terms to small suppliers who have no alternative buyers
- An employer offering a take-it-or-leave-it salary to a candidate with no competing offers
- A dominant tech platform changing its rules and forcing smaller app developers to comply
- A government regulator imposing requirements on companies that cannot negotiate back

**Key concepts:** `power asymmetry`, `bargaining power`, `negotiation`, `leverage`, `dependency`

---

## Q4. What is coalition building?

**Level:** Basic

Coalition building is the practice of assembling a group of allies who share a common goal or interest to advance an agenda. In organizations, coalitions amplify the influence of individual members by combining their resources, votes, or credibility to achieve outcomes none could accomplish alone.

**Real-life applications:**
- Department heads forming a coalition to advocate for a shared budget increase
- Employees across teams uniting to push for a remote work policy
- Industry competitors joining forces to lobby against unfavorable regulation
- Board members aligning to support or oppose a proposed acquisition

**Key concepts:** `coalition building`, `alliance`, `collective influence`, `stakeholder alignment`, `political strategy`

## Q5. What are geopolitical shifts?

**Level:** Basic

Geopolitical shifts are changes in the political relationships, trade policies, alliances, and conflicts between nations that reshape the global business environment. These include new tariffs, sanctions, trade agreements, territorial disputes, and changes in government leadership that affect how and where companies operate.

**Real-life applications:**
- Companies relocating factories after new tariffs make existing locations unprofitable
- Businesses exiting a country's market following the imposition of sanctions
- Firms adjusting supply chains in response to trade war escalations
- Corporations monitoring elections in key markets for policy change signals

**Key concepts:** `geopolitical risk`, `trade policy`, `sanctions`, `political risk`, `global strategy`

## Q6. What is political risk in business?

**Level:** Basic

Political risk is the probability that political decisions, events, or instability will negatively affect a company's operations, assets, or profitability. It includes risks from regulatory changes, nationalization, civil unrest, corruption, and shifts in government policy toward foreign businesses.

**Real-life applications:**
- An oil company losing assets when a government nationalizes energy infrastructure
- A tech firm facing new data localization laws that increase operating costs
- A manufacturer dealing with unexpected import duties after a change in government
- A bank assessing country risk before expanding into emerging markets

**Key concepts:** `political risk`, `country risk`, `regulatory change`, `geopolitical shifts`, `risk assessment`

## Q7. What is nearshoring?

**Level:** Basic

Nearshoring is the practice of relocating business operations or supply chain activities to countries that are geographically closer and often politically more stable than the current location. Companies nearshore to reduce transportation costs, improve supply chain reliability, and lower exposure to distant geopolitical risks.

**Real-life applications:**
- A US company moving manufacturing from Asia to Mexico to reduce shipping times
- A European firm shifting IT services from India to Eastern Europe for time zone alignment
- A retailer sourcing closer to home after pandemic-related shipping disruptions
- An automaker building a plant in a neighboring country to avoid tariffs on imports

**Key concepts:** `nearshoring`, `supply chain diversification`, `geopolitical risk`, `trade policy`, `operational resilience`

## Q8. What is disruptive innovation?

**Level:** Basic

Disruptive innovation is a process where a new technology or business model starts by serving underserved or overlooked market segments with a simpler, cheaper, or more accessible product, and then gradually displaces established competitors. The key insight is that incumbents often ignore the disruptor because the initial offering appears inferior by traditional standards.

**Real-life applications:**
- Budget airlines disrupting full-service carriers by offering no-frills travel to price-sensitive flyers
- Smartphones replacing dedicated GPS devices, cameras, and music players
- Online education platforms challenging traditional university degree programs
- Cloud computing displacing on-premises enterprise software

**Key concepts:** `disruptive innovation`, `incumbent's dilemma`, `technology adoption`, `market disruption`, `competitive displacement`

---

## Q9. What is the incumbent's dilemma?

**Level:** Basic

The incumbent's dilemma is the tension established companies face between protecting their existing profitable business and investing in new innovations that may cannibalize current revenue. Incumbents often rationally choose to defend their cash cows, but this rational short-term decision leaves them vulnerable to disruptors who have nothing to protect.

**Real-life applications:**
- A newspaper hesitating to invest in digital because it would undercut print advertising revenue
- A taxi company resisting app-based dispatch while ride-sharing startups grow
- A camera manufacturer delaying investment in smartphone optics to protect camera sales
- A bank slow to adopt mobile payments for fear of disrupting branch-based services

**Key concepts:** `incumbent's dilemma`, `disruptive innovation`, `cannibalization`, `strategic inertia`, `competitive response`

## Q10. What is digital transformation?

**Level:** Basic

Digital transformation is the integration of digital technology into all areas of a business, fundamentally changing how it operates and delivers value to customers. It goes beyond simply digitizing existing processes — it involves rethinking business models, customer interactions, and organizational structures around digital capabilities.

**Real-life applications:**
- A bank launching a fully digital onboarding process that eliminates branch visits
- A manufacturer using IoT sensors to predict equipment failures before they happen
- A retailer integrating online and in-store inventory for unified customer experience
- A healthcare provider implementing telemedicine to reach rural patients

**Key concepts:** `digital transformation`, `technology adoption`, `business model innovation`, `operational efficiency`, `customer experience`

## Q11. What is stakeholder mapping?

**Level:** Basic

Stakeholder mapping is a visual framework used to categorize stakeholders based on their level of power and interest in a particular decision or initiative. By plotting stakeholders on a grid, leaders can prioritize engagement efforts, focusing attention on those with the most ability and motivation to influence outcomes.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project manager identifying which executives need regular updates versus occasional briefings
- A company preparing for a product launch by mapping customer segments, regulators, and media
- A nonprofit assessing which donors and board members will support a new strategic direction
- An M&A team identifying which stakeholders could block or accelerate a deal

**Key concepts:** `stakeholder mapping`, `power-interest grid`, `stakeholder analysis`, `engagement strategy`, `prioritization`

## Q12. What is stakeholder engagement?

**Level:** Basic

Stakeholder engagement is the deliberate process of communicating with, involving, and building relationships with individuals or groups who have an interest in or influence over an organization's decisions. Effective engagement builds trust, surfaces concerns early, and increases the likelihood that strategic initiatives will succeed.

**Real-life applications:**
- Holding town halls with employees before announcing a major restructuring
- Conducting customer advisory panels to gather feedback on product direction
- Meeting with community leaders before opening a new facility
- Briefing analysts and investors ahead of a major strategic pivot

**Key concepts:** `stakeholder engagement`, `communication strategy`, `trust building`, `stakeholder analysis`, `change management`

## Q13. What is the salience model in stakeholder analysis?

**Level:** Basic

The salience model classifies stakeholders based on three attributes: power (ability to influence the organization), legitimacy (whether their claim is appropriate or recognized), and urgency (how time-sensitive their demands are). Stakeholders who possess all three attributes are considered the most salient and require the highest priority attention.

**Real-life applications:**
- A regulator with power, legitimacy, and an enforcement deadline is the highest-priority stakeholder
- Employees have legitimacy and urgency during layoffs but may lack power individually
- An activist investor gains salience when they acquire enough shares to force a board vote
- A community group becomes more salient when media coverage raises the urgency of their concerns

**Key concepts:** `salience model`, `stakeholder prioritization`, `power`, `legitimacy`, `urgency`

**Real-life applications:**

## Q16. What is regulatory capture?

**Level:** Basic

Regulatory capture occurs when a regulatory agency, created to act in the public interest, instead advances the commercial or political interests of the industry it is supposed to oversee. This happens when regulators become overly dependent on industry expertise, revolving-door employment patterns blur loyalties, or industry lobbying overwhelms public interest advocacy.

**Real-life applications:**
- A former industry executive appointed to lead the agency regulating their former sector
- A financial regulator that relaxes rules after extensive lobbying by the banking industry
- An environmental agency that adopts industry-proposed standards weaker than public health experts recommend
- A telecommunications regulator that favors incumbents in spectrum allocation decisions

**Key concepts:** `regulatory capture`, `government regulation`, `public interest`, `lobbying`, `institutional power`

## Q17. What is a network effect?

**Level:** Basic

A network effect occurs when a product or service becomes more valuable as more people use it. Each additional user adds value for all existing users, creating a positive feedback loop that can drive rapid growth and make it difficult for competitors to gain traction against an established network.

**Real-life applications:**
- A messaging app becomes more useful as more of your contacts join it
- A marketplace attracts more sellers as buyer traffic increases, and vice versa
- A professional network becomes more valuable for job seekers as more recruiters join
- A payment system gains merchant acceptance as more consumers adopt it

**Key concepts:** `network effect`, `positive feedback loop`, `platform economics`, `user growth`, `competitive moat`

---

## Q18. What is the difference between direct and indirect network effects?

**Level:** Basic

Direct network effects occur when the value of a service increases for all users as more users of the same type join — like a phone network becoming more useful as more people get phones. Indirect network effects occur when more users on one side of a platform attract more users on the other side — like more smartphone users attracting more app developers, which makes the smartphone more valuable.

**Real-life applications:**
- Social media platforms exhibit direct network effects as more friends join
- Ride-hailing apps exhibit indirect effects: more riders attract more drivers and vice versa
- Gaming consoles exhibit indirect effects: more console owners attract more game developers
- Credit card networks exhibit indirect effects: more cardholders attract more merchants

**Key concepts:** `direct network effects`, `indirect network effects`, `platform economics`, `two-sided market`, `value creation`

A platform ecosystem is the collection of users, developers, partners, and complementary services that surround and depend on a core platform. - A cloud provider's ecosystem of software partners building on its infrastructure
- A gaming platform's community of players, streamers, and independent game studios

## Q20. What are switching costs?

**Level:** Basic

Switching costs are the expenses, effort, and inconvenience a user or customer faces when moving from one product, service, or platform to another. High switching costs lock users in, giving the existing provider significant power. Switching costs can be financial (contract penalties), procedural (learning a new system), or relational (losing network connections).

**Real-life applications:**
- A company migrating enterprise software faces data conversion costs and employee retraining
- A consumer switching phone ecosystems loses purchased apps and learned workflows
- A business changing banks deals with payment re-routing and credit relationship rebuilding
- A social media user loses their content history and follower network by switching platforms

**Key concepts:** `switching costs`, `lock-in`, `platform power`, `customer retention`, `competitive moat`

A joint venture is a business arrangement where two or more companies create a new, separate entity to pursue a specific project or business objective. Each partner contributes resources — such as capital, technology, or market access — and shares in the governance, risks, and profits of the venture.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two automakers creating a joint venture to develop electric vehicle batteries

## Q22. What is co-opetition?

**Level:** Basic

Co-opetition is a strategy where companies that are competitors in some areas choose to cooperate in others. This happens when the benefits of collaboration — such as shared R&D costs, standard-setting, or market expansion — outweigh the competitive risks in specific situations.

**Real-life applications:**
- Rival smartphone makers jointly developing a universal charging standard
- Competing airlines sharing airport lounges through alliance partnerships
- Pharmaceutical competitors co-funding clinical trials for rare disease treatments
- Tech companies collaborating on open-source software while competing on products built with it

**Key concepts:** `co-opetition`, `strategic alliance`, `competitive collaboration`, `industry standards`, `shared value`

## Q23. What is knowledge spillover in alliances?

**Level:** Basic

Knowledge spillover is the unintended transfer of proprietary information, trade secrets, or capabilities from one alliance partner to another. While alliances are designed to share specific knowledge, the close working relationships they require often lead to broader knowledge transfer that can benefit — or threaten — either partner.

**Real-life applications:**
- An automaker's manufacturing techniques being learned by a joint venture partner who later becomes a competitor
- A startup sharing its algorithm with a larger partner that then builds a competing feature
- Engineers rotating between alliance partners carrying process knowledge with them
- A supplier learning a customer's demand forecasting methods through a collaborative planning arrangement

**Key concepts:** `knowledge spillover`, `intellectual property`, `alliance risk`, `strategic alliance`, `competitive intelligence`

## Q24. What is succession planning?

Succession planning is the process of identifying and developing potential future leaders within an organization to ensure continuity when executives or key personnel leave, retire, or are promoted. Effective succession planning starts years before a transition and includes structured assessment, mentoring, and real-world leadership testing.

**Real-life applications:**
- A CEO identifying three potential successors and rotating them through different divisions
- A family business preparing the next generation for ownership and management responsibilities
- A board maintaining a confidential list of emergency CEO candidates
- A hospital system developing physician leaders to eventually run clinical departments

## Q25. What is a power vacuum?

**Level:** Basic

A power vacuum is a period of uncertainty that occurs when authority is unclear, contested, or absent — typically during leadership transitions, organizational restructurings, or after sudden departures. Power vacuums invite political maneuvering as individuals and factions compete to fill the void, often disrupting operations and strategic priorities.

**Real-life applications:**
- A CEO resigns suddenly and the board takes weeks to name an interim leader
- A department head leaves and two senior managers compete for the role, splitting the team
- A merger creates overlapping leadership roles with unclear decision rights
- A founder steps back without clearly transferring authority, leaving the executive team paralyzed

**Key concepts:** `power vacuum`, `leadership transition`, `organizational politics`, `authority`, `governance`

## Q26. What is organizational inertia?

**Level:** Basic

Organizational inertia is the tendency of established routines, cultures, and power structures to resist change. Even when leaders recognize the need for a new direction, existing processes, habits, and vested interests slow or block transformation. Inertia is strongest in successful companies where current approaches have produced good results.

**Real-life applications:**
- A profitable company resisting digital investment because current revenue is still growing
- Middle managers blocking process changes that would reduce their authority
- Employees defaulting to old workflows months after a new system is implemented
- A culture of risk aversion preventing investment in new business lines

**Key concepts:** `organizational inertia`, `change resistance`, `status quo bias`, `organizational culture`, `transformation`

## Q27. What is board independence?

**Level:** Basic

Board independence refers to the degree to which board members can exercise judgment free from conflicts of interest or undue influence from the company's management. Independent directors — those with no material financial, employment, or personal ties to the company beyond their board role — are considered essential for objective oversight.

**Real-life applications:**
- A board requiring that a majority of directors be independent of management
- An independent audit committee reviewing financial statements without CEO interference
- Regulations mandating independent directors on compensation committees
- Investors evaluating a company's governance quality based on board independence ratios

**Key concepts:** `board independence`, `corporate governance`, `fiduciary duty`, `oversight`, `conflict of interest`

## Q28. What is fiduciary duty?

**Level:** Basic

Fiduciary duty is the legal obligation of board members and executives to act in the best interests of the company and its shareholders. This includes the duty of care (making informed decisions) and the duty of loyalty (putting the company's interests ahead of personal gain). Breaching fiduciary duty can result in personal legal liability.

**Real-life applications:**
- A board member recusing themselves from a vote on a deal involving a company they own
- Directors conducting thorough due diligence before approving a major acquisition
- An executive disclosing a personal conflict of interest before participating in a decision
- Shareholders suing a board for approving an executive compensation package that was clearly excessive

**Key concepts:** `fiduciary duty`, `duty of care`, `duty of loyalty`, `corporate governance`, `board responsibility`

## Q30. What is information asymmetry?

**Level:** Basic

Information asymmetry exists when one party in a transaction or relationship has significantly more or better information than the other. This imbalance creates power advantages for the better-informed party and can lead to unfair outcomes, market inefficiencies, and trust breakdowns.

**Real-life applications:**
- A used car seller knowing about mechanical problems the buyer cannot detect
- A company in acquisition talks having better knowledge of its own financial health than the buyer
- An insurer knowing less about a customer's health risks than the customer does
- A platform collecting user data that the users themselves cannot fully access or understand

**Key concepts:** `information asymmetry`, `adverse selection`, `moral hazard`, `transparency`, `market efficiency`

## Q33. What is BATNA?

**Level:** Basic

BATNA stands for Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement. It represents the most favorable course of action available to a party if the current negotiation fails. Knowing your BATNA — and estimating the other side's — is the foundation of negotiation strategy because it determines your walk-away point and bargaining power.

**Real-life applications:**
- A job candidate with another offer has a stronger BATNA in salary negotiations
- A supplier with multiple interested buyers can negotiate better terms with each
- A company considering a merger knows it can walk away and pursue organic growth instead
- A landlord with a waiting list of tenants has strong leverage in lease negotiations

**Key concepts:** `BATNA`, `negotiation`, `leverage`, `walk-away point`, `alternative options`

---

## Q34. What is anchoring in negotiation?

**Level:** Basic

Anchoring is a cognitive bias where the first number or offer put on the table heavily influences the range of outcomes both parties consider reasonable. In negotiation, the party that sets the anchor often gains an advantage because subsequent offers and counteroffers tend to cluster around that initial reference point.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seller listing a house above market value to anchor buyer expectations higher
- A vendor quoting a high initial price knowing the client will negotiate down from that point
- A salary negotiation where the first number mentioned frames the entire discussion
- A startup founder setting a high valuation in early funding talks to anchor investor expectations

**Key concepts:** `anchoring`, `cognitive bias`, `negotiation tactics`, `framing`, `reference point`

---

## Q35. What is ZOPA?

**Level:** Basic

ZOPA stands for Zone of Possible Agreement. It is the range between each party's walk-away point (reservation price) where a deal is possible. If the buyer's maximum price exceeds the seller's minimum price, a ZOPA exists. If there is no overlap, no deal can be made without one party changing their constraints.

**Real-life applications:**
- In a home sale, ZOPA is the range between the seller's minimum acceptable price and the buyer's maximum budget
- In a salary negotiation, ZOPA is the overlap between what the candidate will accept and what the employer will pay
- In a supplier contract, ZOPA is the price range where both sides can operate profitably
- In M&A, ZOPA is the valuation range where both buyer and target see the deal as worthwhile

**Key concepts:** `ZOPA`, `reservation price`, `negotiation range`, `deal-making`, `bargaining`

## Q38. What is strategic optionality?

Strategic optionality refers to investments or positions that create the right, but not the obligation, to take future action as conditions evolve. - A retailer leasing flexible warehouse space that can be expanded or released at short notice

## Q40. What is the technology adoption curve?

**Level:** Basic

The technology adoption curve describes the pattern by which new technologies spread through a market over time. It typically follows a bell curve: innovators adopt first, followed by early adopters, early majority, late majority, and finally laggards. Understanding where a technology sits on this curve helps companies time their investments and marketing.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company targeting early adopters with a premium-priced version of a new product
- An enterprise software firm waiting for mainstream adoption before investing in a new platform
- A retailer timing its investment in self-checkout based on customer readiness
- A healthcare provider choosing when to adopt a new diagnostic technology based on clinical validation

**Key concepts:** `technology adoption curve`, `early adopters`, `mainstream adoption`, `diffusion of innovation`, `market timing`

## Q42. What is a winner-take-most market?

**Level:** Basic

A winner-take-most market is one where the leading company captures a disproportionately large share of the total value, leaving little for competitors. This dynamic is driven by network effects, economies of scale, and high switching costs. Once a leader emerges, it becomes extremely difficult for rivals to catch up.

**Real-life applications:**
- One search engine dominating 90% of search traffic globally
- A single marketplace capturing the majority of online third-party sales
- One ride-hailing app controlling most of the driver supply in a city
- A dominant social media platform absorbing most digital advertising spending

**Key concepts:** `winner-take-most`, `network effects`, `market concentration`, `economies of scale`, `competitive dynamics`

## Q44. What is a transition mandate?

**Level:** Basic

A transition mandate is the explicit or implicit set of expectations that define what a new leader is brought in to accomplish. Whether a CEO is hired to drive growth, cut costs, integrate an acquisition, or transform the culture, the mandate shapes their priorities, authority, and how their success will be measured.

**Real-life applications:**

- A new school principal mandated to improve test scores and reduce teacher turnover

## Q45. What is a corporate governance code?

**Level:** Basic

A corporate governance code is a set of principles and best-practice guidelines that define how a company should be directed and controlled. These codes cover board composition, executive compensation, shareholder rights, transparency, and accountability. Some codes are legally binding; others operate on a "comply or explain" basis.

**Real-life applications:**
- A publicly listed company following its stock exchange's governance code to maintain listing status

## Q46. What is predictive analytics?

**Level:** Basic

Predictive analytics uses statistical models, machine learning algorithms, and historical data to forecast future outcomes or behaviors. Companies use predictive analytics to anticipate customer needs, optimize operations, detect fraud, and make better strategic decisions by basing them on data-driven probabilities rather than intuition alone.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q47. What is integrative bargaining?

**Level:** Basic

Integrative bargaining is a negotiation approach that seeks to expand the total value available to both parties rather than simply dividing a fixed pie. Instead of competing over who gets more, parties identify shared interests and creative trade-offs that make both sides better off. This approach often leads to stronger, more sustainable agreements.

**Real-life applications:**
- A supplier offering extended payment terms in exchange for a longer contract commitment
- A salary negotiation where the employer offers flexible work arrangements instead of a higher base salary
- Two companies bundling products to create a joint offering neither could sell alone
- A landlord and tenant agreeing to a lower rent in exchange for the tenant handling minor maintenance

**Key concepts:** `integrative bargaining`, `value creation`, `win-win negotiation`, `trade-offs`, `collaborative negotiation`

## Q48. What is antifragility?

**Level:** Basic

Antifragility is a concept describing systems that do not merely resist stress and disorder but actually improve and grow stronger when exposed to them. Unlike resilient systems that return to their original state after a shock, antifragile systems use volatility and disruption as fuel for adaptation and improvement.

**Real-life applications:**
- A startup that uses each customer complaint to improve its product, becoming stronger with each failure
- An immune system that becomes more effective after exposure to pathogens
- A trading firm whose risk models improve after each market shock
- An organization that conducts post-mortems after every project failure and systematically applies lessons learned

**Key concepts:** `antifragility`, `resilience`, `volatility`, `adaptation`, `continuous improvement`

## Q49. What is interest alignment in stakeholder analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Interest alignment refers to the degree to which a stakeholder's goals overlap with the organization's strategic objectives. High alignment means the stakeholder is a natural ally; low alignment signals potential conflict. Understanding alignment helps leaders decide where to invest engagement efforts and where to expect resistance.

**Real-life applications:**
- An investor whose financial return goals align with the company's growth strategy
- A regulator whose safety mandate conflicts with a company's desire for faster product launches
- Employees whose job security concerns align with management's retention goals
- A community group whose environmental priorities conflict with a manufacturer's expansion plans

**Key concepts:** `interest alignment`, `stakeholder analysis`, `strategic fit`, `conflict identification`, `engagement strategy`

## Q50. What role does transparency play in power dynamics?

**Level:** Basic

Transparency about decision-making processes, information access, and resource allocation reduces destructive political behavior in organizations. When people understand how and why decisions are made, they are less likely to resort to backroom deals and manipulation. Transparency shifts power from those who hoard information to those who create value.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company publishing its promotion criteria so employees understand what is needed to advance
- Open salary bands reducing pay inequality and the power of secretive negotiation
- A board publishing its meeting minutes to increase accountability to shareholders
- A project team sharing its decision log so stakeholders understand trade-offs

**Key concepts:** `transparency`, `power dynamics`, `accountability`, `decision-making`, `organizational trust`

## Q51. What are sanctions in international business?

**Level:** Basic

Sanctions are government-imposed restrictions on trade, financial transactions, or other economic activities with specific countries, entities, or individuals. They are tools of foreign policy used to punish, deter, or change the behavior of target countries. Companies must comply with sanctions or face severe legal penalties.

**Real-life applications:**
- A bank screening every transaction against a government sanctions list before processing it
- An exporter unable to sell products to a sanctioned country despite having existing contracts
- A multinational restructuring its subsidiary ownership to comply with new sanctions
- A shipping company re-routing vessels to avoid sanctioned ports

## Q52. What is multi-homing in platform markets?

**Level:** Basic

Multi-homing occurs when users or businesses participate on multiple competing platforms simultaneously rather than committing exclusively to one. When multi-homing is easy, it weakens any single platform's power because users can shift activity to a competitor quickly. Platforms try to discourage multi-homing through exclusive features, loyalty programs, and integration depth.

**Real-life applications:**
- A restaurant listing on multiple food delivery apps to maximize order volume
- A driver working for two or three ride-hailing services simultaneously
- A seller maintaining storefronts on several e-commerce marketplaces
- A content creator posting on multiple social media platforms to diversify audience reach

**Key concepts:** `multi-homing`, `platform competition`, `switching costs`, `user loyalty`, `platform strategy`

## Q53. How does speed of response matter in geopolitical risk?

**Level:** Basic

When geopolitical conditions change — new tariffs, sanctions, or political instability — the speed at which a company responds often matters more than the specific strategy it chooses. Companies that detect signals early, have pre-planned responses, and can mobilize quickly gain first-mover advantages over slower competitors, securing alternative suppliers, market positions, or regulatory relationships before they are taken.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company activating a pre-negotiated backup supplier within days of a tariff announcement
- A firm relocating key staff from a politically unstable country before travel restrictions begin
- An exporter filing for tariff exemptions before the application window closes
- A bank restructuring a sanctioned entity's accounts ahead of a compliance deadline

## Q54. What is the role of government relations in corporate strategy?

Government relations is the function within a company responsible for managing relationships with elected officials, regulators, and government agencies. It serves as both a sensor — detecting policy changes before they happen — and an influencer — advocating for favorable outcomes. When integrated with corporate strategy, government relations helps companies shape, rather than simply react to, the regulatory environment.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q55. What is the first 90 days concept in leadership transitions?

**Level:** Basic

The first 90 days refers to the critical initial period of a new leader's tenure during which they establish credibility, set expectations, build key relationships, and define priorities. Research and practice consistently show that the actions taken in this window disproportionately shape a leader's long-term effectiveness and the organization's trajectory under their leadership.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q56. What is supply chain diversification?

**Level:** Basic

Supply chain diversification is the strategy of sourcing materials, components, or services from multiple suppliers across different locations rather than depending on a single source. Diversification reduces the risk that a disruption at one supplier or in one region will halt the entire production process.

**Real-life applications:**
- An electronics company sourcing chips from suppliers in three different countries

## Q57. What is framing in negotiation?

**Level:** Basic

Framing is how an issue, offer, or choice is presented in a negotiation, and it significantly affects how the other party perceives and responds to it. The same deal can look attractive or unattractive depending on whether it is framed as a gain or a loss, a limited opportunity or a standard offer.

**Real-life applications:**
- Presenting a price increase as an investment in improved service quality rather than a cost hike
- Framing a job offer as a career opportunity with growth potential rather than a lateral move
- Positioning contract terms as mutual protections rather than restrictions
- Describing a merger as a partnership of equals rather than an acquisition

**Key concepts:** `framing`, `negotiation`, `perception`, `communication strategy`, `cognitive bias`

## Q58. What is organizational slack?

**Level:** Basic

Organizational slack refers to the reserves of resources — cash, talent, time, capacity — that exceed what is needed for current operations. While efficiency-focused management tries to eliminate slack, a degree of slack provides the buffer needed to respond to unexpected opportunities or threats without disrupting core operations.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company with cash reserves able to acquire a distressed competitor during a downturn
- A team with spare capacity that can take on an urgent project without delaying other work
- A manufacturer with excess warehouse space that can absorb a sudden surge in inventory
- A firm with cross-trained employees who can cover critical roles during unexpected absences

**Key concepts:** `organizational slack`, `resource buffer`, `operational flexibility`, `resilience`, `strategic reserves`

## Q59. What is the duty of care for board members?

**Level:** Basic

The duty of care requires board members to make informed, deliberate decisions by gathering relevant information, asking critical questions, and exercising the level of judgment that a reasonably prudent person would apply. Board members who approve major decisions without adequate review can be held personally liable for resulting losses.

**Real-life applications:**
- Directors reviewing detailed financial projections before approving a major acquisition
- A board requesting an independent valuation opinion before accepting a buyout offer
- Board members attending meetings prepared, having read all distributed materials
- A director asking management to explain the risks of a proposed strategy before voting

**Key concepts:** `duty of care`, `fiduciary duty`, `informed decision-making`, `board responsibility`, `governance`

## Q60. What is the difference between resilience and antifragility?

**Level:** Basic

Resilience is the ability to absorb a shock and return to the original state — a resilient company bounces back to normal after a crisis. Antifragility goes further: an antifragile system actually improves because of the shock, emerging stronger or more capable than before. Resilience resists damage; antifragility benefits from it.

**Real-life applications:**
- A resilient supply chain restores operations after a disruption; an antifragile one redesigns itself to handle the next disruption better
- A resilient team recovers from a project failure; an antifragile team builds new processes that prevent similar failures
- A resilient investment portfolio preserves value during a downturn; an antifragile one repositions to profit from volatility
- A resilient company survives a competitive threat; an antifragile one absorbs lessons and enters new markets

**Key concepts:** `resilience`, `antifragility`, `adaptation`, `continuous improvement`, `strategic learning`

## Q61. What is the power-interest grid?

**Level:** Basic

The power-interest grid is a stakeholder mapping tool that places stakeholders on a two-by-two matrix based on their level of power (ability to influence outcomes) and their level of interest (how much they care about the issue). The grid produces four categories: manage closely (high power, high interest), keep satisfied (high power, low interest), keep informed (low power, high interest), and monitor (low power, low interest).

**Real-life applications:**
- A project manager deciding which executives need weekly updates versus quarterly summaries
- A public affairs team prioritizing which community groups to engage directly
- A company determining which investors require personal briefings ahead of earnings
- A change management team identifying who needs to be consulted versus informed about a restructuring

**Key concepts:** `power-interest grid`, `stakeholder mapping`, `engagement strategy`, `prioritization`, `stakeholder analysis`

## Q64. How do economies of scale relate to platform power?

**Level:** Basic

Economies of scale reduce the per-unit cost of serving each additional user on a platform. Combined with network effects, this creates a reinforcing cycle: more users mean lower costs per user and greater value per user, making it progressively harder for smaller competitors to match the platform's economics. This dynamic drives market concentration in platform industries.

**Real-life applications:**
- A cloud computing provider spreading infrastructure costs across millions of users
- A marketplace amortizing its technology investment across a growing number of sellers
- A streaming service spreading content licensing costs across a massive subscriber base
- A payment network reducing per-transaction costs as volume grows

**Key concepts:** `economies of scale`, `platform power`, `network effects`, `cost advantage`, `market concentration`

## Q65. What is adverse selection?

**Level:** Basic

Adverse selection is a market problem that occurs when information asymmetry causes the party with less information to attract a disproportionate share of unfavorable counterparts. The classic example is insurance: if insurers cannot distinguish high-risk from low-risk customers, high-risk customers are more likely to buy coverage, driving up costs for everyone.

**Real-life applications:**
- Health insurers raising premiums because sicker individuals are more likely to purchase coverage
- Used car markets where buyers assume lower quality because sellers have more information
- Loan markets where riskier borrowers are more willing to accept high interest rates
- Job markets where the best candidates may not apply if compensation seems below market

**Key concepts:** `adverse selection`, `information asymmetry`, `market failure`, `risk pooling`, `signaling`

## Q66. What is a comply-or-explain governance regime?

**Level:** Basic

A comply-or-explain regime is a governance framework where companies are expected to follow a set of best-practice guidelines but may deviate from specific provisions if they publicly explain why the deviation is appropriate for their circumstances. This approach provides flexibility while maintaining transparency and accountability.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company explaining in its annual report why its CEO also serves as board chair
- A firm disclosing why it has fewer independent directors than the code recommends
- A company justifying why its audit committee includes a non-independent member with relevant expertise
- Investors reviewing a company's explanations to assess governance quality

**Key concepts:** `comply or explain`, `corporate governance code`, `transparency`, `flexibility`, `accountability`

## Q67. What is the role of culture in organizational resilience?

**Level:** Basic

Culture determines how an organization responds to unexpected events at every level. A culture that encourages experimentation, learning from failure, open communication, and decentralized decision-making creates the conditions for rapid adaptation. Conversely, a culture of blame, rigid hierarchy, and information hoarding slows response and amplifies the damage from disruptions.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company where frontline employees are empowered to make decisions during a supply chain disruption
- A team that conducts blameless post-mortems after every incident, building institutional knowledge
- An organization where leaders openly discuss failures, encouraging others to surface problems early
- A startup culture that celebrates rapid iteration, allowing faster pivots when market conditions change

**Key concepts:** `organizational culture`, `resilience`, `learning culture`, `adaptability`, `psychological safety`

## Q68. What is a reservation price in negotiation?

**Level:** Basic

A reservation price is the maximum (for a buyer) or minimum (for a seller) acceptable price at which a party is willing to make a deal. Beyond this point, the party would prefer to walk away and pursue their BATNA. Knowing your own reservation price and estimating the other side's is essential for determining whether a ZOPA exists.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer setting a maximum purchase price before entering a home-buying negotiation
- A supplier calculating the lowest price at which a contract remains profitable
- An employer determining the maximum salary budget for a position before interviewing
- A company establishing the highest premium it will pay for an acquisition target

**Key concepts:** `reservation price`, `BATNA`, `ZOPA`, `walk-away point`, `negotiation strategy`

## Q69. What is the role of diversification in managing uncertainty?

**Level:** Basic

Diversification spreads risk across multiple products, markets, revenue streams, or supply sources so that a negative event in one area does not cripple the entire organization. It is a foundational principle of resilient strategy: companies with diversified operations can absorb localized shocks while maintaining overall performance.

**Real-life applications:**
- A conglomerate with business units in healthcare, technology, and consumer goods offsetting sector downturns
- A retailer selling through both online and physical channels to reduce channel-specific risk
- An investor holding assets across multiple geographies and asset classes
- A manufacturer sourcing raw materials from suppliers in different countries

**Key concepts:** `diversification`, `risk management`, `portfolio strategy`, `resilience`, `uncertainty`

## Q70. What is moral hazard?

**Level:** Basic

Moral hazard occurs when one party takes on more risk because they know that someone else will bear the consequences. It arises when there is a disconnect between who makes a decision and who suffers if it goes wrong. Insurance, bailouts, and principal-agent relationships are common settings for moral hazard.

**Real-life applications:**
- A bank taking excessive risks because it expects a government bailout if it fails
- An insured driver being less careful because their insurance will cover accident costs
- An executive taking aggressive risks because their compensation is structured as upside-only
- A contractor cutting quality corners because the client cannot easily observe the work

**Key concepts:** `moral hazard`, `information asymmetry`, `risk-taking`, `incentive misalignment`, `accountability`

### Power and Influence Map
**Purpose:** Separate who has the title from who has the real leverage.


The decision is to map the influence network before pushing a platform change.

## 4. Formulas

The source is a question set, so the formulas below are managerial tools that operationalize the concepts. They are expanded from model knowledge, not the source. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: ZOPA Width
Formula: `ZOPA width = buyer reservation price - seller reservation price`
Variables:
`buyer reservation price` = maximum the buyer will pay
`seller reservation price` = minimum the seller will accept
Why this formula exists: It answers whether a deal zone exists.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> no deal zone -> change terms or walk away
Value = 0 -> thin zone -> use integrative bargaining carefully
Value > 0 -> viable zone -> trade on issues, not just price
Worked example with numbers: If the buyer will pay up to $1.2M and the seller needs at least $1.0M, the ZOPA is $200k. Decision: use the gap to negotiate service terms or implementation scope. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 2: Switching Cost
Formula: `switching cost = migration cost + learning cost + coordination cost`
Variables:
`migration cost` = data, process, or asset move cost
`learning cost` = time to become productive in the new system
`coordination cost` = management time spent switching
Why this formula exists: It answers how sticky a platform or vendor relationship is.
How to interpret the output:
Low -> easy exit -> more competition
Medium -> moderate lock-in -> need value to retain
High -> strong lock-in -> watch abuse and complacency
Worked example with numbers: If moving CRM systems costs $40k, training costs $20k, and coordination costs $15k, switching cost is $75k. Decision: if retention is weak, the vendor is exposed to churn. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 3: Resilience Capacity
Formula: `resilience capacity = diversification x slack x culture`
Variables:
`diversification` = spread of exposure across assets or markets
`slack` = spare capacity or buffer
`culture` = willingness to learn and adapt
Why this formula exists: It answers how much shock the system can absorb without breaking.
How to interpret the output:
Low -> brittle -> add buffers and redesign
Medium -> workable -> monitor the weakest link
High -> strong -> invest in learning and optionality
Worked example with numbers: A business with decent diversification, moderate slack, and a learning culture will absorb shocks far better than a lean, single-market organization. Decision: protect the weakest factor first. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 4: Moral Hazard Exposure
Formula: `moral hazard exposure = decision control x downside shifted to others`
Variables:
`decision control` = how much freedom the actor has
`downside shifted to others` = how much of the loss someone else bears
Why this formula exists: It answers when incentives are misaligned.
How to interpret the output:
Low -> aligned -> normal oversight
Medium -> watch carefully -> improve monitoring
High -> serious risk -> redesign incentives
Worked example with numbers: If an executive controls a risky project but shareholders and employees bear most of the downside, moral hazard exposure is high. Decision: link compensation to durable outcomes and risk discipline. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 5: Transition Risk
Formula: `transition risk = power vacuum risk + attrition risk + execution risk`
Variables:
`power vacuum risk` = ambiguity over who decides
`attrition risk` = probability key people leave
`execution risk` = chance the work slows or fragments
Why this formula exists: It answers how dangerous a leadership change or restructuring is.
How to interpret the output:
Low -> simple handoff -> standard governance
Medium -> structured transition -> extra communication
High -> formal transition program -> daily attention
Worked example with numbers: A merger with a confusing mandate and two key departures has a high transition risk score. Decision: stop treating it as a simple reorg and run it like a program. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Platform ecosystem with switching costs
Situation: An AI platform has strong network effects, but customers can multi-home and switch if another tool integrates faster. Leadership wants to know whether to invest in complements or lower lock-in friction. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Platform Power Loop + switching cost.
Analysis: If switching cost is only $75k for a customer account, a competitor can still poach users with a better bundle. Building a richer ecosystem or a clearer workflow moat may be more effective than relying on stickiness alone.
Decision rule: If network effects are strong but switching costs are low, invest in complements and ecosystem depth. If switching costs are high, focus on retention quality and governance.
Action: Add integrations, improve onboarding, and measure multi-homing rates by segment.

### Scenario 2: CEO transition and board independence
Situation: A board is planning succession while an activist investor is pushing for governance changes. The incoming leader needs a clear transition mandate and the board needs to preserve independence. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Governance and Power Boundary Matrix + transition risk.
Analysis: If board independence is weak and transition risk is high, the company may look captured or unstable. Clarifying fiduciary duty, committee structure, and first-90-day priorities can lower the risk.

### Scenario 3: Geopolitical shift and supply chain redesign

Applicable framework/metric: Resilience and Optionality Portfolio + geopolitical risk.
Analysis: If route concentration and country exposure are high, the cost of inaction can dwarf the cost of diversification. Nearshoring or supply chain diversification buys strategic optionality and speeds response.
Decision rule: If geopolitical risk is rising and the product is strategic, diversify. If the exposure is minor, monitor. If the component is critical, re-source now.
Action: Build a supplier concentration dashboard, model tariffs and lead time, and secure a backup source.
