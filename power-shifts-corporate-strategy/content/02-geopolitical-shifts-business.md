# Geopolitical Shifts and Business

## Overview
Geopolitical shifts — changes in trade policy, sanctions, regional conflicts, and international alliances — directly reshape the operating environment for global businesses. Companies that once relied on stable supply chains and open markets now face tariffs, export controls, and political risk as everyday strategic concerns. Understanding geopolitics is no longer optional for corporate leaders; it is a core competency.

---

## Why It Matters
A single policy change, such as a new tariff or sanctions regime, can disrupt supply chains overnight, strand billions in foreign assets, or open entirely new markets. Companies that monitor geopolitical trends and build flexible strategies can turn disruption into competitive advantage, while those caught off guard face margin compression, regulatory penalties, and reputational damage.

## Key Principles
- Geopolitical risk is a business risk that belongs on the corporate strategy agenda
- Diversification of suppliers, markets, and partnerships reduces exposure to single-country risk
- Government relations and regulatory intelligence are strategic capabilities, not just compliance functions
- Speed of response to geopolitical change often matters more than the initial position taken

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Sanctions** | Government-imposed restrictions on trade or financial transactions with specific countries or entities |
| **Trade war** | Escalating tariffs and trade barriers between countries that disrupt international commerce |
| **Political risk** | The probability that political decisions or events will negatively affect business operations or profitability |
| **Nearshoring** | Relocating supply chain operations to geographically closer, politically stable countries |

## Use Case
An automotive manufacturer shifts component sourcing from a single Asian supplier to a mix of facilities in Mexico and Eastern Europe after new export controls threaten delivery reliability.

## Scenario
> A European pharmaceutical company learns that pending US legislation may restrict the import of active ingredients from certain countries. The CEO convenes a task force that within six months qualifies two alternative suppliers in Ireland and India, avoiding a potential twelve-month supply gap that competitors later experience.

## Examples
- Energy companies restructured global operations after sanctions on Russian oil exports disrupted supply contracts worth billions
- A consumer electronics firm moved final assembly from China to Vietnam to reduce tariff exposure following a US-China trade dispute

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Geopolitical Exposure Map
**Purpose:** Identify where the company is exposed to geopolitics across the value chain.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
country risk + supplier risk + customer risk + asset risk -> total exposure
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: country risk - stability and policy volatility.
Component 2: supplier risk - concentration and dependency.
Component 3: customer risk - demand or access shocks.
Component 4: asset risk - stranded assets, write-downs, or blocked cash.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI hardware company may have low demand risk but high supplier and export-control risk if critical chips come from one country. A consulting firm may face lower asset risk but higher customer risk if a market suddenly becomes off-limits. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone says “we only have one country issue,” but the whole business model is actually exposed.

### Diversification vs Concentration Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether the business should diversify suppliers, markets, or partnerships.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
high concentration -> high shock risk
high diversification -> lower shock risk but more coordination
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: concentration - how dependent the firm is on one country or partner.
Component 2: diversification - the spread of sources and markets.
Component 3: coordination cost - complexity added by multiple options.
Component 4: resilience gain - how much disruption is avoided.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team buying from a single supplier is efficient until sanctions or export controls hit. Splitting sourcing across Mexico, India, and Eastern Europe may raise coordination work but reduce the chance of a total supply stop. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When procurement wants the cheapest plan, but the business needs continuity.

### Response-Speed Ladder
**Purpose:** Match the speed of action to the speed of geopolitical change.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
monitor -> prepare -> pre-qualify -> switch -> reconfigure
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: alert level - how serious the policy or conflict signal is.
Component 2: preparation depth - from monitoring to full contingency.
Component 3: switching cost - how hard it is to move.
Component 4: response time - how quickly the firm can adapt.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud vendor that pre-qualifies secondary datacenters and backup legal structures can switch faster if export controls change overnight. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the firm is waiting for certainty instead of building a fallback.

## 4. Formulas

The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are practical risk metrics that make the chapter actionable. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: Supplier Concentration Index
Formula: `supplier concentration = share of supply from top supplier or top country`
Variables:
`share of supply` = percentage sourced from one supplier/country
Why this formula exists: It answers how badly a single geopolitical event could hit operations.
How to interpret the output:
Low concentration -> more resilient
Moderate concentration -> watch closely
High concentration -> single-point failure risk
Worked example with numbers: If 70 percent of chips come from one country, concentration is 70 percent. Decision: diversify before policy risk becomes production risk.

### Formula 2: Tariff Pass-Through
Formula: `tariff pass-through = price increase to customer / tariff increase` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
`price increase to customer` = part of tariff passed through
`tariff increase` = new tariff cost
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the business can absorb the shock or must reprice.
How to interpret the output:
Low pass-through -> margin squeeze
Moderate pass-through -> shared burden
High pass-through -> customer bears most of the shock
Worked example with numbers: If a 10 percent tariff produces a 6 percent customer price increase, pass-through is 60 percent. Decision: if demand is elastic, absorb more of it or redesign the supply chain.

### Formula 3: Recovery Time
Formula: `recovery time = time disruption starts -> time operations normalize`
Variables:
`time disruption starts` = policy or conflict shock date
`time operations normalize` = when the business is back within service levels
Why this formula exists: It answers how resilient the operating model really is.
How to interpret the output:
Short recovery -> strong contingency
Moderate recovery -> partial resilience
Long recovery -> weak fallback or overconcentration
Worked example with numbers: If a supplier shock lasts 45 days before production stabilizes, recovery time is 45 days. Decision: add backup suppliers or inventory buffers. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 4: Political Risk Exposure Score
Formula: `risk score = exposure x severity x likelihood` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
`exposure` = share of business at risk
`severity` = business impact if the event occurs
`likelihood` = chance of the event
Why this formula exists: It answers where to focus management attention first.
How to interpret the output:
Low score -> monitor
Moderate score -> build contingencies
High score -> take active mitigation steps
Worked example with numbers: If exposure is 0.6, severity is 0.8, and likelihood is 0.5, the score is 0.24. Decision: prioritize the issue in the risk register and assign an owner.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Map exposure by country, supplier, customer, and asset.
2. Rank critical dependencies by concentration and likelihood of disruption.
3. Build backup suppliers, backup markets, and backup payment routes for the highest-risk items.
4. Assign government relations and regulatory intelligence ownership to a named leader.
5. Create a geopolitical trigger list that forces review when policy or conflict indicators change.
6. Test recovery time with tabletop exercises and launch-war game scenarios.
7. Revisit sourcing, pricing, and market-entry decisions after each major geopolitical shift.
