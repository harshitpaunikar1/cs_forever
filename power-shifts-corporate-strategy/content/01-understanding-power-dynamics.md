# Understanding Power Dynamics

## Overview
Power dynamics describe how influence, authority, and control flow between individuals, teams, and institutions within an organization. Every decision, from budget allocation to hiring, is shaped by who holds power and how they choose to use it. Understanding these dynamics helps leaders anticipate resistance, build coalitions, and drive change more effectively.

---

## Why It Matters
Organizations that ignore internal power dynamics often face stalled initiatives, political infighting, and talent loss. When leaders understand who holds formal authority versus informal influence, they can design strategies that align incentives, reduce friction, and accelerate execution. In practice, the companies that manage power well are the ones that adapt fastest to market shifts.

## Key Principles
- Power is relational, not absolute — it depends on context, relationships, and resources
- Formal authority and informal influence often diverge significantly
- Power shifts over time as information, resources, and alliances change
- Transparency about decision-making processes reduces destructive political behavior

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Formal authority** | Power derived from an official role or title within an organizational hierarchy |
| **Informal influence** | The ability to shape outcomes through relationships, expertise, or reputation rather than title |
| **Power asymmetry** | An imbalance in influence between parties in a relationship or negotiation |
| **Coalition building** | The practice of assembling allies to advance a shared goal or agenda |

## Use Case
A mid-level product manager at a tech company maps the informal influence network to identify which engineers and designers can champion a new feature initiative, bypassing a slow formal approval chain.

## Scenario
> A newly promoted VP discovers that the most influential person in the engineering department is not her direct report but a senior individual contributor who mentors half the team. Rather than asserting hierarchical authority, she invests time building a relationship with this engineer, eventually co-sponsoring a major platform migration that both had independently advocated for.

## Examples
- A CFO who controls budget approvals holds outsized power over product strategy even without a product title
- A junior analyst who built the company's key forecasting model becomes the go-to person for strategic decisions, gaining influence far beyond their rank

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Formal vs Informal Power Map
**Purpose:** Separate title-based authority from actual influence.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                INFORMAL INFLUENCE
              low                     high
FORMAL
AUTHORITY
high      manager with control   true sponsor / power broker
low       blocked contributor     trusted expert / fixer
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Formal authority: role-based decision power.
Informal influence: trust, expertise, and network reach.
High-high: ideal sponsor or executive champion.
Low-formal/high-informal: often the real mover in the room.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product roadmap review may be run by the VP, but the senior architect and the customer-success lead can still determine whether the feature is credible and supportable. The map tells the product manager where the real influence sits before asking for approval [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the person with the title is not the person who can actually move the decision.

### Coalition Strength Matrix
**Purpose:** Judge whether a proposal has enough allies to overcome resistance.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    SUPPORT
              weak                     strong
ALIGNMENT
high      easy launch             durable coalition
low       fragile approval        likely stalled
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Alignment: whether stakeholders share the goal.
Support: whether they are willing to act.
Weak alignment/weak support: high failure risk.
Strong alignment/strong support: best launch condition.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud migration proposal needs engineering, security, finance, and operations to back it. If only engineering supports it, the coalition is too weak to survive budget and risk review [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When a plan is technically sound but keeps getting delayed in committees.

### Dependency and Leverage Matrix
**Purpose:** Identify where power asymmetry comes from.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    DEPENDENCY ON YOU
              low                      high
YOUR DEPENDENCY
low       low leverage            mutual dependence
high      vulnerable dependency   fragile bottleneck
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Your dependency: how much you rely on the other party.
Dependency on you: how much they rely on you.
Mutual dependence: encourages negotiation.
Fragile bottleneck: risky because many others depend on you and you depend on few.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI product team that owns the only working model service has high leverage over other teams. If that same team also depends on a single data engineer, the organization has a fragile bottleneck that needs redesign, not just better politics [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When one team is blocking another and everyone is arguing about fairness instead of structure.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Influence Concentration
Formula: `Influence concentration = top influencer connections / total relevant connections`

Variables:
top influencer connections = number of advice or approval links held by the most central actor
total relevant connections = total links across the decision network

Why this formula exists: It estimates how concentrated decision influence is in one node or a small group.

How to interpret the output:
Low concentration -> more distributed power -> coalition building matters
High concentration -> a few people control the outcome -> sponsor management matters

Worked example with numbers: If the top architect is consulted in 18 of 30 critical decisions, influence concentration is 60%. That means technical credibility is highly centralized and that person is a key stakeholder [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 2: Alliance Coverage
Formula: `Alliance coverage = supportive stakeholders / total critical stakeholders`

Variables:
supportive stakeholders = stakeholders aligned with the initiative
total critical stakeholders = all groups that can speed up or block the initiative

Why this formula exists: It tells leaders whether the coalition is broad enough to move.

How to interpret the output:
Below 50% -> likely blockage
50%-80% -> workable but fragile
Above 80% -> strong launch position

Worked example with numbers: If 8 of 10 critical stakeholders support the change, alliance coverage is 80%. That is often enough for launch, provided the two holdouts are not formal veto points [verified from model knowledge, not source].

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Don't assume the title holder is the only source of power. | Do map formal authority and informal influence separately. |
| Don't push a change without knowing the veto points. | Do identify the people who can stop or slow the decision. |
| Don't treat coalition building as politics for its own sake. | Do build an alliance around shared goals and execution speed. |
| Don't ignore dependency asymmetry in vendor or cross-team work. | Do redesign bottlenecks and reduce single-point dependence. |
| Don't hide decision logic in a black box. | Do increase transparency to reduce destructive politics. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Product manager mapping the influence network
**Situation:** A mid-level product manager needs support for a new feature but the formal approval chain is slow. The PM suspects a senior engineer and a design lead have more influence than the official committee.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Formal vs Informal Power Map + Alliance Coverage.

**Analysis:** If the PM maps the network and wins the support of the senior engineer and design lead, the proposal may move even if the committee is skeptical. The real goal is not to win every title in the room; it is to secure the actors that actually sway the decision [verified from model knowledge, not source].

**Decision rule:** If alliance coverage is above 80%, proceed. If it is 50%-80%, add sponsor work. If it is below 50%, pause and rebuild support.

**Action:** Identify the informal influencers, pre-brief them, and revise the pitch around their concerns.

### Scenario 2: Senior IC as hidden power broker
**Situation:** A newly promoted VP discovers that a senior individual contributor mentors half the engineering team and quietly determines whether changes get adopted.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Formal vs Informal Power Map + Influence Concentration.

**Analysis:** The VP’s formal authority is real, but adoption depends on the IC’s informal influence. If influence concentration is high around that IC, the VP should co-sponsor key changes instead of trying to overrule the network [verified from model knowledge, not source].

**Decision rule:** If the IC is a high-centrality influencer, partner with them. If influence is distributed, build a broader coalition. If the IC is a veto point, do not launch without their buy-in.

**Action:** Invite the IC into the design discussion, give them a visible role, and align the change with team-level benefits.

### Scenario 3: CFO budget leverage over product strategy
**Situation:** A CFO controls the budget approvals that determine whether product initiatives get funded, even though the CFO does not own product strategy.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Dependency and Leverage Matrix.

**Analysis:** Product depends on finance for capital, while finance depends on product for growth. The asymmetry gives the CFO strong leverage, but mutual dependence means the best outcome is a negotiated alliance, not a power struggle [verified from model knowledge, not source].

**Decision rule:** If dependency is mostly one-way, expect veto power. If it is mutual, negotiate terms. If it is fragile and concentrated, redesign the approval flow.

**Action:** Quantify the budget bottlenecks, align on investment criteria, and shorten the approval path.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Map formal authority and informal influence before major decisions.
2. Identify veto points, dependencies, and likely allies.
3. Build a coalition around the goal, not the title hierarchy.
4. Increase transparency in decision rules and escalation paths.
5. Reduce single-point dependencies that create bottlenecks.
6. Re-map the network after reorganizations or leadership changes.
7. Use the map to plan communication, not just to observe politics.
