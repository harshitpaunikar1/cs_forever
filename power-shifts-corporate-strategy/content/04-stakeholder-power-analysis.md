# Stakeholder Power Analysis

## Overview
Stakeholder power analysis is the process of identifying who holds influence over a company's decisions and outcomes, and understanding what each stakeholder wants. Stakeholders include investors, employees, regulators, customers, suppliers, and communities. Mapping their interests, resources, and relationships gives leaders a clear picture of who can accelerate or block strategic initiatives.

---

## Why It Matters
Ignoring powerful stakeholders leads to failed mergers, regulatory crackdowns, employee revolts, and customer boycotts. When leaders systematically assess stakeholder power before making major decisions, they can design strategies that build support where it counts and neutralize opposition before it derails execution. The most effective corporate strategies are those that align the interests of key stakeholders rather than working against them.

## Key Principles
- Not all stakeholders are equal — prioritize based on their power, legitimacy, and urgency
- Stakeholder positions are not fixed; they shift as circumstances and incentives change
- Proactive engagement with stakeholders reduces the cost of conflict and builds strategic flexibility
- Mapping stakeholder relationships reveals hidden alliances and potential sources of resistance

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Stakeholder mapping** | A visual framework for categorizing stakeholders by their level of power and interest in a decision |
| **Salience model** | A classification system that ranks stakeholders based on power, legitimacy, and urgency of their claims |
| **Interest alignment** | The degree to which a stakeholder's goals overlap with the organization's strategic objectives |
| **Stakeholder engagement** | The deliberate process of communicating with and involving stakeholders in decision-making |

## Use Case
Before announcing a major factory relocation, a manufacturing CEO conducts a stakeholder analysis to understand which local politicians, union leaders, and community groups could influence public perception and regulatory approval.

## Scenario
> A pharmaceutical company plans to raise the price of a widely used drug by 30%. Stakeholder analysis reveals that patient advocacy groups have strong media connections and that two key senators sit on the health committee reviewing drug pricing. The company revises its approach, implementing a smaller increase paired with an expanded patient assistance program, avoiding the public backlash that a competitor faced months earlier.

## Examples
- Activist investors used their shareholder voting power to force a major oil company to add climate-focused directors to its board
- A tech company surveyed its top enterprise customers before changing its API pricing, adjusting the model after discovering that three of its five largest clients would have switched providers

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices
The frameworks below translate the source into a decision system for prioritization and engagement [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Power-Interest Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide how much attention each stakeholder deserves.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                  Interest
             Low                    High
Power +---------------------------------------------+
High  | keep satisfied          | manage closely
Low   | monitor                 | keep informed
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: power - ability to influence outcomes.
Component 2: interest - how much the stakeholder cares about the issue.
Component 3: keep satisfied - high power, lower interest stakeholders need reassurance.
Component 4: manage closely - high power, high interest stakeholders need active engagement.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: If a large enterprise customer has low day-to-day interest but high renewal power, the account team should keep them satisfied. If the same customer is deeply involved in an API pricing change, they move into the manage-closely quadrant [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is spending too much time on low-power voices or ignoring high-power ones.

### Salience Triangle
**Purpose:** Rank stakeholders by power, legitimacy, and urgency.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
power + legitimacy + urgency -> stakeholder salience
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: power - ability to force a decision.
Component 2: legitimacy - credibility or rightful claim.
Component 3: urgency - time pressure or issue immediacy.
Component 4: salience - who must be handled first.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: In a data privacy issue, a regulator has power and legitimacy, a customer has legitimacy and urgency, and a journalist may add urgency through media amplification. The salience ranking tells the company whom to answer first and with what level of detail [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When multiple stakeholders have competing claims and you need a priority order.

### Engagement Ladder
**Purpose:** Choose the right engagement intensity for each stakeholder group.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
inform -> consult -> involve -> partner
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: inform - one-way communication.
Component 2: consult - ask for input before deciding.
Component 3: involve - include stakeholders in shaping the outcome.
Component 4: partner - co-design with the stakeholder because stakes are high.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team may inform most customers about a minor UI change, consult enterprise clients on a pricing redesign, involve support teams in workflow design, and partner with a strategic customer on a new integration [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When a single communication plan is being used for stakeholders with very different stakes.

## 4. Formulas
The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are working metrics for prioritizing and managing stakeholders [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Stakeholder Priority Score
Formula: `Stakeholder Priority Score = (Power + Legitimacy + Urgency) / 3`
Variables:
Power = ability to affect outcomes
Legitimacy = validity of the stakeholder's claim
Urgency = time pressure or criticality
Why this formula exists: It answers which stakeholders need immediate attention.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.40 -> monitor
Value 0.40-0.70 -> engage
Value > 0.70 -> manage closely
Worked example with numbers: If a regulator scores 1.0 on power, 0.9 on legitimacy, and 0.8 on urgency, the priority score is 0.9. Decision: address the regulator before scaling the initiative [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 2: Alignment Gap
Formula: `Alignment Gap = Stakeholder Objective Score - Strategy Fit Score`
Variables:
Stakeholder Objective Score = how much the stakeholder wants a different outcome
Strategy Fit Score = how well their objective matches the plan
Why this formula exists: It answers how much negotiation or compensation is needed.
How to interpret the output:
Value near 0 -> easy alignment
Value moderate -> negotiation needed
Value high -> expect resistance or tradeoff
Worked example with numbers: If an enterprise customer wants 0.9 levels of customization and the product strategy can support 0.6, the gap is 0.3. Decision: offer a limited customization tier instead of a full bespoke build [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 3: Engagement Coverage
Formula: `Engagement Coverage = Engaged High-Priority Stakeholders / Total High-Priority Stakeholders`
Variables:
Engaged High-Priority Stakeholders = high-priority stakeholders already contacted and actively managed
Total High-Priority Stakeholders = all stakeholders with high priority scores
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the team has covered the people who matter most.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.50 -> major blind spots
Value 0.50-0.80 -> partial coverage
Value > 0.80 -> strong coverage
Worked example with numbers: If 6 of 8 high-priority stakeholders are actively engaged, coverage is 75%. Decision: continue the rollout but close the remaining gaps before launch [verified from model knowledge, not source].

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat every stakeholder as equally important. | Prioritize by power, legitimacy, and urgency. |
| Assume a quiet stakeholder is unimportant. | Check for hidden power and alliances. |
| Communicate the same way to everyone. | Match engagement intensity to the stakeholder's stake. |
| Wait for conflict to appear before mapping stakeholders. | Map stakeholders before the decision is public. |
| Confuse agreement with alignment. | Test whether the stakeholder's real objective matches yours. |
| Ignore stakeholders outside the org chart. | Include customers, regulators, communities, and suppliers. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Enterprise API pricing change
Situation: A tech company wants to change API pricing. Three major customers may switch providers if the new model is too aggressive.
Applicable framework/metric: Power-Interest Matrix and Engagement Coverage.
Analysis: If all three customers are high power and high interest, they must be managed closely. If only two are engaged, coverage is 67%, which is not enough for a disruptive pricing change [verified from model knowledge, not source].
Decision rule: If a high-power customer is at risk, partner or consult early. If the stakeholder is low power, inform. If coverage is below 80%, delay launch.
Action: Run account-level workshops, test the pricing model with customer finance teams, and offer a transition plan.

### Scenario 2: AI governance and regulator pressure
Situation: A company is rolling out an internal AI assistant while a regulator is asking for model-use controls and documentation.
Applicable framework/metric: Salience Triangle and Stakeholder Priority Score.
Analysis: The regulator likely has high power, high legitimacy, and high urgency, giving a score near 1.0. That makes them the first stakeholder to engage, even if the product team would rather focus on the launch [verified from model knowledge, not source].
Decision rule: If power and legitimacy are high, respond first. If urgency is high but legitimacy is weak, investigate before overcommitting. If the score is moderate, consult rather than partner.
Action: Prepare documentation, involve legal and security early, and create a communication plan for the launch.

### Scenario 3: Board, activist investors, and climate commitments
Situation: A large platform company is facing pressure from activist investors to improve climate disclosures and governance. The board is supportive, but operations worry about cost.
Applicable framework/metric: Stakeholder Priority Score and Alignment Gap.
Analysis: If the investors score high on power and urgency and the board has high legitimacy, the priority score is high. A moderate alignment gap may be resolved with staged disclosure and a practical roadmap instead of a fight [verified from model knowledge, not source].
Decision rule: If the priority score is high, address it now. If the gap is low, communicate. If the gap is high, negotiate a phased commitment.
Action: Create a disclosure roadmap, define measurable milestones, and brief the board before the public announcement.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. List every stakeholder group that can affect the decision, including outside-the-org groups.
2. Score each stakeholder on power, legitimacy, and urgency.
3. Segment stakeholders into inform, consult, involve, or partner.
4. Identify hidden alliances and map who moves together.
5. Decide which concessions are strategic compromises and which would damage the core strategy.
6. Build an engagement plan with owners, messages, and deadlines.
7. Re-score stakeholders after the environment changes, because positions are not fixed.
