# Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning

## Overview

Segmentation divides a broad market into smaller groups that share needs or traits. Targeting chooses which segments to serve. Positioning decides how the brand should be seen inside those segments. The three steps — STP — are the backbone of strategy.

---

## Why It Matters

A company cannot be everything to everyone. STP helps focus limited resources on the groups most likely to buy and pay well. It also shapes the brand message so it resonates sharply instead of sounding generic.

## Key Principles

- Segments must be measurable, reachable, and profitable.
- Target the segments where you have a real competitive edge.
- Positioning lives in the consumer's mind, not on your slides.
- A clear position beats a clever one that nobody understands.
- Revisit STP when markets, tech, or consumer tastes shift.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Segmentation** | Dividing a market into groups with shared characteristics. |
| **Targeting** | Selecting which segments the business will serve. |
| **Positioning** | The place a brand occupies in the customer's mind versus rivals. |
| **Value Proposition** | The specific benefit a brand offers its target segment. |

## Use Case

A fintech startup offering investment advice segments by age and income, targets millennials earning above a threshold, and positions itself as a simple, mobile-first alternative to traditional wealth managers.

## Scenario

> A national ice-cream brand sold one flavor range across all cities. After segmenting by climate and culture, it launched spiced flavors in the north and fruit-heavy ones in coastal cities. Sales rose 22% and brand preference scores doubled in key metros.

## Examples

- Tata Motors sells Nano for budget buyers and Jaguar for luxury — clear segment split.
- Dove positions itself as "real beauty" to stand apart from glamour-driven rivals.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

The frameworks below extend the source with standard STP practice [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Segmentation Screen
**Purpose:** Filter out segments that are not worth pursuing.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Market -> measure size -> test reachability -> test profitability -> shortlist
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Measurable, meaning the segment can be sized and tracked.
Component 2: Reachable, meaning the business can access the segment through a channel.
Component 3: Profitable, meaning the segment can produce enough value after costs.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company can identify small agencies, mid-market retailers, and enterprise teams, but only the enterprise team may be both reachable through the sales force and profitable enough after onboarding and support. The decision is to cut the weaker segments early. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is tempted to target everyone who looks interesting.

### Attractiveness vs Fit Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide which segments deserve priority.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                     Company fit
                  Low                 High
Attractiveness +----------------+----------------+
High           | Partner / watch | Invest now    |
Low            | Ignore          | Niche only    |
               +----------------+----------------+
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Attractiveness, meaning size, growth, margin, and strategic value.
Component 2: Fit, meaning the company's product, channel, and brand advantages.
Component 3: Investment choice, meaning whether to prioritize, partner, or ignore.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A fintech startup may find high-income millennials attractive, but if its onboarding and compliance stack fit that group best, it should invest there before chasing every demographic. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

When to pull this out in a meeting: When sales wants large segments and product wants easy segments, but nobody has combined both views.

### Positioning Map
**Purpose:** Show where the brand sits in the consumer's mind versus competitors.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
             Simplicity
                ^
                |
     premium    |      simple
                |
--------------- + -----------------> Price
                |
     complex    |
                |
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Attribute axis 1, such as price, simplicity, trust, or speed.
Component 2: Attribute axis 2, such as premium feel, innovation, or service quality.
Component 3: Relative distance, meaning whether the brand is clearly different from rivals.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team can position an AI note-taking app as "simple and reliable" rather than "feature-heavy and technical" so the brand is easier to remember for consultants and sales teams. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the message sounds good internally but is hard to tell apart in the market.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below extend the source with standard segmentation and positioning practice [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Segment Attractiveness Score
Formula: `Segment attractiveness score = 0.30*size + 0.25*growth + 0.25*margin + 0.20*reachability`
Variables:
size = normalized market size score
growth = normalized growth score
margin = normalized profit score
reachability = normalized channel access score
Why this formula exists: It answers "Which segments are worth focusing on first?"
How to interpret the output:
Value < 2.5 -> weak segment -> ignore or watch
Value 2.5-3.5 -> test segment -> pilot with low spend
Value > 3.5 -> strong segment -> prioritize and invest
Worked example with numbers: If a segment scores size 4, growth 5, margin 3, and reachability 4 on a 1-5 scale, the attractiveness score is `0.30*4 + 0.25*5 + 0.25*3 + 0.20*4 = 4.0`. That is a target-worthy segment. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Fit Score
Formula: `Fit score = average(product fit, channel fit, brand fit)`
Variables:
product fit = how well the offer solves the segment's need
channel fit = how well the segment can be reached and served
brand fit = how believable the brand is for that segment
Why this formula exists: It answers "Can we actually win this segment?"
How to interpret the output:
Value < 2.5 -> weak fit -> avoid for now
Value 2.5-3.5 -> moderate fit -> partner or test
Value > 3.5 -> strong fit -> invest directly
Worked example with numbers: If product fit = 4, channel fit = 3, and brand fit = 4, the fit score is `3.67`. That suggests a serious go-to-market opportunity. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Positioning Distance
Formula: `Positioning distance = sqrt((x1 - x2)^2 + (y1 - y2)^2)`
Variables:
x1, y1 = coordinates of your brand on the perceptual map
x2, y2 = coordinates of the competitor or desired position
Why this formula exists: It answers "How far are we from the position we want to own?"
How to interpret the output:
Value < 1.0 -> too close -> sharpen the difference
Value 1.0-2.5 -> workable -> keep reinforcing the position
Value > 2.5 -> far apart -> either reposition or keep the gap if it is valuable
Worked example with numbers: If the desired position is (4, 5) and the current position is (2, 2), distance = `sqrt((4-2)^2 + (5-2)^2) = sqrt(13) = 3.6`. The brand is not yet sitting in the intended spot. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Target a segment just because it is large. | Target the segment that is large, reachable, and profitable. |
| Use the same message for every customer group. | Tailor the value proposition to the chosen segment. |
| Position the brand on a slide and assume the market will agree. | Check the perceptual map against actual customer perception. |
| Chase a segment your product cannot serve well. | Make fit a hard filter before spending heavily. |
| Confuse niche strategy with weak strategy. | Use a niche if it is profitable and strategically defensible. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Fintech investing app
Situation: A fintech startup offers investment advice and must choose between students, young professionals, and high-income millennials. The product team wants one segment to dominate the launch instead of spreading the budget thin.
Applicable framework/metric: Segment attractiveness score and fit score.
Analysis: High-income millennials score 4.1 on attractiveness and 3.8 on fit, while students score 3.0 on attractiveness and 2.4 on fit. The first group has the better combined case because it can pay, can be reached, and needs a mobile-first experience. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If attractiveness is below 2.5, ignore the segment. If it is 2.5-3.5, test it. If it is above 3.5 and fit is also above 3.5, invest.
Action: Launch one tightly defined version of the product and one message for the winning segment instead of a broad market push.

### Scenario 2: Regional ice-cream brand
Situation: A national ice-cream brand used one flavor range across every city. It now wants to segment by climate and culture so product and marketing are better matched to local taste.
Applicable framework/metric: Positioning map and segment attractiveness score.
Analysis: The north segment responds better to spiced flavors, while the coast segment responds better to fruit-heavy options. After the split, sales rise 22% and preference scores double in key metros, showing that the local position is more believable. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If a segment is measurable, reachable, and profitable, build for it. If not, keep it as a watchlist segment.
Action: Adjust flavors, packaging, and local messaging, then measure repeat purchase by segment.

### Scenario 3: B2B AI note-taking tool
Situation: A product team is choosing whether to target consultants, sales teams, or students. Each group uses the tool differently, but only one group will produce strong CLV without high support cost.
Applicable framework/metric: Attractiveness vs fit matrix.
Analysis: Consultants have high willingness to pay but moderate channel reach; sales teams have strong product fit and strong channel access through managers; students have low CLV and weak profitability. The matrix pushes the team toward sales teams first. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If fit is high and attractiveness is high, invest now. If attractiveness is high but fit is low, partner or watch. If both are low, ignore.
Action: Narrow the landing page, sales pitch, and onboarding to one primary customer type before adding new segments.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Define the market broadly enough to see options, but narrowly enough to compare segments.
2. Score each segment for size, growth, profitability, and reachability.
3. Score company fit using product, channel, and brand capability.
4. Choose one or two priority segments and write a single-line value proposition for each.
5. Map the chosen position against competitors with a perceptual map.
6. Test the message, channel, and offer with a small pilot before scaling.
7. Re-score the segments whenever market structure, technology, or customer behavior changes.
