# Retailing and Consumer Psychology

## Overview

Retailing is where consumer psychology becomes concrete — through store layout, music, lighting, pricing signs, and shelf placement. Small design choices shift how customers feel, how long they stay, and how much they buy. The store is a stage, and consumers are the audience.

---

## Why It Matters

Most purchase decisions happen in the store, not before walking in. Retailers who apply psychology well turn browsers into buyers and raise basket size without raising prices. Those who ignore it lose sales to rivals who stage a better experience.

## Key Principles

- Place high-margin items at eye level, essentials at the back.
- Use color, music, and smell to set mood and pace.
- Offer a small "decoy" option to make a mid-tier choice look like a bargain.
- Reduce friction at checkout; every extra second loses customers.
- Train staff to read body language and nudge gently, not push.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Planogram** | A map of where each product sits on the shelf. |
| **Atmospherics** | The sensory environment of the store — light, sound, scent. |
| **Decoy Pricing** | Adding an extra option to steer choice toward a target product. |
| **Impulse Buy** | A purchase made without prior plan, often at checkout. |

## Use Case

A supermarket rearranges its fresh produce section with natural wood shelving, gentle lighting, and a bakery scent to create a farmers-market feel — lifting basket size among premium shoppers.

## Scenario

> A clothing chain added mirrors and softer lighting to its fitting rooms and repositioned accessories near the checkout. Conversion rose 14% and impulse accessory sales grew 28% — without any product or price change.

## Examples

- IKEA's winding store path exposes shoppers to far more products than a grid would.
- Big Bazaar uses loud color and hawker-style announcements to mimic a bazaar feel.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Store Influence Funnel
**Purpose:** Show how a shopper moves from entry to checkout, and where store design can change the path.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
entry -> orient -> browse -> compare -> pick -> basket -> checkout
  |        |         |         |        |        |         |
store cues atmospherics planogram  decoy  impulse  pricing  friction
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Entry: first impression and directional clarity.
Orient: whether shoppers quickly understand the store.
Browse: whether shelf placement and lighting invite movement.
Compare: whether pricing signs and product grouping support choice.
Pick: the moment a shopper selects an item.
Basket and checkout: whether impulse additions and payment flow convert interest into revenue.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A supermarket uses eye-level placement for premium coffee, keeps essentials at the back to increase path length, and adds a bakery scent near fresh produce. The funnel helps the team see whether the issue is discovery, comparison, or payment. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the store is busy but basket size or conversion is not moving.

### Atmospherics vs Intent Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether sensory design should be loud, subtle, or almost invisible based on shopper intent.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                   Shopper intent
                low                     high
Atmospherics high | exploratory shopping | premium experience
Atmospherics low  | quick mission buy     | utilitarian convenience
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Shopper intent: whether the customer is browsing or on a mission.
Atmospherics intensity: how much the store uses light, music, scent, and color.
Exploratory shopping: higher sensory intensity helps the shopper stay longer.
Quick mission buy: low sensory noise keeps the visit efficient.
Premium experience: sensory cues can justify higher price and better margin.
Utilitarian convenience: the store should optimize speed and clarity.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A boutique fashion store can use softer lighting and curated music because shoppers are browsing and receptive. A pharmacy should use clearer signage and faster checkout because the buyer is on a mission. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone wants to copy the same store experience for very different shopping missions.

## 4. Formulas
The source is conceptual, so these metrics are the standard ways retail teams quantify store psychology interventions. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: `Basket size uplift % = (New average basket value - Baseline average basket value) / Baseline average basket value`
Variables:
New average basket value = basket value after the store change.
Baseline average basket value = basket value before the change.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the store design is increasing purchase value.
How to interpret the output:
Value below 5% -> weak change; revise the layout or cue.
Value 5% to 15% -> meaningful store test; refine and repeat.
Value above 15% -> strong enough to scale to more stores. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Worked example with numbers: If basket value rises from 800 to 880, uplift is 10%. That supports keeping the new shelf layout. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: `Conversion rate = Buyers / Shoppers`
Variables:
Buyers = people who actually purchase.
Shoppers = people who entered or were counted in the store.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the store turns footfall into sales.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> merchandising or checkout is failing.
Value moderate -> the store is functional but still improvable.
Value high -> the store experience is doing its job well. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Worked example with numbers: If 240 of 1,000 shoppers buy, conversion is 24%. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: `Impulse attachment rate = Impulse purchases / Total transactions`
Variables:
Impulse purchases = items bought without prior planning.
Total transactions = all completed shopping baskets.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the store is capturing unplanned add-on sales.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> checkout or aisle cues are not converting browsing into extras.
Value moderate -> the store is getting some add-on sales.
Value high -> the environment is strongly stimulating impulse buying. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Worked example with numbers: If 90 of 300 transactions include an impulse add-on, impulse attachment rate is 30%. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Put premium items where nobody looks. | Place high-margin items at eye level. |
| Make the store visually busy without a clear path. | Use layout to guide shoppers toward categories and checkout. |
| Treat atmosphere as decoration only. | Use light, music, color, and scent to support the intended shopper mood. |
| Add a decoy option without checking whether it really shifts choice. | Measure whether decoy pricing steers customers toward the target product. |
| Let checkout friction erase the rest of the store experience. | Remove delays, confusion, and payment pain at the last step. |
| Push staff to hard-sell every customer. | Train staff to read body language and nudge gently. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Premium supermarket produce
Situation: A supermarket wants premium shoppers to spend more in the fresh produce section. The retail team adds natural wood shelving, gentle lighting, and a bakery scent to create a farmers-market feel.  
Applicable framework/metric: Store Influence Funnel and basket size uplift %.  
Analysis: If average basket value rises from 1,200 to 1,320, uplift is 10%. That suggests the atmospherics are helping shoppers stay longer and buy more.  
Decision rule: If uplift is above 15%, scale. If it is 5% to 15%, refine and retest. If it is below 5%, adjust the sensory cues or shelf placement.  
Action: Keep the produce story consistent, measure basket value by zone, and compare premium versus value shoppers separately. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Scenario 2: Clothing chain fitting rooms
Situation: A clothing chain wants more fitting-room visitors to convert and add accessories. It adds mirrors, softer lighting, and accessories near checkout so the shopper sees the outfit and the add-ons together.  
Applicable framework/metric: Atmospherics vs Intent Matrix and impulse attachment rate.  
Analysis: If conversion rises 14% and impulse accessory sales rise 28%, the store is monetizing both mood and convenience.  
Decision rule: If conversion improves but basket size does not, keep the layout but strengthen cross-sell cues. If impulse sales rise without conversion, the store may be over-pushing.  
Action: Keep the mirrors and lighting, test accessory placement by zone, and train staff to suggest only one relevant add-on. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Scenario 3: Electronics store decoy pricing
Situation: An electronics retailer wants shoppers to choose the mid-tier laptop rather than the cheapest one. It introduces a decoy option and places premium accessories at eye level.  
Applicable framework/metric: Store Influence Funnel and conversion rate.  
Analysis: If the mid-tier model’s share moves from 22% to 31%, the decoy is doing its job. If total conversion also rises from 18% to 21%, the layout is not just steering choice but adding overall demand.  
Decision rule: If the target product share rises without hurting total conversion, keep the pricing architecture. If shoppers just defect to the decoy or leave, redesign the comparison set.  
Action: Rebuild the comparison board, audit shelf placement, and track attachment of warranty and accessories. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Map the store journey from entrance to checkout and note where shoppers hesitate.
2. Identify high-margin products that should move to eye level or better-adjacent positions.
3. Tune atmospherics by store mission: exploratory, premium, or quick convenience.
4. Test decoy pricing in one category before applying it across the whole store.
5. Measure basket value, conversion, and impulse attachment at the zone level.
6. Train frontline staff to read body language and intervene lightly, not aggressively.
7. Fix checkout friction last, because it can erase the value created earlier in the store.
