# Consumer Research Methods

## Overview

Consumer research is the organized way of learning about customers — their needs, habits, and opinions. Methods range from simple surveys and focus groups to observation, experiments, and big-data analysis. The right method depends on the question being asked and the budget in hand.

---

## Why It Matters

Guessing about customers is expensive. Research turns gut feel into evidence, reducing the risk of launching the wrong product or running the wrong campaign. It is a small investment that saves much larger mistakes.

## Key Principles

- Match the method to the question: surveys for scale, interviews for depth.
- Combine quantitative and qualitative methods for a full picture.
- Watch what people do, not just what they say.
- Avoid leading questions that push toward a preferred answer.
- Revisit research regularly — consumer tastes shift.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Qualitative Research** | Methods that explore motives and feelings through words and observation. |
| **Quantitative Research** | Methods that measure behavior and opinions using numbers. |
| **Focus Group** | A small moderated discussion used to probe attitudes. |
| **A/B Testing** | Comparing two versions to see which performs better. |

## Use Case

Before launching a new cereal, a food company runs focus groups for flavor feedback, then a 2,000-person survey to size the market, and in-store tests to measure actual purchase.

## Scenario

> A subscription box startup thought customers wanted more variety. A survey confirmed it, but focus groups showed the real issue was delivery reliability. Fixing logistics lifted renewal rates 18% — something more variety alone would have missed.

## Examples

- Nielsen uses panels to track what households buy each week.
- Tech firms run A/B tests on landing pages to pick winning headlines.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Consumer research methods are the tools used to learn what customers need, what they say, and what they actually do.
For an IT/AI/Product/Consulting leader, the point is to reduce launch risk, avoid building on anecdotes, and choose the cheapest method that can answer the decision at hand.
The decision it helps make is whether to use surveys, focus groups, observation, experiments, or big-data analysis for the question and budget available.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Question-to-Method Fit Matrix
**Purpose:** Match the research method to the decision question instead of using the cheapest or most familiar method.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                   Decision need
             Explore why           Validate what works
Words        Focus group            Survey
Behavior     Observation            Experiment / A/B testing / big-data analysis
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Explore why - use when the team does not yet understand the motive or language.
Component 2: Validate what works - use when the team already has a hypothesis and needs proof.
Component 3: Words - useful for attitudes, objections, and concept language.
Component 4: Behavior - useful for actions, friction, and actual uptake.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team hears that users "want more features" but churn data shows they drop during onboarding. The matrix pushes the team to start with observation, then run a survey, then A/B test a shorter flow. That produces a decision on whether the issue is messaging, product complexity, or both.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone proposes a focus group for a problem that needs a conversion test, or a survey for a problem that needs observation.

### Say-Do Gap Map
**Purpose:** Compare what customers say with what they actually do.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                 Observed behavior
               Low                 High
Stated intent
High        Say more, do less     Say and do
Low         Say less, do less     Quiet adopters
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Stated intent - what respondents claim they will do.
Component 2: Observed behavior - what they actually do in real settings.
Component 3: Gap size - the distance between talk and action.
Component 4: Risk - how dangerous it is to trust the verbal answer alone.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS team hears that customers say they would use a new analytics feature, but product telemetry shows almost nobody clicks the current equivalent. The map shows that the right next move is observation and experiment, not more narrative feedback.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the research result sounds positive but the field data says the opposite.

### Triangulation Loop
**Purpose:** Combine qualitative research, quantitative research, and behavioral evidence into one decision.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Focus group -> Survey -> Observation / Experiment -> Decision
      ^                                               |
      +----------------- Big-data analysis -----------+
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Qualitative research - explains language and motive.
Component 2: Quantitative research - estimates how common the pattern is.
Component 3: Observation / experiment - checks real behavior.
Component 4: Big-data analysis - adds scale and repeatability.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consumer app team starts with a focus group, quantifies the issue in a survey, checks drop-off in logs, and A/B tests a redesigned screen. The loop turns one-off comments into an evidence-backed launch decision.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team has too much opinion and not enough triangulated evidence.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below are practical heuristics [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Survey Response Rate [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Survey response rate = completed responses / invitations sent`
Variables:
completed responses = usable answers returned
invitations sent = total people asked
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the survey produced enough data to trust the read.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.10 -> weak reach or poor incentive -> redesign the sample and invitation
Value 0.10-0.30 -> workable for many business surveys -> check bias and representation
Value > 0.30 -> strong field performance -> proceed with analysis
Worked example with numbers: If 420 people respond out of 1,500 invited, response rate is 28%. That is good enough for a product research survey, assuming the sample is representative.

### Formula 2: A/B Lift [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `A/B lift = metric for version B - metric for version A`
Variables:
metric for version A = baseline conversion, click-through, or retention
metric for version B = test variant conversion, click-through, or retention
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the change actually improved behavior.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> version B is worse -> keep version A or redesign
Value 0-0.02 -> small lift -> test again or segment by cohort
Value > 0.02 -> meaningful lift -> consider rollout
Worked example with numbers: If onboarding completion rises from 54% to 66%, A/B lift is 12 percentage points. That is enough to justify rollout if the sample size was adequate.

### Formula 3: Say-Do Gap [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Say-do gap = stated interest rate - observed action rate`
Variables:
stated interest rate = share of respondents saying they will act
observed action rate = share who actually act in real usage
Why this formula exists: It answers how much verbal research is overstating demand.
How to interpret the output:
Value near 0 -> stated intent is a fair proxy for action
Value 0.05-0.20 -> moderate gap -> use mixed methods
Value > 0.20 -> large gap -> trust behavior more than speech
Worked example with numbers: If 68% say they want the feature but only 41% use it after release, the gap is 27 points. That means product decisions should lean on observation and experiments, not only surveys.

### Formula 4: Research ROI [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Research ROI = (avoided loss or incremental value - research cost) / research cost`
Variables:
avoided loss or incremental value = cost saved by avoiding a bad launch or by improving performance
research cost = budget spent on the study
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the research paid for itself.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> the study cost more than it saved
Value 0-1 -> break-even to modest value -> keep only if decision risk was high
Value > 1 -> strong return -> repeat the method in similar cases
Worked example with numbers: If a 2 lakh rupee study prevents a 15 lakh rupee bad launch, research ROI is `(15 - 2) / 2 = 6.5`. That is a clear yes for the next similar launch.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Use a focus group to estimate market size. | Use a survey or panel when you need scale. |
| Trust what people say without checking what they do. | Compare stated intent with observation or telemetry. |
| Run an A/B test without a specific hypothesis. | Define one change and one success metric first. |
| Treat big-data analysis as proof of causality. | Use big-data analysis to spot patterns, then test them. |
| Spend heavily on research with no decision attached. | Tie every study to a product, pricing, or launch choice. |
| Ask broad questions that cannot change a decision. | Ask only questions that can alter a real action. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Subscription Box Says Variety, But Churn Says Reliability
Situation: A subscription box startup runs a survey that says 68% of customers want more variety. In focus groups, however, delivery reliability comes up in 11 of 12 sessions, and support logs show late arrivals are driving cancellations. The team needs to know whether to invest in assortment or operations.
Applicable framework/metric: Say-Do Gap Map and Triangulation Loop.
Analysis: The stated-interest rate for variety is 68%, but only 41% of churned customers mention variety while 79% mention delivery reliability. The say-do gap on variety is 27 points, which is too large to trust as the main driver. The better hypothesis is that reliability is the bottleneck.
Decision rule: If the say-do gap is above 20 points, trust behavior more than speech. If it is between 5 and 20 points, use mixed methods. If it is below 5 points, the survey result is probably directionally sound.
Action: Fix fulfillment, then rerun the survey after the service issue is reduced so the team does not optimize the wrong lever.

### Scenario 2: SaaS Onboarding Redesign
Situation: A SaaS product team sees that only 54% of new users finish onboarding. The team wants to know whether a shorter flow or a more explanatory flow will perform better.
Applicable framework/metric: Question-to-Method Fit Matrix and A/B Lift.
Analysis: Version A completes onboarding at 54% and version B completes at 66%, so the lift is 12 percentage points. That is a direct behavioral answer, which is stronger than asking users which flow they prefer after the fact.
Decision rule: If lift is negative, keep the control. If lift is between 0 and 2 points, retest or segment. If lift is above 2 points and the sample is adequate, roll out the winner.
Action: Ship version B to a larger cohort, then watch downstream retention before declaring victory.

### Scenario 3: Consumer Packaged Goods Panel and In-Store Test
Situation: A CPG team is deciding whether a new pack design should launch nationally. Nielsen-style panel data show repeat purchase is flat, while an in-store test shows the new pack raises unit sell-through from 1.8 to 2.3 units per store per day in two pilot regions.
Applicable framework/metric: Triangulation Loop and Research ROI.
Analysis: The panel suggests the long-run effect is uncertain, but the in-store test shows a short-run lift of 0.5 units per store per day. If the margin contribution from that lift exceeds the test cost, the launch can be justified; if not, the team should refine the pack before scaling.
Decision rule: If field lift is above the cost threshold, expand. If the lift is positive but small, iterate. If the lift is flat or negative, stop the rollout.
Action: Hold the national launch, refine the pack graphics, and run one more regional store test.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Write a one-page research decision memo that names the business choice, not just the information need.
2. Classify the question as exploratory, descriptive, or causal before choosing a method.
3. Use focus groups for language and motive, surveys for scale, and observation for actual behavior.
4. Define one metric for each experiment so A/B testing does not become a vague opinion contest.
5. Pull big-data analysis into the loop only after the team has a clear hypothesis to test.
6. Triangulate the result with at least two methods before changing product, pricing, or launch plans.
7. Record the decision, the evidence, and the follow-up metric in a research log so the next team can reuse it.
