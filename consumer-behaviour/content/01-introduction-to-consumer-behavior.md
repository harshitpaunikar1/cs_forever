# Introduction to Consumer Behavior

## Overview

Consumer behavior is the study of how people choose, buy, use, and dispose of products and services. It looks at the thinking, feelings, and actions that shape every purchase, from a bottle of water to a new car. The goal is to understand the customer well enough to predict what they will do next.

---

## Why It Matters

Every marketing rupee is wasted if it targets the wrong person with the wrong message. Understanding consumer behavior tells a business who to sell to, what to offer, where to reach them, and what to charge. It is the difference between a product that flies off the shelf and one that sits in the warehouse.

## Key Principles

- Consumers are not always rational; emotion and habit drive many choices.
- Buying is a process, not a single moment.
- Social, cultural, and personal factors all influence decisions.
- Needs come before wants; both must be met to win loyalty.
- Understanding the customer beats shouting louder than competitors.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Buying Process Map
**Purpose:** Show consumer behavior as a sequence, not a single moment.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Need -> want -> search -> evaluate -> purchase -> use -> dispose
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: need recognition - the trigger that starts the process.
Component 2: information search and evaluation - how the consumer reduces uncertainty.
Component 3: purchase and use - the actual transaction and the experience afterward.
Component 4: disposal - the end state that can influence the next purchase.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A beverage brand notices urban youth search online before trying an energy drink, while parents buy the same category in bulk after comparing size and value. The map shows why the same product needs different packaging and message at different stages.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team talks only about advertising and ignores search, evaluation, and post-purchase use.

### Influence Stack
**Purpose:** Separate what the consumer wants from what the surrounding context pushes them to do.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Personal -> Social -> Cultural
   \           |          /
        choice and purchase
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: personal factors - age, income, personality, life stage.
Component 2: social factors - family, friends, peers, reference groups.
Component 3: cultural factors - norms, values, and shared meaning.
Component 4: purchase choice - the final action that reflects the stack.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A car buyer may want mileage because income is tight, but the final brand choice may tilt toward status after neighbor comments. The stack makes clear that messaging should address both utility and identity.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When demand looks irrational until you inspect who the buyer is trying to impress or avoid disappointing.

### Need-Want-Loyalty Ladder
**Purpose:** Connect basic motivation to repeat behavior and brand stickiness.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Need -> Want -> Trial -> Repeat purchase -> Loyalty
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: need - the underlying requirement.
Component 2: want - the specific product expression of that need.
Component 3: trial and repeat purchase - whether the first experience converts into habit.
Component 4: loyalty - whether the consumer keeps choosing the same brand.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A toothpaste brand may win trial because of dentist recommendation, but loyalty only appears if the taste, trust, and convenience all stay aligned after the first purchase.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team celebrates first purchase but has not shown repeat behavior.

## 4. Formulas
The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are practical marketing heuristics [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Purchase Conversion Rate [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Purchase Conversion Rate = buyers / exposed consumers`
Variables:
buyers = consumers who actually purchase
exposed consumers = consumers who saw the offer, ad, or sample
Why this formula exists: It answers how many exposed people convert into buyers.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.05 -> weak message or poor fit
Value 0.05-0.15 -> workable for many consumer offers
Value > 0.15 -> strong offer-message match
Worked example with numbers: If 140 of 1,000 sampled shoppers buy, conversion is 14%. Decision: keep the product, then test packaging or placement to push above 15%.

### Formula 2: Repeat Purchase Rate [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Repeat Purchase Rate = repeat buyers / total buyers`
Variables:
repeat buyers = buyers who come back after the first purchase
total buyers = all first-time buyers
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the product creates habit, trust, or loyalty.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.40 -> the product is attracting curiosity more than retention
Value 0.40-0.70 -> acceptable, but worth strengthening the experience
Value > 0.70 -> strong habit or loyalty signal
Worked example with numbers: If 500 of 800 first-time buyers repurchase, repeat purchase rate is 62.5%. Decision: improve retention messaging or product experience before spending more on acquisition.

### Formula 3: Brand Preference Share [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Brand Preference Share = brand choices / category choices`
Variables:
brand choices = times the brand is selected
category choices = total choices in the category
Why this formula exists: It answers how much of the category the brand is winning in the consumer's mind.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> brand is not mentally distinctive
Value medium -> brand is in consideration but not dominant
Value high -> brand has clear preference or status
Worked example with numbers: If a premium car model is chosen 34 times out of 50 evaluated purchases, preference share is 68%. Decision: keep the status message, but add trust and ownership-cost cues.

### Formula 4: Social Influence Lift [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Social Influence Lift = purchase rate with social proof - baseline purchase rate`
Variables:
purchase rate with social proof = conversion after friend, family, or influencer cues
baseline purchase rate = conversion without those cues
Why this formula exists: It answers how much other people change the buyer's decision.
How to interpret the output:
Value near 0 -> the market is mostly independent
Value positive and large -> referral, review, and community effects matter
Value negative -> social proof may be hurting the brand
Worked example with numbers: If baseline conversion is 10% and conversion after testimonials is 16%, social influence lift is 6 percentage points. Decision: invest in referrals and UGC before increasing paid media.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat consumer behavior as purely rational price comparison. | Design for emotion, habit, and social influence as well as logic. |
| Assume one campaign works for all buyers. | Segment by need, want, life stage, and context. |
| Confuse first purchase with loyalty. | Measure repeat purchase and post-purchase use. |
| Ignore the role of family, peers, and culture. | Test messaging against social and cultural factors. |
| Sell only features when the buyer is buying status or identity. | Match the message to the real motive behind the purchase. |
| Optimize for acquisition while neglecting disposal and replacement. | Think through the full buying and usage cycle. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Energy Drink Launch for Urban Youth
Situation: A beverage brand is launching an energy drink in city markets and wants to know whether flavor, packaging, or peer influence matters more. The team has samples out in colleges and convenience stores.
Applicable framework/metric: Buying Process Map and Purchase Conversion Rate.
Analysis: 140 of 1,000 sampled consumers buy, so conversion is 14%. After adding peer testimonials and a smaller can size, conversion rises to 17%. That suggests the buying process is sensitive to both social proof and trial friction.
Decision rule: If conversion is below 5%, the offer-message fit is poor. If it is 5%-15%, refine packaging or placement. If it is above 15%, scale distribution and test repeat purchase.
Action: Keep the smaller pack, add social proof in-store, and retest conversion by neighborhood segment.

### Scenario 2: Toothpaste Brand and Dentist Recommendation
Situation: A toothpaste brand is trying to convert first-time trial into repeat buying. The early sales were helped by dentists, but the business wants to know whether it is building habit or only temporary trial.
Applicable framework/metric: Need-Want-Loyalty Ladder and Repeat Purchase Rate.
Analysis: Out of 800 first-time buyers, 500 repurchase within the quarter, so repeat purchase rate is 62.5%. That is strong enough to show habit formation, but not yet loyalty across every segment.
Decision rule: If repeat purchase is below 40%, the brand is still a trial brand. If it is 40%-70%, improve experience and reminders. If it is above 70%, lock in loyalty programs and subscription replenishment.
Action: Strengthen dentist endorsements, simplify replenishment reminders, and segment retention messaging by family size and usage frequency.

### Scenario 3: Car Choice and Neighborhood Status
Situation: A family chooses between a fuel-efficient car and a more status-heavy brand. Mileage matters, but neighborhood reputation and peer comparison also affect the final choice.
Applicable framework/metric: Influence Stack and Brand Preference Share.
Analysis: In a test panel, the status brand is chosen 34 times out of 50 evaluations, so brand preference share is 68%. The buyer is responding to personal budget pressure and social status at the same time.
Decision rule: If preference share is low, reposition the brand. If it is medium, sharpen one clear identity cue. If it is high, protect the positioning and avoid confusing the message.
Action: Keep the efficiency story for utility buyers, but add social proof and ownership pride for status-sensitive buyers.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Map the buying process from need recognition through disposal for the target segment.
2. Separate personal, social, and cultural drivers in the customer research brief.
3. Measure conversion, repeat purchase, and preference share for each major segment.
4. Test two or three messages that reflect different motives, not just different features.
5. Use social proof or referral mechanics where peer influence is strong.
6. Design the product, packaging, and price for the actual usage pattern, not only the first purchase.
7. Track repeat purchase and post-purchase feedback before scaling the campaign.
