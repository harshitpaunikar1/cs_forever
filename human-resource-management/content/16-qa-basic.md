# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational questions covering core HRM concepts.

---

## Q1. What is Human Resource Management (HRM)?

**Level:** Basic

Human Resource Management (HRM) is the function in an organization that focuses on managing people at work. It includes hiring employees, training them, setting work rules, paying them, supporting their growth, and maintaining a safe and positive workplace. HRM aims to help employees perform well and help the organization achieve its goals through effective people practices.

**Real-life applications:**

- HR teams recruit and onboard new employees.
- They set policies like leave rules and working hours.
- They help resolve workplace conflicts and complaints.
- They support performance reviews and employee development.

**Key concepts:** `HRM` `workforce` `policies` `employee management` `organizational goals`

---

## Q2. Why is HRM important for an organization?

**Level:** Basic

HRM is important because employees are essential to achieving business results. Good HRM ensures the right people are hired, trained, and supported to do their jobs well. It also helps reduce turnover, improve morale, and maintain fairness and compliance in the workplace. When HRM is strong, teams work better and the organization performs more consistently.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies use HRM to keep employee turnover low.
- HRM helps prevent workplace disputes through clear rules.
- HR supports managers in building productive teams.
- It improves service quality by training employees properly.

**Key concepts:** `productivity` `retention` `morale` `compliance` `performance`

---

## Q3. What are the main functions of HRM?

**Level:** Basic

The main functions of HRM include planning workforce needs, recruiting candidates, selecting employees, training and developing skills, managing performance, setting compensation and benefits, maintaining employee relations, ensuring workplace safety, and handling employee separation. These functions work together to manage the employee life cycle from hiring to exit.

**Real-life applications:**

- HR plans staffing needs before a new project starts.
- It recruits and interviews candidates for open positions.
- HR organizes training to improve skills and reduce errors.
- HR manages payroll, benefits, and employee relations issues.

**Key concepts:** `recruitment` `selection` `training` `performance management` `compensation` `employee relations`

## Q4. What is the difference between HRM and personnel management?

**Level:** Basic

Personnel management mainly focuses on administrative tasks like attendance, payroll, and enforcing rules. HRM is broader and more strategic; it includes developing employees, improving engagement, and aligning people practices with organizational goals. In simple terms, personnel management is more about maintaining employees, while HRM is about developing and using talent effectively.

**Real-life applications:**

- Personnel handles salary processing and leave records.
- HRM designs training programs for better performance.
- HRM supports leadership development and engagement initiatives.
- HRM uses workforce data to plan future staffing needs.

**Key concepts:** `personnel management` `HRM` `administration` `strategy` `employee development`

## Q5. What is a “workforce” in HRM terms?

**Level:** Basic

A workforce refers to all the people who work for an organization, including full-time employees, part-time employees, contract workers, and sometimes interns. HRM manages the workforce by ensuring the organization has enough people with the right skills to meet its goals.

**Real-life applications:**

- A hospital workforce includes doctors, nurses, and support staff.
- A factory workforce includes operators, supervisors, and technicians.
- HR plans schedules and staffing levels to avoid shortages.
- Workforce data helps plan hiring and training needs.

**Key concepts:** `workforce` `employees` `staffing` `skills` `labor supply`

## Q6. What is HR planning?

**Level:** Basic

HR planning is the process of deciding how many employees an organization needs and what skills they should have, now and in the future. It involves estimating demand for workers, checking available supply, and planning hiring, training, or redeployment to fill gaps.

**Real-life applications:**

- Retail firms plan extra hiring during festival seasons.
- IT firms plan staffing for new client projects in advance.
- Schools plan teacher hiring based on student enrollment.
- Hospitals plan nursing staff to handle patient demand.

**Key concepts:** `HR planning` `demand forecasting` `supply forecasting` `workforce needs` `gap analysis`

## Q7. What is a job analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Job analysis is the process of studying a job to understand what tasks it involves and what skills, knowledge, and responsibilities are required. It helps HR create clear job descriptions and job specifications, which support fair hiring, training, and performance evaluation.

**Real-life applications:**

- HR studies a cashier job before recruiting cashiers.
- It identifies required skills like accuracy and customer service.
- Managers use job details to assign work properly.
- Training is designed based on actual job tasks.

**Key concepts:** `job analysis` `job description` `job specification` `tasks` `responsibilities`

## Q8. What is a job description?

**Level:** Basic

A job description is a written document that explains what a job involves. It typically includes the job title, key duties, responsibilities, reporting relationships, and sometimes working conditions. It helps candidates understand the role and helps organizations set clear expectations.

**Real-life applications:**

- Candidates read job descriptions before applying.
- Managers use it to clarify what tasks belong to the role.
- HR uses it to design interviews around job duties.
- It supports fair evaluation by defining expected work.

**Key concepts:** `job description` `duties` `responsibilities` `role clarity` `reporting line`

## Q9. What is a job specification?

**Level:** Basic

A job specification lists the qualifications needed to perform a job successfully. It typically includes education, experience, skills, and personal abilities required. While a job description describes the work, a job specification describes the person who should do the work.

**Real-life applications:**

- HR uses job specifications to screen resumes.
- It helps set minimum requirements like a degree or certification.
- It guides interview questions about skills and experience.
- It reduces hiring mistakes by focusing on job fit.

**Key concepts:** `job specification` `qualifications` `skills` `experience` `job fit`

## Q10. What is recruitment?

**Level:** Basic

Recruitment is the process of attracting and encouraging suitable candidates to apply for job vacancies. It involves creating job postings, selecting recruitment channels, and building a pool of potential applicants. Recruitment focuses on bringing in options; selection decides who gets hired.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies post jobs on portals to reach applicants.
- Employee referrals bring candidates faster and often cheaper.
- Campus recruitment helps hire fresh graduates.
- Recruitment quality affects the quality of final hires.

**Key concepts:** `recruitment` `candidate pool` `job posting` `sourcing` `employer branding`

## Q11. What is selection in HRM?

**Level:** Basic

Selection is the process of choosing the best candidate from the applicant pool. It involves steps like interviews, tests, reference checks, and final decision-making. The goal is to match the right person to the right job for better performance and lower turnover.

**Real-life applications:**

- Organizations use interviews to assess communication skills.
- Skill tests confirm if a candidate can do the job tasks.
- Reference checks reduce the risk of false information.
- Better selection lowers early resignations and poor performance.

**Key concepts:** `selection` `interviews` `assessments` `reference checks` `job fit`

## Q12. What is onboarding?

**Level:** Basic

Onboarding is the process of helping a new employee settle into the organization and become productive. It includes orientation, training on job duties, introductions to the team, and explaining company policies and culture. Good onboarding improves confidence and reduces early turnover.

**Real-life applications:**

- New hires receive welcome sessions and work tools.
- They learn policies like leave rules and reporting procedures.
- Managers assign a buddy to guide them in the first weeks.
- Organizations track onboarding to improve new hire success.

**Real-life applications:**

- HR tracks turnover rates to spot problems early.
- High turnover in one team may signal poor management.
- Exit interviews help identify why employees leave.
- Reducing turnover saves hiring and training expenses.

## Q37. What is time-to-hire?

**Level:** Basic

Time-to-hire is the number of days it takes to fill a job position from the moment it is approved or posted until the candidate accepts the offer. It helps HR measure hiring efficiency and identify delays in sourcing, interviewing, or decision-making.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies track time-to-hire to reduce vacancy losses.
- Long hiring time may cause good candidates to accept other offers.
- HR improves scheduling and approvals to shorten hiring cycles.

## Q38. What is cost-per-hire?

**Level:** Basic

Cost-per-hire is the total cost of hiring a new employee divided by the number of hires made. It may include advertising, agency fees, recruiter time, assessments, and onboarding costs. HR uses it to manage budgets and compare hiring methods.

**Real-life applications:**

- HR compares referral hiring cost versus agency hiring cost.
- Budget planning becomes easier with cost-per-hire data.
- High costs may lead to improving internal recruiting methods.
- Cost tracking supports efficient use of recruitment resources.

**Key concepts:** `cost-per-hire` `recruitment cost` `hiring budget` `sourcing channels` `efficiency`

## 4. Formulas

The source is a Q&A set, so the formulas below are the practical metrics that turn the answers into operating checks. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: Workforce Gap
Formula: `workforce gap = demand forecast - supply forecast`
Variables:
`demand forecast` = number of people and skills needed
`supply forecast` = number of people and skills available
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the organization is understaffed or overstaffed.
How to interpret the output:
Positive gap -> shortage -> hire, train, or redeploy
Zero gap -> balanced workforce -> maintain current plan
Negative gap -> surplus -> slow hiring or reassign capacity
Worked example with numbers: If a project needs 25 analysts and only 20 are available, the workforce gap is 5. Decision: recruit, cross-train, or delay scope until coverage exists. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 2: Selection Ratio
Formula: `selection ratio = hires / applicants`
Variables:
`hires` = candidates hired
`applicants` = candidates who applied
Why this formula exists: It answers how selective the hiring process is.
How to interpret the output:
Low ratio -> selective funnel -> quality focus matters
Medium ratio -> balanced funnel -> keep both speed and quality in view
High ratio -> loose funnel -> risk of weak-fit hires
Worked example with numbers: If 3 hires come from 150 applicants, the selection ratio is 2%. Decision: the hiring bar is high, so the screening tools must be job-related and reliable. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 3: Turnover Rate
Formula: `turnover rate = exits / average headcount`
Variables:
`exits` = employees who left in the period
`average headcount` = average number of employees during that period
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the organization is keeping the people it hires.
How to interpret the output:
Low turnover -> stable team -> keep the current people system
Moderate turnover -> review managers and workload
High turnover -> fix onboarding, compensation, or culture
Worked example with numbers: If 6 employees leave and average headcount is 60, turnover rate is 10%. Decision: that should trigger a review of retention, manager quality, and role clarity. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
