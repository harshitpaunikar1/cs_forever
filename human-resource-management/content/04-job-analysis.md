# Job Analysis


## Overview

Job analysis means understanding a job clearly—what tasks are done, what skills are needed, and what responsibilities the role has.


## Why It Matters

When jobs are clearly defined, hiring becomes easier, training becomes targeted, and performance expectations become fair.


## Key Principles

- Describe tasks and responsibilities accurately
- Identify required skills, education, and experience
- Keep job details updated as work changes
- Use job analysis to support HR decisions

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Job Description (JD)** | Tasks and responsibilities of a job |
| **Job Specification** | Skills and qualifications needed |
| **KSA** | Knowledge, Skills, and Abilities |
| **Competency** | Important capability for success in a role |


## Use Case

Creating a job description for “Sales Executive” before starting recruitment.


## Scenario

> A company hires quickly without clear roles. New hires get confused and performance suffers. HR conducts job analysis and clarifies duties, reporting lines, and needed skills.


## Examples

- Writing a JD for an accountant with tasks like invoicing and reconciliation.
- Defining a customer support role needing patience, communication, and product knowledge.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

The frameworks below are [verified from model knowledge, not source]; the source gives the idea, and these turn it into an operating tool.

### Job Analysis Funnel
**Purpose:** Translate observed work into hiring and performance artifacts.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
tasks -> responsibilities -> KSA -> JD/specification -> hiring/training/performance
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Tasks: what the person actually does.
Responsibilities: what they own.
KSA: what they must know, do, and be able to do.
JD/specification: the formal document used for hiring and management.
Hiring/training/performance: the downstream HR decisions.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product operations role initially looks like "miscellaneous coordination." The funnel forces the team to list recurring tasks, turn them into responsibilities, and then define the KSA needed for automation reporting, vendor coordination, and stakeholder updates [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When a role is fuzzy and everyone is describing the job differently.

### Role Clarity Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether a job is ready to hire, needs redesign, or needs more documentation.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    TASK CLARITY
              clear                     unclear
ACCOUNTABILITY
clear      hire now / monitor      document and standardize
unclear    redesign the role       pause hiring and define first
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Task clarity: whether the day-to-day work is known.
Accountability: whether ownership and reporting are clear.
Clear/clear: strong hiring signal.
Unclear/unclear: the role is not ready for recruitment.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI startup wants to hire a "growth generalist" but cannot say whether the person owns CRM, paid media, or analytics. The matrix says the role should be redesigned before hiring, or the company will pay for confusion with poor performance and attrition [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When leadership wants to hire quickly but the role has not been defined.

### Role Update Loop
**Purpose:** Keep the JD and job specification current as work changes.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
work changes -> review role -> update JD/spec -> retrain or rehire
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Work changes: new tools, new customers, new process, or new regulation.
Review role: compare current work to the existing document.
Update JD/spec: revise tasks, skills, and qualifications.
Retrain or rehire: decide whether to build capability internally or hire it.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting analyst role starts requiring prompt evaluation, dashboard QA, and stakeholder storytelling. The loop shows that the JD should change before the team blames the person for not doing work that was never written down [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the job has drifted since the last org redesign.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: JD Coverage
Formula: `JD coverage = documented tasks / observed tasks`

Variables:
documented tasks = tasks written in the job description
observed tasks = tasks actually performed on the job

Why this formula exists: It checks whether the written role matches real work.

How to interpret the output:
Below 0.7 -> role documentation is stale or incomplete
0.7-0.9 -> usable but needs cleanup
Above 0.9 -> role definition is strong

Worked example with numbers: If a role actually has 20 recurring tasks but only 14 are written in the JD, coverage is 0.70. That means the role is only partially captured and may be causing hiring or performance confusion [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 2: KSA Gap
Formula: `KSA gap = required level - current level`

Variables:
required level = proficiency needed for the role
current level = proficiency the candidate or employee has now

Why this formula exists: It tells HR and managers whether to train, coach, or recruit.

How to interpret the output:
Gap near 0 -> ready for role
Moderate gap -> train or onboard
Large gap -> hire differently or redesign the role

Worked example with numbers: If the role needs a 5/5 level in stakeholder communication and the candidate has 3/5, the gap is 2. That means onboarding must close the gap or the hire will struggle in the first quarter [verified from model knowledge, not source].

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Don't write a JD from memory or assumptions. | Do observe the work and document actual tasks and responsibilities. |
| Don't conflate skills with experience. | Do separate KSA, qualifications, and years of experience. |
| Don't hire before the role is clear. | Do define reporting lines and success criteria first. |
| Don't leave a JD untouched after process or technology changes. | Do update the role when work changes. |
| Don't use vague competency language that no manager can assess. | Do tie competencies to observable behavior and outcomes. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Sales Executive JD before recruitment
**Situation:** A company wants to hire a Sales Executive but the team cannot agree on whether the role is lead generation, closing, account management, or all three. HR wants speed, but the sales head wants "someone good."

**Applicable framework/metric:** Job Analysis Funnel + JD Coverage.

**Analysis:** If the team cannot list the actual tasks, the JD will be incomplete and hiring will be noisy. Once the funnel is complete, the role can be screened for the right KSA, such as prospecting, negotiation, CRM discipline, and product knowledge [verified from model knowledge, not source].

**Decision rule:** If documented task coverage is below 80%, pause hiring and clarify the role. If coverage is between 80% and 95%, hire but refine the role. If coverage is above 95%, recruit immediately.

**Action:** Interview the sales manager, write the JD, map the KSA, and screen candidates against the specification.

### Scenario 2: Startup role confusion after rapid hiring
**Situation:** A fast-growing startup hired several people quickly and now new employees are duplicating each other’s work while missing critical tasks. The founders want to know whether the problem is performance or role design.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Role Clarity Matrix.

**Analysis:** If both task clarity and accountability are low, the issue is job design, not just individual performance. The company should define responsibilities, reporting lines, and success criteria before it starts counseling employees [verified from model knowledge, not source].

**Decision rule:** If accountability is clear but tasks are fuzzy, document the job. If tasks are clear but accountability is fuzzy, fix reporting. If both are unclear, redesign the role before judging people.

**Action:** Run a role-mapping workshop, update org charts, and rewrite the JD and job specification.

### Scenario 3: AI operations role evolves after automation
**Situation:** An AI-enabled service team automates part of its workflow, and the old job description no longer matches reality. Employees are now expected to do quality checks, client escalation handling, and model feedback review.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Role Update Loop + KSA Gap.

**Analysis:** When work changes, the JD and specification must change too. If the new KSA requirements are higher in analytics and judgment, HR can decide whether to retrain current staff or recruit for the new profile [verified from model knowledge, not source].

**Decision rule:** If the KSA gap is small, train. If the gap is moderate, coach and stage the transition. If the gap is large, redesign the role or hire a different profile.

**Action:** Update the role documentation, reset performance expectations, and launch targeted training.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Observe the job directly before writing anything.
2. Break the role into tasks, responsibilities, and KSA.
3. Draft the JD and job specification from the observed work, not from generic templates.
4. Verify reporting lines and accountability with the manager.
5. Compare current work to the document and calculate JD coverage.
6. Use KSA gaps to decide whether to train, coach, or recruit.
7. Revisit the job analysis whenever the process, technology, or business model changes.
