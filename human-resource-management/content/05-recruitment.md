# Recruitment


## Overview

Recruitment means attracting a pool of people who might fit the job, so the company can choose the best ones later.


## Why It Matters

If recruitment is weak, you may not get enough good candidates. Strong recruitment improves quality of hires and reduces time and cost.


## Key Principles

- Use the right channels (job portals, referrals, campus, etc.)
- Create clear, honest job postings
- Build employer brand (why people should join)
- Track results (time-to-hire, cost-per-hire)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Candidate Pool** | Group of people who applied/are interested |
| **Employer Branding** | Company image as a workplace |
| **Cost-per-Hire** | Total hiring cost per employee hired |
| **Yield Ratio** | Conversion rate between stages (applied → interviewed → hired) |


## Use Case

Hiring 30 customer support agents quickly for a new product launch.


## Scenario

> A company struggles to hire engineers. HR improves job ads, adds employee referral bonuses, and participates in tech events to attract stronger applicants.


## Examples

- Using LinkedIn + referrals to hire a marketing manager.
- Campus hiring for entry-level trainees.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Recruitment Funnel Map
**Purpose:** Turn sourcing into a measurable flow from awareness to hire.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
attract -> apply -> screen -> interview -> offer -> accept -> join
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: top of funnel - reach and awareness.
Component 2: mid funnel - screening and interviews.
Component 3: bottom of funnel - offers and acceptance.
Component 4: outcome - hiring quality and speed.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team needs 30 support agents before a launch. If 600 people see the role, 60 apply, 20 are screened, 10 are interviewed, 5 receive offers, and 4 accept, the funnel shows exactly where the bottleneck sits. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When leadership says “we need more candidates” without knowing where the funnel is breaking.

### Channel Portfolio Scorecard
**Purpose:** Compare recruiting channels by quality, speed, and cost.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
low cost / low quality   low cost / high quality
high cost / low quality  high cost / high quality
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: cost - spend needed per applicant or hire.
Component 2: speed - how quickly the channel fills the funnel.
Component 3: quality - how strong the hire signal is.
Component 4: scalability - whether the channel can fill more roles later.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: Job portals may deliver volume fast, referrals may deliver stronger fit, campus hiring may build a junior pipeline, and tech events may work well for scarce engineers. The scorecard shows which mix is best for the role, not just which channel is cheapest. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is overspending on one channel or starving the funnel with only one source.

### Employer Brand Flywheel
**Purpose:** Show how reputation compounds recruiting performance over time.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
good candidate experience -> stronger brand -> better applicants -> stronger hires -> even better candidate experience
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: brand promise - what candidates expect before applying.
Component 2: candidate experience - what they feel during the process.
Component 3: hire quality - the strength of the final slate and offers.
Component 4: reinforcement - whether new hires and rejected candidates speak well of the process.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm that communicates clear growth paths, fast feedback, and honest role expectations gets more senior applicants and fewer dropouts. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When hiring is slow because the market does not trust the brand or the process.

## 4. Formulas

The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are the standard recruiting metrics that make the chapter actionable. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: Cost-per-Hire
Formula: `cost-per-hire = total recruiting cost / number of hires`
Variables:
`total recruiting cost` = ads, recruiter time, events, tools, agency fees
`number of hires` = people who were actually hired
Why this formula exists: It answers how expensive each hire is.
How to interpret the output:
Low value -> efficient channel mix
Moderate value -> acceptable if quality is strong
High value -> channel mix, process, or brand problem
Worked example with numbers: If recruiting costs $30,000 and 6 people are hired, cost-per-hire is $5,000. Decision: keep the channel if the new hires are strong; otherwise redesign the funnel.

### Formula 2: Yield Ratio
Formula: `yield ratio = candidates at next stage / candidates at previous stage`
Variables:
`candidates at next stage` = people who move forward
`candidates at previous stage` = people who entered the earlier stage
Why this formula exists: It answers where the recruiting funnel leaks.
How to interpret the output:
Low yield -> weak sourcing or screening problem
Moderate yield -> acceptable but improvable
High yield -> strong channel or over-filtered stage
Worked example with numbers: If 60 applicants become 12 screened candidates, the yield ratio is 20 percent. Decision: if the next stage falls sharply, improve targeting or tighten the job ad.

### Formula 3: Time-to-Hire
Formula: `time-to-hire = offer accepted date - requisition opened date`
Variables:
`offer accepted date` = date the candidate accepts
`requisition opened date` = date the job was opened
Why this formula exists: It answers how fast the hiring process is moving.
How to interpret the output:
Short time -> speed advantage
Moderate time -> normal hiring cycle
Long time -> launch risk or process friction
Worked example with numbers: If a role opens on May 1 and is accepted on May 26, time-to-hire is 25 days. Decision: if the role is launch-critical, shorten the process or pre-build the pipeline.

### Formula 4: Offer Acceptance Rate
Formula: `offer acceptance rate = accepted offers / offers made`
Variables:
`accepted offers` = offers that were accepted
`offers made` = total offers extended
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the employer value proposition is actually working.
How to interpret the output:
Low rate -> pay, role design, or brand issue
Medium rate -> room to improve messaging or package fit
High rate -> strong candidate attraction and alignment
Worked example with numbers: If 5 offers are made and 4 are accepted, the acceptance rate is 80 percent. Decision: keep the package and focus on scaling sourcing.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat recruitment as a last-minute admin task. | Run it as a pipeline with explicit funnel metrics. |
| Spend on every channel equally. | Match channel mix to role scarcity, speed, and quality. |
| Optimize for applications only. | Optimize for qualified candidates and eventual hires. |
| Ignore employer brand until offers are rejected. | Build employer brand before the market starts ignoring you. |
| Measure hiring speed without measuring hire quality. | Track time-to-hire and quality-of-hire together. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Product launch staffing
Situation: A company is launching a new product and needs 30 customer support agents quickly. The business cannot afford a slow funnel because launch tickets will spike. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Recruitment Funnel Map and Time-to-Hire.
Analysis: If the team needs 30 hires in 21 days and the current funnel converts only 4 hires from 600 applicants, the process is too leaky or too slow. If the time-to-hire is 35 days, the launch is exposed. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If time-to-hire is above the launch window, pre-hire or use temporary staffing; if the funnel is healthy, scale the same channels; if offer acceptance is low, fix pay or shift patterns.
Action: Open referrals, add a high-volume portal campaign, and run a daily hiring dashboard until the launch is covered.

### Scenario 2: Engineering hiring in a scarce market
Situation: An AI product company struggles to hire engineers. HR adds referral bonuses, runs tech events, and rewrites job ads to attract stronger applicants. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Channel Portfolio Scorecard and Yield Ratio.
Analysis: If referrals convert at 30 percent from screen to interview while job portals convert at 10 percent, referrals deserve more budget. If the brand is weak, more applicants may still be low quality. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If a channel has both strong yield and acceptable cost, scale it; if cost is low but quality is weak, keep it as filler only; if a channel performs poorly on both, stop spending there.
Action: Rebalance recruiting spend toward referrals and events, then measure quality-of-hire after 90 days.

### Scenario 3: Campus pipeline for growth roles
Situation: A consulting firm wants a steady pipeline of entry-level analysts. Campus hiring looks cheaper than using search firms, but leadership wants proof that it also builds the right long-term pool. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Employer Brand Flywheel and Cost-per-Hire.
Analysis: If campus hiring costs $2,000 per hire and search costs $8,000, campus is attractive only if retention and performance are not worse. A stronger employer brand should raise acceptance rates and reduce later vacancy costs. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If cost-per-hire is low and quality-of-hire stays high, keep campus hiring; if acceptance is low, improve the EVP; if campus produces weak performers, use it only for selected roles.
Action: Build a campus calendar, standardize messaging, and compare the first-year performance of campus hires with other sources.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Define the role requirements and the target candidate profile before sourcing.
2. Map the funnel stages and set target conversion rates for each stage.
3. Compare job portals, referrals, campus hiring, and events by cost, speed, and quality.
4. Track time-to-hire, cost-per-hire, and offer acceptance rate weekly.
5. Tune the employer value proposition so the process matches what the market wants.
6. Measure quality-of-hire after onboarding so recruiting does not optimize for vanity metrics.
7. Reallocate budget toward the channels that produce the strongest hires at acceptable speed.
