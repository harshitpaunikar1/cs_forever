# Overview of HRM


## Overview

Human Resource Management (HRM) means managing people at work. It includes hiring the right people, helping them do good work, paying them fairly, developing their skills, and keeping the workplace healthy.


## Why It Matters

People are the ones who do the work, solve problems, and serve customers. Good HRM helps a company perform better by building a capable, motivated, and stable workforce.


## Key Principles

- Treat employees fairly and respectfully
- Hire based on job needs, not personal bias
- Help employees learn and improve
- Create clear rules and healthy work culture
- Align people practices with company goals

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **HRM** | Managing employees and workplace practices |
| **Policy** | A rule or guideline for employees |
| **Culture** | “How things are done” in a workplace |
| **Workforce** | All employees in an organization |


## Use Case

A growing company needs to create basic HR policies (attendance, leave, conduct) and set up hiring and training.


## Scenario

> A small startup grows from 10 to 50 people. Confusion starts about working hours, leave, and roles. HR creates clear policies, job roles, and a basic onboarding plan.


## Examples

- HR creates an onboarding checklist so every new employee gets the same support.
- HR sets a leave policy to avoid confusion and conflict.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
HRM is the operating system for how a company hires, manages, develops, and retains people.
For IT/AI/Product/Consulting leaders, it is the difference between a team that scales cleanly and one that burns out, confuses roles, or loses talent. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
The decision it helps make is how to structure policies, culture, and people practices so execution stays consistent as the company grows.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### People Lifecycle Map
**Purpose:** Show how HRM links hiring, support, development, and retention into one system. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Hire -> Onboard -> Enable -> Review -> Develop -> Retain/Exit
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: attraction and hiring, which bring people into the company.
Component 2: onboarding and enablement, which get people productive quickly.
Component 3: review and development, which improve performance and skills over time.
Component 4: retention or exit, which keeps the workforce healthy and the team composition right.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product org can hire great PMs but still fail if onboarding is weak and feedback loops are absent. The lifecycle map forces leaders to fix the whole system, not just recruiting. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team thinks recruiting is the only HR problem.

## 4. Formulas

The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are standard HR management metrics used to make the ideas operational. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: Turnover Rate
Formula: `turnover rate = exits / average headcount`
Variables:
`exits` = number of employees who left in a period
`average headcount` = average number of employees during that period
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the workforce is stable or leaking talent.
How to interpret the output:
Low rate -> stable team -> keep current policies and watch engagement
Moderate rate -> manageable churn -> review managers, pay, and workload
High rate -> retention problem -> fix role clarity, culture, or compensation
Worked example with numbers: If 8 employees leave in a quarter and average headcount is 80, turnover rate is 10%. Decision: that level may be acceptable in some fast-growing teams, but it should trigger a manager review. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 2: Time-to-Fill
Formula: `time to fill = date offer accepted - date requisition opened`
Variables:
`date offer accepted` = when the candidate accepts
`date requisition opened` = when the role was approved and posted
Why this formula exists: It answers how fast the organization can convert hiring demand into staffed capacity.
How to interpret the output:
Short time -> recruiting is efficient -> preserve the process
Medium time -> acceptable but improvable -> tighten sourcing
Long time -> capacity risk -> redesign the hiring funnel
Worked example with numbers: If a role opens on March 1 and the offer is accepted on April 5, time to fill is 35 days. Decision: if the role is critical, that delay may justify a stronger recruiter or better candidate pipeline. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 3: Training ROI
Formula: `training ROI = (benefit - cost) / cost`
Variables:
`benefit` = estimated value created by improved performance
`cost` = cost of training design and delivery
Why this formula exists: It answers whether development spending is creating enough business value.
How to interpret the output:
Negative ROI -> training is not paying back -> redesign or stop
Positive but small ROI -> keep only if compliance or strategic value matters
High ROI -> scale the program -> apply to more teams
Worked example with numbers: If a leadership workshop costs $20,000 and reduces rework worth $35,000, ROI is 75%. Decision: keep the program and roll it out to similar teams if the gain is repeatable. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat HR as only an admin function. | Link HRM to execution, retention, and capability. |
| Hire fast without role clarity. | Define the job and expectations before recruiting. |
| Use one policy for every situation. | Build clear rules, then allow controlled exceptions. |
| Ignore culture because it is hard to measure. | Track engagement, attrition, and behavior patterns. |
| Spend on training without checking impact. | Measure training ROI against a real business outcome. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Startup scaling from 10 to 50 people
Situation: A startup is growing quickly and people are confused about working hours, leave, and who owns what. Managers are answering the same questions repeatedly, and new hires are taking too long to become productive. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Policy Stack and People Lifecycle Map.
Analysis: If time-to-fill is 35 days and first-90-day retention is falling, the company is not just hiring slowly; it is also failing to onboard well. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If confusion is rising, create policies and onboarding. If role clarity is weak, rewrite job descriptions. If the culture is drifting, align managers first.
Action: Publish a short employee handbook, assign onboarding owners, and standardize manager check-ins.

### Scenario 2: Consulting team attrition
Situation: A consulting practice is losing strong performers after busy delivery cycles. Leaders think the problem is pay, but exit interviews mention burnout, role ambiguity, and weak manager feedback. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Culture-Execution Matrix and turnover rate.
Analysis: If turnover is 10% per quarter, that is expensive in client-facing work because every exit reduces delivery capacity and increases rework. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If turnover is high and engagement is low, fix workload and management norms. If turnover is moderate but concentrated in one team, fix the local manager. If turnover is low, keep current practices and monitor.
Action: Review span of control, reset project load, and run manager coaching for the affected teams.

### Scenario 3: Skills gap in an AI delivery team
Situation: A product analytics team keeps missing deadlines because only a few people know how to run a critical toolchain. The leader is deciding whether to hire more specialists or train existing staff. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Training ROI and People Lifecycle Map.
Analysis: If training costs $20,000 and prevents $35,000 of rework, ROI is 75%. That is a better move than waiting for a slow hire if the capability gap is urgent. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If the needed skill is urgent and teachable, train. If the skill is scarce and strategic, hire. If both are true, do both.
Action: Build a skills matrix, assign training, and create backup coverage for critical tools.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Write down the roles the business actually needs before opening hiring.
2. Create simple policies for attendance, leave, and conduct so managers do not improvise.
3. Define onboarding milestones for the first 30, 60, and 90 days.
4. Track turnover, time-to-fill, and training ROI in one people dashboard.
5. Review culture signals through engagement, retention, and manager behavior.
6. Align HR practices with company goals instead of treating them as separate admin tasks.
