# Market Entry Strategies

## Overview

Market entry strategy is the plan for how a startup breaks into a new market — geography, customer segment, or product category. Options range from direct launch to partnerships, acquisitions, franchising, or licensing. The choice depends on speed, cost, local knowledge, and how defended the market already is.

---

## Why It Matters

Entering the wrong way burns cash and signals weakness to incumbents. The right entry creates a beachhead that is hard to dislodge and gives the team time to build scale. A manager picks the entry mode that trades risk, speed, and control in a way the business can afford.

## Key Principles

- Pick the narrowest beachhead where you can win quickly.
- Match the entry mode to the cost of being wrong — pilot first, scale later.
- Study local rules, taxes, and customer habits before building product.
- Partner when you lack distribution or trust; go direct when you lack margin to share.
- Plan the second market while still proving the first — entry is a skill that compounds.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Beachhead** | A small, winnable segment used to establish presence before wider expansion. |
| **Greenfield entry** | Building from scratch in a new market with your own operations. |
| **Joint venture** | A shared-ownership entity with a local partner for faster market access. |
| **Franchising** | Licensing your brand and playbook to local operators for a fee. |

## Use Case

An Indian food brand enters the UAE by partnering with a local distributor for two years, then opens its own office once demand is proven.

## Scenario

> An e-commerce startup that was strong in Delhi wanted to enter Chennai. Instead of opening a full hub, they ran a 90-day pilot using three rented vans and a shared warehouse with a local partner. Revenue crossed Rs 30 lakh a month in week eight. They then signed a two-year lease for their own warehouse, onboarded 60 local sellers, and launched full operations in month four.

## Examples

- A SaaS firm enters the US market by acquiring a tiny competitor with an existing customer list rather than starting cold.
- A quick-service restaurant chain enters a new state through master-franchise agreements to limit capital outlay.

## Jargon Buster

| Term | Plain-English Definition | Why It Matters in Practice |
|------|--------------------------|---------------------------|
| Beachhead | The single, tightly scoped customer segment or geography you target first — chosen because you can win it decisively before expanding | Winning a beachhead builds the proof point, the reference customers, and the unit economics needed to fund expansion; skipping it disperses resources and produces nothing convincing |
| Greenfield Entry | Building presence in a market from scratch — no prior assets, no inherited customers, no legacy code or brand | Offers full control over positioning and culture but demands the most time and capital; appropriate when no acquirable asset exists or when existing players carry toxic reputations |
| Brownfield Entry | Entering a market by acquiring, licensing, or partnering with an already operating entity — inheriting its infrastructure, customers, and contracts | Compresses time-to-revenue dramatically; risk is inheriting technical debt, poor culture, or mis-aligned customer expectations that must be unwound post-close |
| Joint Venture | A separate legal entity co-owned by two or more parties that pools capital, IP, or distribution to pursue a shared opportunity neither could exploit alone | Common in regulated markets (fintech, healthtech, defense-adjacent AI) where a foreign entrant needs a local license-holder; governance disputes are the primary failure mode |
| Franchising | Licensing a proven operating model — brand, processes, technology stack, training — to an independent operator in exchange for fees and royalties | Scales distribution with minimal capital outlay; used by SaaS firms that license reseller playbooks or consulting firms that license methodology to regional partners |
| Licensing | Granting a third party the right to use IP (software, patents, datasets, models) in exchange for royalties or flat fees, without transferring ownership | Generates revenue without operational presence; the preferred entry mode when the market is too small to justify an office but large enough to justify a reseller agreement |
| Acquisition Entry | Purchasing an existing company in the target market to gain its customers, team, technology, or regulatory approvals instantly | Fastest path to market share when integration costs are manageable; in AI/SaaS, acquiring a small competitor for its labelled dataset or its compliance certifications is increasingly common |
| Go-to-Market Motion | The repeatable sequence of activities — demand generation, sales process, onboarding, success — through which a company converts a target segment from unaware to paying and retained | Defines whether you enter through product-led growth, sales-led outreach, channel partnerships, or community; the wrong motion in a new market wastes CAC before you detect the mismatch |
| Bowling Pin Expansion | Geoffrey Moore's model where the beachhead segment is pin 1; winning it topples adjacent segments by momentum and reference credibility | Forces sequencing discipline: fully dominate one vertical before entering the next rather than spreading thin across many simultaneously |
| Market Attractiveness | A composite score of market size, growth rate, competitive intensity, regulatory complexity, and strategic fit used to rank entry candidates | Prevents entering large but structurally unattractive markets (high NPS churn, commoditised pricing, entrenched incumbents with switching-cost moats) |

### 4. Market Attractiveness Assessment (Weighted Scoring)

Score each candidate market on five dimensions (1-5 scale), apply weights, and rank.

```
  DIMENSION              WEIGHT    SCORING GUIDE
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Market Size (TAM)        25%     1=<$50M, 3=$100-500M, 5=>$1B
  Growth Rate              20%     1=<5%/yr, 3=15-25%/yr, 5=>35%
  Competitive Intensity    20%     1=3+ funded incumbents,
                                   3=1-2 weak incumbents,
                                   5=no funded competitor
  Regulatory Ease          15%     1=requires local license,
                                   3=standard compliance,
                                   5=no sector regulation
  Strategic Fit            20%     1=core competence mismatch,
                                   3=partial overlap,
                                   5=direct extension of moat

  TOTAL SCORE = Σ(Score × Weight)
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  THRESHOLD:
  > 3.5  → Priority entry; allocate dedicated squad
  2.5-3.5 → Opportunistic; pilot with minimal commitment
  < 2.5  → Deprioritise or monitor; do not invest
```

**Example:** An Indian AI document-processing SaaS scores the UAE market: TAM=3 ($200M), Growth=4 (30%/yr), Competition=4 (1 weak incumbent), Regulation=2 (DIFC license needed), Fit=5 (direct extension). Weighted score = 0.75+0.80+0.80+0.30+1.00 = **3.65 → Priority entry.**

## Formulas, Thresholds & Rules of Thumb

### Pilot Duration
- **Standard pilot window:** 60-90 days for B2B SaaS; 30-45 days for consumer/D2C
- **Minimum viable signal period:** At least two full billing cycles for subscription models before drawing conclusions on churn
- **Rule of thumb:** If you cannot reach 80% of pilot revenue target by Day 60, extend — do not scale. Dead pilots should be killed by Day 90, not nursed past 120.

### Revenue-per-Pilot Threshold
```
  Pilot Go/No-Go Revenue Threshold =
    (Fixed cost of full market entry) ÷ (Target payback months)
    ÷ (Expected pilot-to-full-market revenue multiplier)

  Example:
    Full entry cost = Rs 1.2 Cr
    Target payback = 18 months
    Pilot represents ~10% of full market
    Threshold = 1,20,00,000 ÷ 18 ÷ 10 = Rs 66,667/month in pilot
```
- If pilot MRR crosses threshold by Week 8, proceed to full entry planning.
- If pilot MRR is 50-80% of threshold by Week 12, extend pilot with targeted fixes.
- If pilot MRR is <50% of threshold at Week 10, kill and analyse root cause before re-entry.

### Market Size Minimums
- **Series A stage:** Target TAM ≥ $500M; SAM ≥ $50M; SOM year-3 target ≥ $5M ARR
- **Seed stage beachhead:** SAM as small as $10M acceptable if NPS>60 and gross margin>70%
- **Rule:** Never enter a market where your 3-year revenue ceiling (at 10% market share) is less than your cost of entry

### Cost of Entry vs. Expected LTV
```
  Entry Viability Ratio = (Expected LTV of target segment customers)
                           ÷ (Total cost of market entry)

  Healthy ratio: > 5x
  Marginal ratio: 3-5x (proceed only with low-commitment entry mode)
  Unviable ratio: < 3x (do not enter; wait for cost reduction or LTV improvement)

  Total cost of market entry includes:
    - Legal/regulatory setup
    - First 12 months of local team salaries
    - Marketing/demand generation for pilot
    - Product localisation (language, compliance, integrations)
    - Partner/distributor onboarding costs
```

### CAC Payback Threshold by Entry Mode
| Entry Mode | Acceptable CAC Payback | Red-Flag Threshold |
|------------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Greenfield direct sales | ≤ 18 months | > 24 months |
| Channel/partner-led | ≤ 12 months | > 18 months |
| Acquisition (inherited customers) | ≤ 9 months | > 15 months |
| PLG (product-led growth) | ≤ 6 months | > 12 months |

### Rule of Thumb: The 1/10/100 Entry Test
- **1 pilot customer** signed before committing to a new market = qualitative signal
- **10 paying customers** in the new market = quantitative proof of willingness to pay
- **100 customers** = enough data to optimise GTM motion and justify full infrastructure investment

## Do / Don't

### Do
1. **Do define a precise beachhead ICP before signing a single sales contract** — "mid-market CFOs in manufacturing using SAP" beats "enterprises in India" every time
2. **Do run a time-boxed pilot (60-90 days) with explicit go/no-go criteria written before the pilot starts** — post-hoc rationalisation of a failing pilot destroys capital
3. **Do study local tax, data-residency, and labour laws before writing a line of code** — GDPR, India's DPDPA, UAE's DIFC regulations, and Singapore's PDPA each change your infrastructure architecture
4. **Do map distribution before building product** — who will sell this, how will they reach buyers, what commission structure makes them prioritise you over incumbents
5. **Do negotiate data rights explicitly in every partnership or JV agreement** — in AI businesses, losing access to training data or inference logs is losing the business
6. **Do use the bowling-pin model — win one segment completely before touching the second** — a 40% share in one segment is more fundable than a 4% share across ten
7. **Do localise the sales motion, not just the product** — enterprise sales in Japan require relationship-building over 6-18 months; PLG that works in the US will not substitute
8. **Do plan your exit from the entry mode** — if you enter via a distributor, negotiate buyback rights and customer data ownership from day one so you can go direct when the economics justify it
9. **Do instrument your pilot with the same metrics you use in your core market** — CAC, LTV, NPS, churn, and activation rate must be comparable to diagnose what is different
10. **Do stress-test currency, macro, and geopolitical scenarios before committing to capex in a new geography** — a two-year lease signed at peak enthusiasm is an anchor when the market turns

### Don't
1. **Don't enter a market because a single large enterprise asked you to** — one anchor customer is not a market; validate that there are at least 10 similar buyers before building local presence
2. **Don't build full product localisation before proving demand** — ship an English MVP with manual workarounds; localise after ten paying customers confirm the core value proposition
3. **Don't sign a distribution partnership without a non-exclusivity sunset clause** — an exclusive distribution deal that locks you out of your own market after the partner underperforms is the most common market entry death trap
4. **Don't conflate brand awareness with buying intent** — getting press coverage in a new market is not a pipeline signal; signed LOIs and paid pilots are
5. **Don't enter two new markets simultaneously unless you have independent, fully staffed GTM teams for each** — splitting a single BD person across two markets produces mediocre results in both
6. **Don't underestimate integration costs in acquisition entry** — culture, tech-stack, data model, and customer-contract harmonisation typically cost 30-50% of the acquisition price in hidden effort
7. **Don't assume the pricing model that works in Market A will work in Market B** — willingness to pay, preferred billing cadence (annual vs monthly), and discount expectations vary significantly by geography and vertical
8. **Don't skip competitive intelligence in the target market** — incumbents often behave differently defending home turf than they do in analyst reports; do 10 customer interviews before declaring a market under-served
9. **Don't launch without a local support and success capability** — a new-market customer who cannot get help in their time zone or language will churn before you can iterate
10. **Don't treat market entry as a one-time decision** — market entry is a continuous process of expansion and contraction; build a quarterly review cadence to reallocate resources toward markets showing the strongest signals

## Metric-Driven Scenarios with Anti-Examples

### Scenario 1: AI SaaS Firm Entering the US Mid-Market from India

**Trigger:** An Indian AI-powered contract analytics SaaS (MRR Rs 80 lakh, NPS 62, gross margin 74%) receives inbound trial requests from 12 US companies over 90 days without any US marketing spend. The founding team considers opening a US office.

**Analysis:**
- 12 inbound trials = organic demand signal; acceptable proof for pilot, insufficient for office commitment
- LTV of US mid-market legal tech customer estimated at $45,000 USD
- Cost of 12-month US greenfield (1 AE, 1 CSM, Delaware C-corp, AWS US-East infra) = ~$400,000 USD
- Entry viability ratio = ($45,000 × projected 15 customers year 1) ÷ $400,000 = 1.7x — below threshold
- Revised plan: PLG motion with self-serve US pricing tier + 1 remote US-based CSM contractor ($80,000/yr)
- Entry viability ratio on revised plan = $675,000 ÷ $80,000 = **8.4x — viable**

**Decision:** Enter US via PLG + remote CSM. No office. Re-evaluate at 50 US paying customers or $200K ARR.

**Result:** Month 6: 34 US paying customers, $148K ARR. Month 9: 51 customers, $220K ARR. Company raises Series A with US revenue as the lead headline; opens SF office in month 14 with proven economics.

**Anti-Example:** A comparable Indian HR-tech SaaS received 8 US inquiries and immediately hired a VP of Sales in San Francisco at $280K OTE, leased a WeWork office, and flew the founding team to four US conferences in year 1. Total US spend: $620K. US ARR at month 12: $95K. CAC payback: 78 months. The board forced a US retreat; the VP of Sales departed; momentum in India was disrupted by distraction. Entry viability ratio was never computed before commitment.

### Scenario 2: Consulting Firm Expanding Practice to Southeast Asia via JV

**Trigger:** A Bengaluru-based AI transformation consulting firm (Rs 45 Cr revenue, 120 consultants) wins a $2.1M project for a Singapore bank. The project reveals strong demand for AI governance consulting in Singapore and Malaysia. The firm evaluates three entry options: greenfield office, JV with a local firm, or subcontracting to a local partner.

**Analysis:**
- Market attractiveness score for Singapore: 3.8 (priority tier)
- Market attractiveness score for Malaysia: 3.1 (opportunistic tier)
- Greenfield Singapore office: 18-month break-even, $1.8M setup cost
- JV with established local strategy firm (50/50): $400K contribution, access to 80 existing client relationships, shared back-office
- Subcontracting: zero setup cost, 30% margin haircut, no brand presence, no data rights on deliverables

**Decision:** JV with local partner for Singapore (priority market). Subcontract Malaysia work through JV partner for 12 months, then assess brownfield acquisition of a small Malaysian boutique if revenue crosses $500K.

**Result:** JV closes in month 3. By month 12, Singapore JV generates $3.4M revenue. Two Malaysia enterprise clients convert. The firm acquires a 4-person Kuala Lumpur boutique in month 15 for $180K — brownfield entry that costs less than one month of greenfield setup. By month 24, combined SEA revenue is $6.8M with 68% gross margin.

**Anti-Example:** A rival Pune-based IT services firm entered Singapore by hiring three senior consultants directly from the market at premium compensation ($220K each), without a local partner. Eighteen months in, they had $890K revenue but $1.9M in costs. None of the three hires had pre-existing relationships with the mid-market banking segment the firm was targeting. The firm closed the Singapore entity and took a Rs 8 Cr write-off. The failure was attributed to entering the wrong sub-segment (mid-market banking) in a JV-dependent relationship economy without a local credibility anchor.

### Scenario 3: Product Company Using Acquisition Entry into Enterprise Segment

**Trigger:** A Series B AI-powered HR analytics platform ($4.2M ARR, 95% SMB customers, average ACV $12,000) wants to move upmarket to enterprise (ACV $80,000+). Direct enterprise sales would require 18-24 months to build pipeline. The CEO identifies a struggling enterprise HRIS company with $1.8M ARR, 14 enterprise logos, and a team of 6 — asking price $2.4M.

**Analysis:**
- Acquiring company inherits: 14 enterprise reference logos, 2 ex-Workday engineers (embedded HRIS expertise), existing compliance certifications (SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001)
- Cost of equivalent greenfield enterprise sales build: estimated $3.1M over 24 months (VP Enterprise, 2 AEs, 1 SE, 1 CSM, demand gen) with no guaranteed logos
- Acquisition cost = $2.4M + $300K integration = $2.7M
- Expected incremental ARR from 14 logos cross-sold to AI analytics layer: $80K × 8 logos (conservative 57% attach rate) = $640K year 1
- CAC payback on acquisition = $2.7M ÷ ($640K ÷ 12) = **50 months** — marginal
- Revised model including 6 new enterprise logos from inherited sales pipeline: $80K × 14 = $1.12M ARR, payback = **28.9 months** — acceptable for brownfield enterprise entry

**Decision:** Acquire; retain all 6 employees for 24 months with earnout tied to cross-sell attach rate.

**Result:** Month 6: 9 of 14 logos adopting AI analytics add-on (64% attach — above plan). Month 12: $1.3M incremental ARR from acquisition. Month 18: 3 new enterprise logos sourced by inherited sales team. The acquisition effectively compressed the enterprise entry timeline from 24 months to 8 months for material revenue.

**Anti-Example:** A competing HR-tech company evaluated a similar acquisition but balked at the price and chose instead to hire one enterprise AE from Salesforce at $300K OTE. The AE's network was in CRM, not HRIS. By month 12, the AE had generated $240K in pipeline, none closed. By month 18, the AE departed. The company had spent $450K (salary + overhead) with zero enterprise ARR. Total timeline loss versus the acquisition path: 20+ months. The CFO later noted that the acquisition would have paid back in 29 months while the greenfield build had infinite payback — it produced nothing.

## Practitioner Playbook

**Step-by-step guide to executing a disciplined market entry for an IT/AI/Product/Consulting firm.**

1. **Define the entry hypothesis in writing before any spending.** State: target segment (precise ICP), geography, entry mode, 90-day success metrics, and the one assumption that — if wrong — kills the thesis. Share with the full leadership team and get written sign-off.

2. **Run a market attractiveness assessment on at least three candidate markets.** Use the weighted scoring framework (Section 2, Framework 4). Allocate resources only to markets scoring above 3.5. Document markets scoring 2.5-3.5 as the watch list for next quarter.

3. **Map the regulatory and data-residency landscape before building.** Engage a local legal advisor for a two-week review of: data-localisation requirements, sector-specific licenses, employment law for contractors vs. employees, tax treaty implications, and any AI-specific regulations (EU AI Act applicability, India's DPDPA, Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework).

4. **Identify and score three to five potential partners, distributors, or JV candidates.** Score on: existing customer relationships, cultural fit, financial stability, willingness to share customer data back to your platform, and exit/buyback provisions. Never enter a partnership negotiation without a BATNA (best alternative to negotiated agreement).

5. **Design the pilot with explicit go/no-go criteria written before launch.** Specify: pilot duration (default 90 days), minimum revenue threshold, minimum NPS threshold, minimum activation rate, and the kill condition (if metric X is not met by day Y, we exit). Distribute this document to the board before the pilot starts.

6. **Staff the pilot with your best people, not your spare capacity.** The most common pilot failure mode is assigning junior or disengaged team members to a new market and then concluding the market does not work. Send the person who would be your ideal first Country Manager to run the pilot personally.

7. **Instrument the pilot with identical metrics to your core market.** Track: CAC by channel, LTV by customer cohort, NPS at 30/60/90 days, activation rate, expansion revenue rate, and competitive win/loss. Build a weekly dashboard shared with the founding team.

8. **Conduct a structured mid-pilot review at Day 45.** If revenue is below 60% of threshold, convene a war room to identify root cause: wrong ICP, wrong pricing, wrong channel, wrong entry mode, or wrong market. Make one targeted intervention. If revenue is above 80% of threshold, begin full-entry planning in parallel.

9. **At Day 90, apply the go/no-go criteria without compromise.** Present results against pre-committed thresholds. Extend the pilot only if: (a) you have clear evidence of a fixable single variable causing underperformance AND (b) the fix can be implemented within 30 additional days. Otherwise, kill or pivot the entry mode.

10. **On a green light, build the local operating model before committing to headcount.** Define: org chart for local team (who reports to whom, what is centralised vs. local), pricing and discount authority, partner commission structure, support SLAs in local time zone, and the first 30 customers' onboarding plan.

11. **Sign local infrastructure agreements with escape clauses for the first 24 months.** Prefer flexible leases, cloud infrastructure over owned hardware, contractor-first hiring with conversion options. Lock in fixed costs only after month 18 when unit economics are proven.

12. **Plan the second market entry while still executing the first.** The bowling-pin model requires that your team understands where they are going next, not just where they are. By month 6 of your first market, run the market attractiveness assessment on the next two candidate markets so capital allocation decisions for year 2 are ready when the board asks.

13. **Build a market entry retrospective at the 12-month mark.** Document: what you assumed, what was true, what surprised you, what you would do differently, and what the entry has taught you about your product's global applicability. This retrospective becomes the input for the next market entry's hypothesis.

14. **Negotiate data and IP ownership at the start of every partnership, not after it succeeds.** Once a partnership is generating revenue, the leverage to negotiate favourable data terms disappears. Ensure your contract specifies: who owns customer data generated on your platform, who owns derivative models trained on that data, what happens to data if the partnership ends, and whether the partner can train competing models using insights from your platform.

## Content Critique & Depth Gaps

**What is missing for IIM/HBS MBA-level treatment of this topic:**

### 1. Game-Theoretic Analysis of Incumbent Response
The source content treats market entry as a one-sided decision. At MBA depth, entry strategy must account for incumbent reaction functions — will they cut price, accelerate product, acquire your distribution partner, or sue you for IP infringement? Schelling's commitment theory and Tirole's industrial organisation frameworks model how incumbents signal and execute retaliation. Missing: analysis of entry-deterrence strategies (limit pricing, capacity precommitment) and how startups should sequence entry to minimise incumbent incentive to fight back.

### 2. Real Options Framework for Entry Timing
Entering a market is not a binary now/never decision — it is a real option. The Black-Scholes intuition applied to market entry says: the value of waiting increases when market uncertainty is high, when your cost of entry is not time-sensitive, and when information is arriving that will resolve uncertainty cheaply. Missing: a real options valuation approach that helps founders decide whether to enter now, wait 6 months, or acquire an option via a small partnership rather than full commitment.

### 3. Cultural Distance and Psychic Distance Models
Johanson and Vahlne's Uppsala internationalisation model argues that firms enter markets in order of increasing psychic distance (cultural, linguistic, regulatory similarity). Missing: a structured framework for scoring psychic distance between home and target market and using it to sequence international expansion. This is particularly relevant for Indian AI firms entering the US, EU, Middle East, or Southeast Asia where psychic distance varies dramatically.

### 4. Portfolio Approach to Market Entry
HBS case studies treat market entry as a portfolio of bets, not a single thesis. Missing: a framework for managing 3-5 simultaneous market entry experiments with shared learnings, capital reallocation triggers, and a governance model for killing underperformers without political friction.

### 5. Pricing Power Transfer Across Markets
The source content does not address how pricing strategy must shift between markets. A SaaS firm commanding $100K ACV in the US may need to price at $15K ACV in India for the same product — but doing so risks channel conflict and customer perception problems if those markets interact. Missing: a price architecture framework for multi-market companies that prevents internal price arbitrage and channel conflict.

### 6. Exit Strategy and Market Withdrawal
No entry strategy is complete without an exit criterion. Missing: a structured framework for market withdrawal — when to declare a market a failure, how to manage customer obligations during withdrawal, and how to preserve brand equity in markets you are exiting. This is especially important for consulting and IT services firms that have signed multi-year contracts in markets they later find uneconomical.

### 7. Network Effects and Critical Mass in New Markets
For platform businesses and AI companies where value is a function of data volume or user network, entering a new market means starting below the critical mass threshold. Missing: a critical mass calculation framework and the strategies used to cross it quickly (seeding supply, subsidising early demand, importing data from adjacent markets).

### 8. Regulatory Arbitrage as an Entry Strategy
Some of the most successful market entries exploit regulatory asymmetry — entering a market where incumbents are constrained by legacy regulations that new entrants are exempt from. Missing: a systematic approach to identifying and quantifying regulatory arbitrage opportunities and the associated risk of regulatory catch-up (the incumbent lobbies to close the gap).

## Quick-Recall Card

- **Market entry is a sequenced portfolio of bets, not a single leap** — define the beachhead, prove the unit economics, then expand
- **Four primary entry modes:** Greenfield (full control, high cost), Acquisition/Brownfield (fast revenue, integration risk), JV/Partnership (shared risk, governance risk), Licensing/Franchising (asset-light, low control)
- **Pilot first, always** — 60-90 days, pre-committed go/no-go metrics, kill criteria defined before launch
- **Market attractiveness threshold:** Weighted score > 3.5 = priority entry; 2.5-3.5 = opportunistic pilot; < 2.5 = pass
- **Entry viability ratio:** LTV of projected year-1 customers ÷ total cost of entry — must be > 5x for greenfield, > 3x for partnership modes
- **Bowling pin rule:** Achieve >40% share in beachhead before touching adjacent segment — do not spread thin
- **In AI/SaaS:** Never cede data rights, model weights, or inference logs in a partnership agreement — these are the moat
- **Local legal review is not optional** — data residency, sector licensing, and labour law can change your architecture and cost structure by 30-50%
- **CAC payback benchmarks:** PLG ≤ 6 months, channel-led ≤ 12 months, direct sales ≤ 18 months, acquisition-led ≤ 9 months on inherited base
- **Plan market 2 while executing market 1** — entry is a compounding skill; sequencing decisions made at month 6 of market 1 determine the quality of market 2's start
- **Negotiate data and IP terms at the start of every partnership** — leverage disappears once the partnership is generating revenue
- **Kill criteria are not negotiable** — extending a failing pilot past its pre-committed kill date is the most common way to turn a small loss into a large one

As a PM/Consultant/AI Lead, the one question to answer with this framework is: "Which single market segment can we win decisively in the next 90 days with the resources we have today, and what does winning look like in measurable terms?"
