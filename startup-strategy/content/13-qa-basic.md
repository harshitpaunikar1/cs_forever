# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and concepts covering startup strategy. Questions 1 through 70.

## Q1. What is a startup?

**Level:** Basic

A startup is a young company built to search for a scalable and repeatable business model. It operates under extreme uncertainty, with limited resources, and usually aims for high growth. Unlike a traditional small business, it is designed to scale far beyond its founders.

**Real-life applications:**
- Classifying a new venture to decide whether VC or bank loans are appropriate.
- Choosing organizational structure for a growth-focused team.
- Setting realistic milestones during the early validation phase.
- Explaining the risk profile to early employees or co-founders.

**Key concepts:** `startup`, `scalability`, `uncertainty`, `growth`, `business model`

---

## Q2. What is an entrepreneurial mindset?

**Level:** Basic

An entrepreneurial mindset is a way of thinking that favors opportunity-spotting, experimentation, and ownership of outcomes. It treats failure as feedback and acts before having all the answers. This mindset is common to founders, intrapreneurs, and early-stage hires.

**Real-life applications:**
- Encouraging employees to run small experiments without heavy approvals.
- Hiring for attitude alongside technical skills.
- Turning a complaint from a customer into a product idea the same week.
- Coaching a team to own results, not just tasks.

**Key concepts:** `mindset`, `ownership`, `action bias`, `resilience`, `learning`

---

## Q3. What is opportunity identification?

**Level:** Basic

Opportunity identification is the process of finding a problem worth solving and customers willing to pay for a solution. It combines observation, customer interviews, and market research. The aim is to find gaps that are urgent, painful, and underserved.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running 20 customer interviews before writing any code.
- Spotting workarounds customers use that reveal a need.
- Sizing a market before deciding to commit.
- Ranking multiple ideas by pain, frequency, and reach.

**Key concepts:** `opportunity`, `problem-solution fit`, `market gap`, `pain point`, `validation`

---

## Q4. What is the Business Model Canvas?

**Level:** Basic

The Business Model Canvas is a one-page template with nine blocks that describe how a business creates, delivers, and captures value. The blocks cover customer segments, value proposition, channels, relationships, revenue, resources, activities, partners, and costs. Founders use it to see the whole company on one sheet.

**Real-life applications:**
- Brainstorming a new product idea in a single workshop.
- Comparing two business models side by side.
- Briefing an investor in 10 minutes instead of 40 slides.
- Spotting a missing decision, like no defined channel.

**Key concepts:** `business model`, `value proposition`, `canvas`, `channels`, `revenue streams`

---

## Q5. What is Lean Startup methodology?

**Level:** Basic

Lean Startup is a way to build companies through rapid Build–Measure–Learn cycles. The team ships a minimum viable product, measures real user behavior, and decides whether to persevere or pivot. It aims to cut waste by testing assumptions cheaply.

**Real-life applications:**
- Launching a landing page before building a product.
- Running a two-week experiment to test a new feature.
- Deciding to pivot a failing product after three months of data.
- Using a manual process before writing automation code.

**Key concepts:** `lean startup`, `MVP`, `build-measure-learn`, `validated learning`, `pivot`

---

## Q6. What is a Minimum Viable Product (MVP)?

**Level:** Basic

An MVP is the simplest version of a product that can test one important assumption with real users. It is not a cheap version of the final product; it is a learning tool. The goal is to get real feedback with the least effort.

**Real-life applications:**
- A single landing page to test demand before coding.
- A WhatsApp service that mimics an app to study workflows.
- A Figma prototype shown to 10 prospects to check interest.
- A paid pilot with one customer before generalizing.

**Key concepts:** `MVP`, `validation`, `experiment`, `prototype`, `customer feedback`

---

## Q7. What is product-market fit?

**Level:** Basic

Product-market fit is the state where a product clearly satisfies a strong market demand. Customers pull the product from the company, retention holds steady, and growth starts to become organic. It signals the end of search mode and the start of scale mode.

**Real-life applications:**
- Measuring 40% "very disappointed" score in surveys to confirm fit.
- Watching week-8 retention to decide on scaling marketing.
- Pausing hiring until fit signals appear in two cohorts.
- Using fit data to pitch a Series A round.

**Key concepts:** `product-market fit`, `retention`, `demand`, `organic growth`, `scalability`

---

## Q8. What is customer acquisition cost (CAC)?

**Level:** Basic

CAC is the total sales and marketing spend divided by the number of new customers acquired in the same period. It tells you how much you pay for each customer. Managers use it to decide which channels and campaigns are worth scaling.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing paid search against content marketing spend efficiency.
- Setting payback-period targets for new channels.
- Deciding when to hire more sellers versus running more ads.
- Reporting channel economics to the board.

**Key concepts:** `CAC`, `unit economics`, `channel efficiency`, `marketing spend`, `payback`

---

## Q9. What is customer lifetime value (LTV)?

**Level:** Basic

LTV is the total revenue — or sometimes gross profit — a customer brings across their full relationship with the company. It depends on average order value, purchase frequency, and retention duration. LTV paired with CAC reveals whether the business model is sustainable.

**Real-life applications:**
- Calculating how much to spend acquiring each new user.
- Setting pricing to target a specific LTV-to-CAC ratio.
- Identifying high-value customer segments to prioritize.
- Evaluating loyalty-program investments.

**Key concepts:** `LTV`, `retention`, `unit economics`, `CAC ratio`, `customer value`

---

## Q10. Why is the LTV-to-CAC ratio important?

**Level:** Basic

The LTV-to-CAC ratio compares how much a customer earns you against how much they cost to acquire. A healthy SaaS target is 3:1 or higher; below 1:1 means the business loses money on every customer. It is one of the simplest tests of a viable business model.

**Real-life applications:**
- Judging whether a marketing campaign is worth continuing.
- Benchmarking against industry standards when raising funds.
- Deciding to invest in retention over acquisition.
- Evaluating which customer segments are truly profitable.

**Key concepts:** `LTV/CAC`, `unit economics`, `payback period`, `profitability`, `efficiency`

---

## Q11. What is a pivot in startup terminology?

**Level:** Basic

A pivot is a structured change in strategy based on what the team has learned. It may change the customer segment, product, or revenue model — while keeping the founding vision. Pivots are normal; many successful companies pivoted at least once.

**Real-life applications:**
- Moving a failing B2C product to B2B where buyers respond better.
- Switching from ad-based to subscription revenue after low click-through.
- Turning one popular feature into the whole product.
- Exiting a saturated geography and relaunching in a new one.

**Key concepts:** `pivot`, `strategy`, `learning`, `iteration`, `adaptation`

---

## Q12. What is bootstrapping?

**Level:** Basic

Bootstrapping is funding a startup from personal savings, founder salaries, or customer revenue without outside capital. It keeps ownership and control with the founders. The trade-off is slower growth and tighter cash flow.

**Real-life applications:**
- A consultant reinvesting fees into building a software product.
- A D2C brand using pre-orders to fund first inventory.
- Avoiding dilution during the risky early years.
- Building a lifestyle business without VC pressure to exit.

**Key concepts:** `bootstrapping`, `self-funding`, `cash flow`, `ownership`, `control`

---

## Q13. What is a seed round?

**Level:** Basic

A seed round is the first priced equity investment, typically used to find product-market fit. It usually ranges from a few lakhs to a few crores depending on geography and sector. Investors are usually angels, micro-VCs, or seed-stage funds.

**Real-life applications:**
- Funding 12–18 months of product development and early hires.
- Bringing on experienced angels who also advise.
- Setting valuation benchmarks for later rounds.
- Proving early traction before the Series A.

**Key concepts:** `seed round`, `equity`, `angel investors`, `valuation`, `early stage`

---

## Q14. What is venture capital?

**Level:** Basic

Venture capital (VC) is money raised from a fund and invested in high-growth startups in exchange for equity. VCs aim for large exits — IPOs or acquisitions — within a 7–10 year horizon. They typically take board seats and offer strategic guidance.

**Real-life applications:**
- Raising capital to scale a proven product across markets.
- Accessing investor networks for hiring and partnerships.
- Planning an exit strategy that aligns with fund timelines.
- Accepting dilution in exchange for growth speed.

**Key concepts:** `venture capital`, `equity`, `exit`, `growth`, `dilution`

---

## Q15. What is an angel investor?

**Level:** Basic

An angel investor is an individual who invests personal money in early-stage startups. Angels usually write small cheques but come with experience and networks. They are often former founders or senior industry executives.

**Real-life applications:**
- Getting a first cheque before institutional funds engage.
- Pulling in domain expertise along with capital.
- Building credibility for the next fundraising round.
- Filling a gap while revenue is too early for VCs.

**Key concepts:** `angel investor`, `early stage`, `equity`, `mentorship`, `seed`

---

## Q16. What is a value proposition?

**Level:** Basic

A value proposition is a clear statement of the benefit a product delivers, for whom, and why it is better than the alternatives. It is the core of a company's pitch to customers. A strong value proposition is specific, believable, and easy to repeat.

**Real-life applications:**
- Writing a single sentence for the website hero section.
- Aligning sales pitch and marketing copy around one message.
- Deciding which features to emphasize in a product demo.
- Training new hires on what the company promises customers.

**Key concepts:** `value proposition`, `differentiation`, `benefit`, `positioning`, `customer`

---

## Q17. What is a customer segment?

**Level:** Basic

A customer segment is a distinct group of buyers with similar needs, behaviors, or characteristics. Startups target one narrow segment first before expanding. Clear segmentation makes marketing, product, and pricing decisions sharper.

**Real-life applications:**
- Targeting only working professionals under 30 for a first product launch.
- Creating distinct pricing plans for small, medium, and enterprise buyers.
- Tailoring onboarding flows to different user types.
- Prioritizing which segment to serve when resources are limited.

**Key concepts:** `segment`, `targeting`, `niche`, `persona`, `customer profile`

---

## Q18. What is a target market?

**Level:** Basic

A target market is the specific group of customers a business intends to serve with its product. It is chosen based on size, willingness to pay, and fit with the company's capabilities. Targeting keeps marketing and product focused.

**Real-life applications:**
- Defining the first 1,000 users a new app will chase.
- Picking cities where a logistics startup will launch first.
- Writing ad copy that speaks to one specific buyer persona.
- Deciding to sell only to SMEs, not enterprises.

**Key concepts:** `target market`, `TAM`, `segmentation`, `focus`, `positioning`

---

## Q19. What does TAM mean?

**Level:** Basic

TAM — Total Addressable Market — is the total revenue opportunity if the company captured 100% of a market. It is a rough upper bound, not a forecast. Investors use TAM to judge whether a business can grow to a meaningful size.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sizing the market before choosing a product direction.
- Convincing investors the opportunity is large enough to fund.
- Comparing the potential of two product ideas.
- Setting long-term revenue ambition.

**Key concepts:** `TAM`, `market size`, `SAM`, `SOM`, `opportunity`

---

## Q20. What is competitive positioning?

**Level:** Basic

Competitive positioning is the claim a company stakes in the customer's mind about why it is the better choice. It narrows competition to one clear attribute — speed, price, niche, or trust. A strong position makes every marketing rupee work harder.

**Real-life applications:**
- Framing a product as "the Trello for design agencies."
- Choosing a pricing model that signals premium or budget.
- Walking away from feature requests that dilute the position.
- Aligning sales scripts around a single point of difference.

**Key concepts:** `positioning`, `differentiation`, `competition`, `niche`, `brand`

---

## Q21. What is a go-to-market strategy?

**Level:** Basic

A go-to-market (GTM) strategy is the plan for how a company will reach customers and win their first sale. It covers channel choice, pricing, messaging, and sales motion. A clear GTM avoids the mistake of building without a path to users.

**Real-life applications:**
- Choosing inside sales for mid-market accounts versus self-serve for SMBs.
- Mapping channels before hiring the first marketer.
- Designing a launch plan with sequenced media beats.
- Testing two GTMs in parallel and doubling down on the winner.

**Key concepts:** `GTM`, `channels`, `sales motion`, `launch`, `strategy`

---

## Q22. What is market entry strategy?

**Level:** Basic

Market entry strategy is the plan for how a startup enters a new market — geography, segment, or category. Options include direct launch, partnership, joint venture, franchising, or acquisition. The right choice depends on speed, cost, and required local knowledge.

**Real-life applications:**
- Choosing a distributor partner for a new country.
- Starting with one city before expanding nationally.
- Acquiring a small competitor to enter faster.
- Running a 90-day pilot before signing long leases.

**Key concepts:** `market entry`, `expansion`, `beachhead`, `partnership`, `pilot`

---

## Q23. What is scaling?

**Level:** Basic

Scaling is growing the business rapidly without a proportional rise in costs or a drop in quality. It depends on systems, repeatable processes, and operating leverage. Scaling before product-market fit is one of the most common startup mistakes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Adding 10 cities in a year using a playbook instead of ad hoc launches.
- Automating onboarding so one rep can handle 200 customers.
- Investing in infrastructure that supports 10x growth.
- Hiring ahead of the growth curve for a critical function.

**Key concepts:** `scaling`, `growth`, `operating leverage`, `systems`, `efficiency`

---

## Q24. What is a strategic partnership?

**Level:** Basic

A strategic partnership is a formal agreement between companies to create more value together than each could alone. Forms include distribution, technology, co-marketing, and supply deals. Good partnerships bring scale, trust, or reach that money alone cannot buy quickly.

**Real-life applications:**
- A startup embedding insurance into a payment app with 5M users.
- Two SaaS firms offering a joint bundle to enterprise buyers.
- A logistics partner getting capacity priority with a large retailer.
- Revenue-share deals with consulting firms for distribution.

**Key concepts:** `partnership`, `alliance`, `distribution`, `co-marketing`, `revenue share`

---

## Q25. What is runway?

**Level:** Basic

Runway is the number of months a startup can operate at its current burn rate before cash runs out. It is calculated as cash balance divided by monthly burn. Managers watch runway weekly and plan fundraising 9 months before it ends.

**Real-life applications:**
- Deciding when to start a fundraise so the round closes in time.
- Cutting costs to extend runway in tough markets.
- Communicating survival timelines to the team.
- Pacing hiring plans against cash reserves.

**Key concepts:** `runway`, `burn rate`, `cash`, `survival`, `fundraising`

---

## Q26. What is burn rate?

**Level:** Basic

Burn rate is the monthly net loss of cash after revenue. Gross burn is total spending; net burn subtracts revenue. Lower burn means more time to find fit or close the next round.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking burn against plan every month.
- Triggering hiring freezes when burn exceeds a threshold.
- Reporting monthly burn to investors in board updates.
- Running scenarios to see how a hire affects runway.

**Key concepts:** `burn rate`, `cash flow`, `expenses`, `runway`, `financial planning`

---

## Q27. What is dilution?

**Level:** Basic

Dilution is the reduction in a founder's or early shareholder's ownership percentage when the company issues new shares. Every round of equity funding causes dilution. Managing dilution well means raising only what you need at the right valuation.

**Real-life applications:**
- Modeling dilution from a 20% equity round on the cap table.
- Using venture debt to avoid dilution when revenue is predictable.
- Setting ESOP pool sizes to balance founder and employee ownership.
- Comparing two term sheets by post-money ownership.

**Key concepts:** `dilution`, `equity`, `cap table`, `ownership`, `fundraising`

---

## Q28. What is an ESOP?

**Level:** Basic

An Employee Stock Option Plan (ESOP) is a program that gives employees the right to buy company shares at a preset price over time. It aligns staff with long-term value creation. ESOPs are standard in startups to attract talent despite lower salaries.

**Real-life applications:**
- Offering stock options to a first engineering hire.
- Reserving a 10% pool before a Series A round.
- Creating vesting schedules that reward long tenure.
- Buying back employee options during liquidity events.

**Key concepts:** `ESOP`, `stock options`, `compensation`, `equity`, `vesting`

---

## Q29. What is a term sheet?

**Level:** Basic

A term sheet is a short, mostly non-binding document that lists the key terms of a funding round. It covers valuation, investment amount, board composition, and investor rights. Signing one usually leads to detailed legal contracts.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing offers from two investors side by side.
- Negotiating liquidation preference and board seats.
- Setting expectations before lawyers draft final documents.
- Understanding protective provisions before accepting capital.

**Key concepts:** `term sheet`, `valuation`, `board`, `legal`, `investment`

---

## Q30. What is pre-money valuation?

**Level:** Basic

Pre-money valuation is what a company is worth just before new capital is added. After the round, the post-money valuation equals pre-money plus the investment. The split between pre-money, investment, and post-money determines founder dilution.

**Real-life applications:**
- Negotiating with investors on the company's pre-money value.
- Modeling dilution scenarios on the cap table.
- Setting a realistic valuation based on traction benchmarks.
- Comparing term sheets with different pre-money numbers.

**Key concepts:** `pre-money`, `post-money`, `valuation`, `equity`, `dilution`

---

## Q31. What is post-money valuation?

**Level:** Basic

Post-money valuation is the company's worth right after new capital is invested. It equals pre-money valuation plus the new investment. Investors often quote post-money to simplify ownership calculations.

**Real-life applications:**
- Calculating an investor's ownership stake after the round.
- Comparing dilution between two term sheets.
- Setting targets for the next round's valuation.
- Reporting valuation milestones to the board.

**Key concepts:** `post-money`, `pre-money`, `valuation`, `equity`, `ownership`

---

## Q32. What is a pitch deck?

**Level:** Basic

A pitch deck is a short slide deck — usually 10 to 15 slides — that introduces a startup to investors. It covers problem, solution, market, traction, team, and ask. The best decks tell a clear story in under 10 minutes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Opening doors to first investor conversations.
- Preparing consistent messaging across co-founders.
- Iterating the deck based on investor feedback.
- Customizing for different audiences — customers, VCs, employees.

**Key concepts:** `pitch deck`, `investors`, `storytelling`, `fundraising`, `traction`

---

## Q33. What is an elevator pitch?

**Level:** Basic

An elevator pitch is a 30- to 60-second summary of the business, covering what it does, for whom, and why it is special. It is designed to trigger interest quickly. Founders memorize multiple versions for different audiences.

**Real-life applications:**
- Introducing a startup at a networking event.
- Delivering a concise intro in investor meetings.
- Aligning the team around a clear, short description.
- Writing website headlines and app store descriptions.

**Key concepts:** `elevator pitch`, `messaging`, `storytelling`, `value proposition`, `sales`

---

## Q34. What is validation?

**Level:** Basic

Validation is the process of confirming, through real customer behavior, that an assumption about the business is true. It turns opinions into evidence. Validation can come from signups, payments, retention, or direct use.

**Real-life applications:**
- Counting pre-orders from a landing page before building product.
- Running a pilot with five customers to validate willingness to pay.
- Comparing two pricing pages to validate what users prefer.
- Using cohort data to confirm retention holds.

**Key concepts:** `validation`, `experiment`, `proof`, `assumption`, `evidence`

---

## Q35. What is a customer interview?

**Level:** Basic

A customer interview is a 20–40 minute structured conversation with a potential or current user to learn about their problems, workflows, and willingness to pay. It uses open-ended questions, not sales pitches. Interviews reveal insights that surveys cannot.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running 20 interviews before writing product specs.
- Segmenting users based on patterns across conversations.
- Validating or killing a product idea early.
- Prioritizing features based on stated pain.

**Key concepts:** `customer interview`, `research`, `discovery`, `qualitative`, `insight`

---

## Q36. What is a landing page?

**Level:** Basic

A landing page is a focused web page built for a single conversion goal — a signup, pre-order, or lead. Startups use it to test demand before building a product. It is often the fastest MVP possible.

**Real-life applications:**
- Measuring signup rate to validate interest in an idea.
- Running ads that send traffic to a dedicated page.
- A/B testing two value propositions for clarity.
- Collecting early waitlist emails to seed launch.

**Key concepts:** `landing page`, `conversion`, `MVP`, `test`, `demand`

---

## Q37. What is a funnel?

**Level:** Basic

A funnel is a model of the stages a customer passes through — awareness, interest, consideration, conversion, retention. Each stage has a conversion rate. Startups optimize each step to turn more prospects into paying, loyal users.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking drop-off from visitor to signup to paid user.
- Identifying the worst-performing step to focus on.
- Designing onboarding to maximize activation.
- Comparing funnel metrics across marketing channels.

**Key concepts:** `funnel`, `conversion`, `retention`, `acquisition`, `metrics`

---

## Q38. What is churn?

**Level:** Basic

Churn is the share of customers or revenue lost over a given period. High churn signals weak product-market fit or poor onboarding. Reducing churn is usually cheaper than acquiring new customers.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking monthly revenue churn in a SaaS business.
- Running exit surveys to learn why users leave.
- Designing retention programs for at-risk accounts.
- Comparing churn across customer segments.

**Key concepts:** `churn`, `retention`, `LTV`, `subscription`, `attrition`

---

## Q39. What is retention?

**Level:** Basic

Retention is the percentage of users who keep using the product over time. It is the inverse of churn and the strongest signal of real value. Good retention curves flatten after the early drop instead of decaying to zero.

**Real-life applications:**
- Plotting cohort retention week by week for a consumer app.
- Setting retention targets before scaling marketing.
- Designing features that encourage repeat use.
- Comparing retention across user sources to find the best channel.

**Key concepts:** `retention`, `cohort`, `engagement`, `PMF`, `loyalty`

---

## Q40. What is an activation metric?

**Level:** Basic

An activation metric captures the moment a new user experiences the core value of the product. Examples are sending the first message, completing the first trade, or inviting a teammate. Moving more signups to activation is often the biggest growth lever.

**Real-life applications:**
- Defining "activated user" as someone who completes a task on day one.
- Redesigning onboarding to hit the activation milestone faster.
- Tracking activation rate by channel to spot bad-fit traffic.
- Using activation as a leading indicator of retention.

**Key concepts:** `activation`, `onboarding`, `aha moment`, `retention`, `funnel`

---

## Q41. What are vanity metrics?

**Level:** Basic

Vanity metrics are numbers that look impressive but don't guide decisions — total signups, page views, cumulative downloads. They feel good but hide the real health of the business. Actionable metrics like active users and revenue tell the truth.

**Real-life applications:**
- Stopping board reporting of cumulative signups in favor of MAU.
- Replacing "downloads" with "weekly active users".
- Auditing dashboards to remove misleading numbers.
- Coaching marketers to focus on CAC and retention, not impressions.

**Key concepts:** `vanity metric`, `actionable metric`, `KPI`, `analytics`, `reporting`

---

## Q42. What are actionable metrics?

**Level:** Basic

Actionable metrics are numbers tied to a specific decision a team will make. Examples are activation rate, cohort retention, CAC payback, and revenue per user. A good metric changes your next move.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting a weekly target for activated users per channel.
- Tracking revenue cohort curves to spot product issues.
- Defining a KPI tree from north-star metric down to daily actions.
- Tying team OKRs to actionable metrics rather than outputs.

**Key concepts:** `actionable metric`, `KPI`, `OKR`, `decision making`, `data`

---

## Q43. What is a north-star metric?

**Level:** Basic

A north-star metric is the single most important number that captures the value the product delivers. Examples include nights booked for Airbnb, messages sent for WhatsApp, and orders delivered for Swiggy. It keeps every team aligned.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting company-wide OKRs under the north-star metric.
- Aligning product, marketing, and ops around one goal.
- Cutting projects that don't move the metric.
- Reviewing the metric weekly in leadership meetings.

**Key concepts:** `north star`, `KPI`, `alignment`, `strategy`, `metrics`

---

## Q44. What is a cohort?

**Level:** Basic

A cohort is a group of users who share a defining event in the same time window — same signup month, same campaign. Cohort analysis compares behavior across these groups over time. It reveals whether product changes improve retention.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing month-1 retention across signup cohorts.
- Spotting a drop in retention tied to a specific product change.
- Measuring the impact of a marketing campaign on long-term behavior.
- Segmenting analytics by cohort rather than averages.

**Key concepts:** `cohort analysis`, `retention`, `segment`, `analytics`, `behavior`

---

## Q45. What is growth hacking?

**Level:** Basic

Growth hacking is a data-driven, experiment-heavy approach to growing a startup quickly and cheaply. It blends marketing, product, and engineering to find unconventional ways to acquire and retain users. Classic examples include Dropbox's referral program and Airbnb's Craigslist integration.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running weekly A/B tests on signup pages.
- Building a referral loop that rewards both sides.
- Automating outbound messaging with personalization.
- Hacking an existing platform for distribution.

**Key concepts:** `growth hacking`, `experiments`, `acquisition`, `referral`, `virality`

---

## Q46. What is a referral program?

**Level:** Basic

A referral program rewards existing customers for bringing new ones. The reward can be cash, credit, free months, or features. Well-designed referrals cut CAC dramatically because trust travels through the social graph.

**Real-life applications:**
- Giving both referrer and referee Rs 100 credit.
- Tracking viral coefficient to measure referral strength.
- Automating reward delivery inside the app.
- Running time-limited referral campaigns around launches.

**Key concepts:** `referral`, `virality`, `CAC`, `incentive`, `word of mouth`

---

## Q47. What is virality?

**Level:** Basic

Virality is the property of a product whose users bring in more users as a side effect of use. The viral coefficient "k" measures how many new users each current user generates. A k above 1 means the user base grows even without marketing.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building sharing features directly into the product workflow.
- Adding visible branding to invitations or outputs.
- Tracking k weekly for viral loops.
- Testing incentives to lift referral rates.

**Key concepts:** `virality`, `viral coefficient`, `growth loop`, `referral`, `network effect`

---

## Q48. What is a network effect?

**Level:** Basic

A network effect exists when a product becomes more valuable as more people use it. Classic examples are phones, social networks, and marketplaces. Network effects can lock in a market position and make competition difficult.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building a marketplace where more buyers attract more sellers.
- Creating messaging apps where user value grows with reach.
- Using network effects as a moat in investor pitches.
- Designing product features that get better with more users.

**Key concepts:** `network effect`, `marketplace`, `moat`, `lock-in`, `scale`

---

## Q49. What is a marketplace startup?

**Level:** Basic

A marketplace startup connects two or more groups — buyers and sellers, hosts and guests, drivers and riders. It takes a fee or cut of each transaction. Marketplaces rely on solving the chicken-and-egg problem of getting both sides on board.

**Real-life applications:**
- Subsidizing early sellers to attract buyers.
- Running one side first, like listings, before opening to buyers.
- Focusing on a narrow city or niche to reach liquidity.
- Tracking GMV and take rate as core metrics.

**Key concepts:** `marketplace`, `two-sided`, `liquidity`, `GMV`, `take rate`

---

## Q50. What is a SaaS business?

**Level:** Basic

A SaaS (Software as a Service) business sells software access, usually via subscription, hosted on the vendor's cloud. Customers pay monthly or annually, and the vendor handles updates and infrastructure. SaaS offers recurring revenue and high gross margins when scaled.

**Real-life applications:**
- Launching a CRM priced at Rs 2,000 per user per month.
- Selling annual contracts for predictable revenue.
- Tracking MRR and ARR as core financial metrics.
- Investing in customer success to reduce churn.

**Key concepts:** `SaaS`, `subscription`, `ARR`, `MRR`, `retention`

---

## Q51. What is ARR?

**Level:** Basic

Annual Recurring Revenue (ARR) is the total value of subscription revenue on an annualized basis. It is the most-used SaaS metric because it shows the steady-state run rate of the business. ARR guides hiring, valuation, and fundraising.

**Real-life applications:**
- Reporting ARR growth in board meetings.
- Valuing a SaaS company at a multiple of ARR.
- Setting annual targets based on net ARR additions.
- Segmenting ARR by customer size or industry.

**Key concepts:** `ARR`, `MRR`, `recurring revenue`, `SaaS`, `valuation`

---

## Q52. What is MRR?

**Level:** Basic

Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR) is the total predictable subscription revenue in a given month. It includes new, expansion, contraction, and churn components. MRR is a leading indicator of where ARR is headed.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking new MRR from signups each week.
- Monitoring expansion MRR from upsells.
- Catching churn spikes in a specific segment early.
- Setting sales quotas in MRR units.

**Key concepts:** `MRR`, `ARR`, `recurring revenue`, `churn`, `expansion`

---

## Q53. What is freemium?

**Level:** Basic

Freemium is a pricing model where the basic version is free and advanced features require payment. It lowers the barrier to try the product. The challenge is converting enough free users to paid to make economics work.

**Real-life applications:**
- Offering a free tier with usage caps to drive upgrades.
- Tracking free-to-paid conversion rate weekly.
- Designing premium features that solve clear upgrade pain.
- Balancing support costs for free users against paid revenue.

**Key concepts:** `freemium`, `pricing`, `conversion`, `free tier`, `upgrade`

---

## Q54. What is a freemium-to-paid conversion rate?

**Level:** Basic

It is the percentage of free users who upgrade to a paid plan. Typical SaaS benchmarks range from 2% to 5%. Higher conversion rates usually mean the free-to-paid boundary is set well.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing different feature limits to lift conversion.
- Segmenting conversion by user behavior to target upgrade prompts.
- Setting quarterly goals around raising conversion from 2% to 3%.
- Evaluating whether freemium is worth the support cost.

**Key concepts:** `conversion`, `freemium`, `pricing`, `upgrade`, `experimentation`

---

## Q55. What is a value metric?

**Level:** Basic

A value metric is the unit a SaaS company charges against — seats, messages, GB of storage, transactions. A good value metric grows with customer success. Choosing the right one makes expansion revenue natural.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pricing a storage app per GB used.
- Charging a CRM per active user per month.
- Billing a messaging platform per thousand messages sent.
- Revising the value metric as customer needs change.

**Key concepts:** `value metric`, `pricing`, `expansion`, `alignment`, `SaaS`

---

## Q56. What is a subscription model?

**Level:** Basic

A subscription model charges customers a recurring fee — monthly, quarterly, or annually — for ongoing access. It offers predictable revenue for the business and smooth costs for the customer. It is the default model for SaaS and increasingly common in consumer services.

**Real-life applications:**
- Selling annual OTT plans for predictable cash flow.
- Offering grocery subscriptions for daily essentials.
- Bundling services into a single monthly fee.
- Running retention campaigns tied to renewal dates.

**Key concepts:** `subscription`, `recurring revenue`, `retention`, `pricing`, `LTV`

---

## Q57. What is a freemium vs free trial choice?

**Level:** Basic

Freemium offers a permanently free plan; a free trial gives full product access for a limited time. Free trial usually converts better for products where the full value appears only with deep use. Freemium is better when the free plan itself drives virality.

**Real-life applications:**
- Choosing a 14-day free trial for complex B2B software.
- Using freemium for consumer apps with network effects.
- Adding a trial within a freemium model for premium features.
- Running A/B tests between trial and freemium signups.

**Key concepts:** `freemium`, `free trial`, `conversion`, `pricing model`, `acquisition`

---

## Q58. What is customer success?

**Level:** Basic

Customer success is a function that proactively ensures customers get value from the product. It covers onboarding, training, check-ins, and expansion. Strong customer success reduces churn and drives renewals.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running kickoff calls with every new enterprise customer.
- Tracking health scores to spot at-risk accounts.
- Hosting quarterly business reviews with key clients.
- Escalating technical issues quickly to keep customers happy.

**Key concepts:** `customer success`, `onboarding`, `retention`, `renewal`, `expansion`

---

## Q59. What is onboarding?

**Level:** Basic

Onboarding is the process of guiding a new customer to first value — the moment they feel the product works for them. It combines UX, emails, tutorials, and sometimes human help. Good onboarding is the single biggest lever on activation and retention.

**Real-life applications:**
- Designing a 5-step first-run flow in a mobile app.
- Sending an email sequence over the first week after signup.
- Offering setup calls with mid-market customers.
- Measuring time-to-first-value and reducing it each quarter.

**Key concepts:** `onboarding`, `activation`, `UX`, `retention`, `time-to-value`

---

## Q60. What is time-to-value?

**Level:** Basic

Time-to-value is how long it takes a new user to experience the core benefit of the product after signup. Shorter time-to-value means higher activation and lower churn. It is often measured in minutes for consumer apps and days for enterprise SaaS.

**Real-life applications:**
- Reducing signup steps from five to two to cut time-to-value.
- Pre-populating example data so users see value immediately.
- Moving setup tasks to post-first-value instead of before.
- Tracking time-to-value by user segment.

**Key concepts:** `time-to-value`, `activation`, `onboarding`, `UX`, `retention`

---

## Q61. What is a cap table?

**Level:** Basic

A cap table is a spreadsheet that lists every shareholder, the class of shares they own, and the ownership percentage. It updates after every funding round, option grant, or share transfer. A clean cap table makes future fundraising much smoother.

**Real-life applications:**
- Modeling the impact of a new funding round.
- Assigning ESOP grants with proper vesting.
- Tracking founder and investor ownership over time.
- Sharing with lawyers before signing term sheets.

**Key concepts:** `cap table`, `equity`, `ownership`, `dilution`, `ESOP`

---

## Q62. What is a SAFE?

**Level:** Basic

A Simple Agreement for Future Equity (SAFE) is a convertible instrument invented by Y Combinator. It converts into equity at the next priced round, usually at a discount or valuation cap. SAFEs are faster and cheaper than traditional equity rounds for very early deals.

**Real-life applications:**
- Raising a small pre-seed round quickly with SAFEs.
- Comparing SAFE terms with convertible notes.
- Using valuation caps to reward early believers.
- Tracking SAFEs on the cap table as notional shares.

**Key concepts:** `SAFE`, `convertible`, `valuation cap`, `pre-seed`, `discount`

---

## Q63. What is a convertible note?

**Level:** Basic

A convertible note is a short-term loan that converts into equity at a later priced round, usually with a discount and valuation cap. It lets early investors fund without setting a valuation today. Notes carry interest and a maturity date, unlike SAFEs.

**Real-life applications:**
- Bridging a startup between seed and Series A.
- Accepting investment while valuation is still unclear.
- Setting a valuation cap to protect both sides.
- Converting multiple notes cleanly at the next round.

**Key concepts:** `convertible note`, `bridge`, `valuation cap`, `interest`, `conversion`

---

## Q64. What is a founder's agreement?

**Level:** Basic

A founder's agreement is a written contract between co-founders covering equity split, roles, vesting, decision rights, and exit clauses. It is best signed before product work begins. A clear agreement prevents the most common early-stage breakups.

**Real-life applications:**
- Defining responsibilities between technical and business co-founders.
- Setting a 4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff.
- Agreeing on who owns the IP.
- Writing a clause for what happens if a co-founder leaves.

**Key concepts:** `founder agreement`, `vesting`, `equity split`, `governance`, `IP`

---

## Q65. What is vesting?

**Level:** Basic

Vesting is the schedule by which a person earns the right to their shares or options over time. A typical startup schedule is 4 years with a 1-year cliff. Vesting prevents someone from leaving after three months with a big slice of equity.

**Real-life applications:**
- Granting ESOPs with a 4-year vesting to a new engineer.
- Using a 1-year cliff to filter out early departures.
- Accelerating vesting in acquisition deals.
- Clawing back unvested shares when a co-founder leaves.

**Key concepts:** `vesting`, `cliff`, `equity`, `ESOP`, `retention`

---

## Q66. What is an accelerator?

**Level:** Basic

An accelerator is a fixed-term, cohort-based program that provides seed funding, mentorship, and network access in exchange for equity. Examples include Y Combinator, Techstars, and Sequoia Surge. Programs usually run 3 to 6 months and end with a demo day.

**Real-life applications:**
- Applying to YC for $500k funding and mentorship.
- Using a demo day to line up follow-on investors.
- Leveraging the alumni network for hiring and customers.
- Speeding up early product iteration with expert advice.

**Key concepts:** `accelerator`, `cohort`, `demo day`, `seed`, `mentorship`

---

## Q67. What is an incubator?

**Level:** Basic

An incubator provides longer-term support to very early startups, often with office space, shared services, and light funding. Unlike accelerators, incubators are less cohort-driven and often nonprofit or university-run. They suit founders still shaping the idea.

**Real-life applications:**
- Joining a university incubator for lab access and legal help.
- Using shared office space to reduce early overhead.
- Accessing government grants via incubator partnerships.
- Getting guidance on IP and patent filings.

**Key concepts:** `incubator`, `early stage`, `support`, `office space`, `mentorship`

---

## Q68. What is the difference between an accelerator and an incubator?

**Level:** Basic

Accelerators are short, cohort-based, equity-taking programs for startups with a product already in hand; incubators support very early-stage ideas for longer periods with less structure. Accelerators push quickly toward demo day; incubators nurture over time. Founders choose based on stage and speed.

**Real-life applications:**
- Picking an incubator when still validating the idea.
- Moving to an accelerator after reaching MVP.
- Using incubators for deep-tech or research-heavy startups.
- Comparing equity terms between the two options.

**Key concepts:** `accelerator`, `incubator`, `cohort`, `stage`, `equity`

---

## Q69. What is a minimum marketable product?

**Level:** Basic

A Minimum Marketable Product (MMP) is the smallest product version a company will actually sell to customers, not just test with. Unlike an MVP focused on learning, the MMP is polished enough to justify its price. It sits between the MVP and the full product.

**Real-life applications:**
- Charging customers for the MMP to validate willingness to pay.
- Sequencing features into "MVP → MMP → scale product" phases.
- Using the MMP to generate first case studies.
- Balancing quality and speed in the go-to-market plan.

**Key concepts:** `MMP`, `MVP`, `launch`, `pricing`, `product strategy`

---

## Q70. What is first-mover advantage?

**Level:** Basic

First-mover advantage is the edge a company gains by being first into a new market — brand recognition, customer loyalty, and scale ahead of rivals. It is not guaranteed; followers often learn from early mistakes. The advantage is strongest in markets with network effects or high switching costs.

**Real-life applications:**
- Racing to capture a new geography before competitors arrive.
- Locking in key customers with long-term contracts.
- Building a brand synonymous with the category.
- Reinvesting early margins into a defensive moat.

**Key concepts:** `first mover`, `advantage`, `moat`, `switching costs`, `brand`

## 1. Jargon Buster

| Term | Plain-English Definition | Why It Matters in Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Startup | A temporary organization designed to search for a repeatable and scalable business model under conditions of extreme uncertainty. | Distinguishes it from an SME; a startup's job is discovery, not execution of a known model. |
| Entrepreneur | Someone who creates value by identifying a market gap and assembling resources (capital, talent, tech) to exploit it, bearing personal risk. | Mindset of ownership and iteration; critical for IT founders who must sell, build, and pivot simultaneously. |
| Business Model Canvas (BMC) | A one-page visual tool with 9 building blocks: Customer Segments, Value Proposition, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams, Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partners, Cost Structure. | Forces founders to see the whole system on one page before writing code or hiring. |
| MVP (Minimum Viable Product) | The smallest version of a product that lets you test a core hypothesis with real users using the least effort. | Saves months of wasted engineering; in AI products, an MVP can be a prompt-based prototype before any ML model is trained. |
| PMF (Product-Market Fit) | The degree to which a product satisfies strong market demand — signalled when retention curves flatten and users are genuinely disappointed if the product disappears. | The single most important milestone before scaling spend; premature scaling without PMF is the #1 startup killer. |
| CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost) | Total sales and marketing spend divided by the number of new customers acquired in the same period. | Determines whether a growth channel is economically viable; AI startups often undercount CAC by ignoring founder time. |
| LTV (Lifetime Value) | The total net revenue a business expects to earn from one customer over the entire relationship. | Paired with CAC to judge unit economics; SaaS LTV = ARPU × Gross Margin % ÷ Churn Rate. |
| Pivot | A structured course correction that keeps the company running while testing a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, segment, or business model. | Not the same as failure; Instagram pivoted from Burbn (check-ins) to photo sharing. Knowing when to pivot vs. persevere is a CEO's hardest call. |
| Bootstrapping | Growing a company using only internal cash flows and personal savings — no external equity investors. | Preserves equity and forces capital efficiency; many profitable SaaS/consulting firms are bootstrapped to Series A quality metrics. |
| Seed Round | The first formal institutional fundraise, typically $500K–$3M, used to prove product-market fit. Often uses SAFEs or convertible notes in IT/AI. | Sets dilution expectations; post-seed, investors expect a path to Series A metrics within 18 months. |
| Runway | The number of months a company can operate before running out of cash, given current burn rate. Runway = Cash Balance ÷ Monthly Net Burn. | Existential: a startup with 3 months of runway is in crisis mode; 18+ months allows deliberate strategy. |
| Burn Rate | The net amount of cash a company spends each month (gross burn = total spend; net burn = spend minus revenue). | Tracked weekly in early stage; high burn relative to growth signals poor capital efficiency. |
| Value Proposition | The specific bundle of benefits a product delivers to solve a customer's problem better than alternatives — expressed in plain language, not features. | In B2B SaaS/AI, a weak VP is the most common reason enterprise deals stall; it must address ROI, not just capability. |
| Churn Rate | The percentage of customers (or revenue) lost in a given period. Customer churn = (Lost Customers ÷ Customers at Start) × 100. | In SaaS, monthly churn above 2% makes growth nearly impossible; in AI tools, high churn signals the "wow" moment isn't repeating. |
| North-Star Metric | The single metric that best captures the core value your product delivers to customers — and that the whole team optimizes toward. | Aligns engineering, product, and growth around one number; avoids the trap of optimizing for revenue while user value declines. |
| TAM (Total Addressable Market) | The total revenue opportunity if a product captured 100% of the target market, with no competition. | Used in pitch decks to size the prize; investors in AI/ML expect TAM > $1B for VC-backable plays. |
| ICP (Ideal Customer Profile) | A detailed description of the type of company (B2B) or person (B2C) that gets the most value from your product and converts with least friction. | Narrows GTM focus; without ICP, sales cycles are long and churn is high because you sell to anyone who breathes. |
| PMF Signal | A quantitative or qualitative indicator that product-market fit is emerging — e.g., >40% of users saying "very disappointed" without the product, NPS > 50, or organic word-of-mouth referrals spiking. | Distinguishes real traction from polite feedback; critical before hiring a sales team or launching paid ads. |
| Burn Multiple | Net Burn ÷ Net New ARR. Measures how many dollars are burned to generate each dollar of new annual recurring revenue. | A burn multiple < 1.5 is efficient; > 2 signals the growth engine is expensive; used by Series A/B investors to judge capital efficiency. |
| Pivot Trigger | A pre-defined quantitative threshold (e.g., less than 30% retention at week 4, or fewer than 10 paying customers after 90 days) that signals the current hypothesis has failed and a structured pivot is warranted. | Removes emotion from the pivot decision; founders without pivot triggers pivot too late or too early. |
| Innovation Accounting | A system for measuring startup progress using leading indicators (activation rate, retention cohort shape, NPS) rather than lagging financial metrics, as proposed by Eric Ries in The Lean Startup. | Gives boards and investors honest signal during the search phase when revenue is zero or noise. |

## 3. Formulas, Thresholds & Rules of Thumb

### 3.1 CAC Formula

```
CAC = Total Sales & Marketing Spend (period)
      ─────────────────────────────────────
      Number of New Customers Acquired (same period)

Notes:
- Include: salaries (sales + marketing), ad spend, tools/software, agency fees, 
  event costs, SDR/BDR time, content production
- Exclude: customer success costs (post-acquisition) and product costs
- Blended CAC = all channels combined
- Channel CAC = spend per channel ÷ customers from that channel

IT/AI trap: Many AI startups forget to include founder selling time in early-stage CAC,
making CAC appear lower than it truly is at scale.
```

### 3.2 LTV Formulas

**SaaS / Subscription LTV:**
```
LTV = ARPU × Gross Margin %
      ──────────────────────
         Monthly Churn Rate

Where:
  ARPU = Average Revenue Per User per month
  Gross Margin % = (Revenue - COGS) / Revenue  [target > 70% for SaaS]
  Monthly Churn Rate = % customers lost per month

Example: ARPU = $100, GM = 75%, Monthly Churn = 2%
  LTV = ($100 × 0.75) / 0.02 = $75 / 0.02 = $3,750
```

**Transactional / E-commerce LTV:**
```
LTV = Average Order Value × Purchase Frequency × Average Customer Lifespan (years)
      × Gross Margin %

Example: AOV = $200, Frequency = 4x/year, Lifespan = 3 years, GM = 40%
  LTV = $200 × 4 × 3 × 0.40 = $960
```

**Simplified LTV (when churn is hard to measure):**
```
LTV ≈ ARPU / Churn Rate   [before applying gross margin]
```

### 3.3 LTV:CAC Ratio Benchmarks

```
LTV:CAC Ratio    |  Interpretation                          | Action
─────────────────|──────────────────────────────────────────|──────────────────────────────
< 1:1            |  Destroying value — losing money on each | Stop scaling; fix unit economics
                 |  customer even before overhead           | or shut down the channel
─────────────────|──────────────────────────────────────────|──────────────────────────────
1:1 – 2:1        |  Marginal — barely covering acquisition  | Optimize before scaling;
                 |  but not overhead, R&D, G&A              | reduce CAC or increase LTV
─────────────────|──────────────────────────────────────────|──────────────────────────────
3:1              |  Healthy — industry benchmark for Series | Safe to scale; Series A
                 |  A SaaS readiness                        | investors' minimum threshold
─────────────────|──────────────────────────────────────────|──────────────────────────────
4:1 – 5:1        |  Strong — room to invest in growth       | Accelerate paid channels;
                 |  while maintaining profitability path    | consider international expansion
─────────────────|──────────────────────────────────────────|──────────────────────────────
> 5:1            |  Exceptional / possibly under-investing  | May be growing too slowly;
                 |  in growth channels                      | increase marketing spend
```

### 3.4 CAC Payback Period

```
CAC Payback Period = CAC ÷ (ARPU × Gross Margin %)

Target benchmarks:
  ≤ 6 months:   Best-in-class SaaS (enterprise or PLG with high ARPU)
  ≤ 12 months:  Healthy SaaS benchmark for Series A readiness
  12–18 months: Acceptable if NRR > 120% (expansion revenue compensates)
  > 18 months:  Requires large capital base; risky for early-stage startups
  > 24 months:  Red flag for most VC investors; business under stress

Implication: The longer the payback, the more capital you need to fund growth
— making the startup dependent on external financing and sensitive to market downturns.
```

### 3.5 Burn Multiple

```
Burn Multiple = Net Cash Burned (period) ÷ Net New ARR Added (same period)

Benchmarks:
  < 1x:    Outstanding — burning less than you're growing
  1x–1.5x: Good — efficient growth engine
  1.5x–2x: Acceptable early-stage with clear efficiency path
  2x–3x:   Concerning — investigate channel efficiency
  > 3x:    Inefficient — must cut burn or dramatically accelerate growth

Burn Multiple is preferred over burn rate alone because it normalizes spend
against growth: a $500K/month burn is fine if you're adding $600K MRR,
but catastrophic if you're adding $50K MRR.
```

### 3.6 Runway Formula

```
Runway (months) = Cash Balance ÷ Net Monthly Burn Rate

Net Monthly Burn = Gross Monthly Expenses - Monthly Revenue Collected

Rules of thumb:
  - Raise when you have 9–12 months of runway remaining (6 months to close + buffer)
  - Default alive threshold: Month where cumulative revenue ≥ cumulative expenses
  - Maintain 18+ months post-raise runway for strategic flexibility
  - Extend runway by: cutting headcount (50–70% of most startup costs),
    improving NRR, switching to annual contracts, or reducing infrastructure costs
```

### 3.7 Viral Coefficient (k-factor)

```
k = (Average Invites Sent per User) × (Conversion Rate of Invites to Sign-ups)

Interpretation:
  k > 1.0:  Viral — each user brings more than one new user; exponential growth
  k = 1.0:  Neutral — growth is linear, not exponential
  k < 1.0:  Sub-viral — word-of-mouth aids but doesn't drive primary growth
  k = 0.3:  Each 10 users bring 3 new users (still valuable as supplement)

In AI tools (e.g., Figma, Notion, Loom): k is driven by output sharing —
the product's artifact (design, doc, video) becomes the distribution vehicle.
Product-led viral loops are more defensible than paid acquisition.
```

### 3.8 Freemium Conversion Rate Benchmark

```
Free-to-Paid Conversion Rate = Paid Users ÷ Total Free Users × 100

Industry benchmarks:
  2–5%:   Standard SaaS freemium conversion (acceptable)
  5–10%:  Strong product-led growth signal
  > 10%:  Exceptional; re-examine whether freemium tier is too restrictive
  < 1%:   Freemium tier likely too generous; users get full value for free

Key levers to improve freemium conversion:
  1. Trigger conversion at the moment of maximum perceived value
  2. Gate collaboration/team features (not core solo features)
  3. Use usage-based limits that align with value metric growth
  4. Time-limit advanced features during onboarding (free trial within freemium)
```

### 3.9 NPS Thresholds

```
Net Promoter Score = % Promoters (9–10) - % Detractors (0–6)

Ranges:
  > 70:   World-class (Apple, Tesla level)
  50–70:  Excellent — strong referral engine; scale aggressively
  30–50:  Good — acceptable for B2B SaaS; focus on detractor root causes
  0–30:   Mediocre — product or support gaps need addressing
  < 0:    Crisis — word-of-mouth is negative; retention at risk

B2B SaaS benchmark: NPS > 30 is baseline; > 50 is a PMF signal.
In consulting platforms, NPS correlates strongly with contract renewal rates.
Run NPS at: Day 30 activation milestone, quarterly for retained customers,
and post-onboarding to measure time-to-value satisfaction.
```

## 4. Do / Don't

### DO

1. **Do talk to customers before writing code.** Run 20+ unscripted problem interviews using the Mom Test framework before building anything. In AI startups, the biggest waste is training a model on the wrong problem.

2. **Do define your ICP before scaling GTM.** Identify the single profile of customer who gets maximum value, converts fastest, and churns least. Build your first 10 case studies exclusively from ICP customers.

3. **Do set a pivot trigger before you start.** Define in advance the measurable threshold (e.g., < 30% D30 retention) that will signal the current hypothesis has failed. Remove emotion from the decision.

4. **Do measure retention before acquisition.** Fix the leaky bucket before pouring more water in. In SaaS, even a 1% improvement in monthly churn has a larger LTV impact than doubling your sign-up rate.

5. **Do use SAFE notes for early fundraising.** SAFEs are founder-friendly, fast to close, have no interest or maturity date, and are the standard instrument for pre-seed and seed rounds in the US startup ecosystem.

6. **Do track actionable metrics over vanity metrics.** Monthly Active Users and page views feel good but don't tell you if the business is healthy. Track retention cohort shape, activation rate, and NRR instead.

7. **Do price on value, not cost.** SaaS and AI products have near-zero marginal cost of delivery — pricing based on customer ROI or outcomes (value metric pricing) captures far more revenue than cost-plus.

8. **Do get a signed founder agreement and vesting schedule on Day 1.** Standard 4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff protects all co-founders from an early departure leaving someone with unearned equity. No investor will fund a team without it.

9. **Do design the onboarding flow to deliver value in the first session.** Time-to-value (TTV) is the single biggest driver of activation and Day 7 retention. Every extra click before the aha moment costs you users.

10. **Do run cohort analysis, not aggregate averages.** Average retention hides the fact that your early cohorts churn faster than recent ones (or vice versa). Cohort curves tell the true health story.

---

### DON'T

1. **Don't scale before PMF.** The most expensive mistake in startups is hiring a 20-person sales team before the product is proven. You will burn runway and entrench a broken business model.

2. **Don't confuse activity with traction.** Press mentions, Twitter followers, and pitch competition wins are vanity metrics. They feel like progress but don't validate that customers will pay repeatedly.

3. **Don't build in stealth for more than 3 months.** Extended stealth allows you to optimize for a problem that has already been solved, or a customer who doesn't exist. Ship early, ship ugly, learn fast.

4. **Don't pitch TAM without SAM and SOM.** Claiming a "$500B market" with no serviceable segment analysis signals naivety to investors. Show TAM, SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market), and SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market) with bottom-up logic.

5. **Don't ignore churn in the pursuit of growth.** A 5% monthly churn means you replace your entire customer base in 20 months. No growth rate can outrun high churn long-term.

6. **Don't give away equity to advisors casually.** Advisor equity of 0.1–0.25% is standard for meaningful introductions and ongoing help. Avoid giving 1%+ to advisors who only attend monthly calls.

7. **Don't let the cap table become messy early.** Too many investors at seed (> 15 angels) creates coordination problems at Series A. Use a SAFE with a lead investor structure or consolidate via a rolling fund.

8. **Don't optimize for MRR growth while NRR is below 80%.** If you're losing more revenue from churn than you're adding from new sales, your headline growth is a mirage. Investors will find this in due diligence.

9. **Don't skip competitive landscape analysis.** "We have no competition" is a red flag: it means either the market doesn't exist, or the founders haven't looked hard enough. Every product competes with the status quo at minimum.

10. **Don't set your freemium tier so generous that users never need to upgrade.** The free plan must create enough value to attract users and enough friction (usage limits, team features, advanced analytics) to drive upgrade.

## 5. Metric-Driven Scenarios with Anti-Examples

### Scenario A: SaaS Startup Measuring PMF with the Sean Ellis Test

**Context:** A B2B project management SaaS for IT consulting firms, 6 months post-launch, 120 active users, $8K MRR.

**Trigger:**  
The founding team is debating whether to start scaling marketing spend. The CMO wants to launch a Google Ads campaign. The CPO argues they need to confirm PMF first. They decide to run the Sean Ellis survey (the "40% Test"): "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product? (a) Very disappointed, (b) Somewhat disappointed, (c) Not disappointed."

**Analysis:**  
Survey sent to 80 users who have used the product at least twice in the last 30 days (the correct filter — inactive users would dilute results). Results:
- Very disappointed: 31 users = 38.75%
- Somewhat disappointed: 28 users = 35%
- Not disappointed: 21 users = 26.25%

Result: 38.75% — just below the 40% PMF threshold. The team also checks:
- D30 retention: 41% (below the 50%+ benchmark for healthy B2B SaaS)
- NPS: 34 (good but not excellent)
- Organic referrals: 3 new sign-ups in last month from referrals (low)

**Decision:**  
Do NOT scale paid acquisition. Instead, focus the next sprint on: (1) interviewing the 31 "very disappointed" users to find the common activation pattern and double down on it, (2) redesigning onboarding to replicate the activation journey of top users, (3) running the Sean Ellis test again in 60 days.

**Result:**  
After onboarding redesign, D30 retention rises to 54%, Sean Ellis score reaches 47%, and organic referrals double. The team greenlit a $15K/month paid acquisition test with a target CAC under $600 and 12-month payback. Within 3 months, MRR doubles to $16K.

**Anti-Example:**  
The CMO overrules the CPO and launches ads immediately. CAC comes in at $850. Onboarding is still broken, so new users churn at 55% in the first 30 days. MRR grows to $12K but the business burns $45K in one quarter on acquisition. By Month 9, burn multiple is 4.2x. Investors pass on the Series A due to poor unit economics, and the startup has to do a down round.

---

### Scenario B: AI Tool Startup Misusing Vanity Metrics

**Context:** An AI writing assistant startup for marketing teams. Launched 4 months ago. The team celebrates 50,000 registered users and 2M AI-generated documents in the dashboard.

**Trigger:**  
The CEO uses these metrics in a board meeting to argue for a $2M Series A raise at a $10M valuation. One board advisor (ex-SaaS operator) asks: "What is your D30 retention? What percentage of those 50,000 users have returned in the last 30 days?"

**Analysis:**  
The team pulls the data. Findings:
- D1 retention: 62% (good — users activated)
- D7 retention: 28% (moderate)
- D30 retention: 9% (critical — 91% of users have not returned by Day 30)
- MRR: $3,200 (only 160 paid users at $20/month)
- Free-to-paid conversion: 0.32% (below the 2% freemium floor)
- Documents generated: Most by < 200 power users creating thousands of docs each

The 2M documents figure is a single-cohort vanity metric. The 50,000 sign-ups is an acquisition metric, not a retention metric. The business has an acquisition engine but no retention engine.

**Decision:**  
Postpone the raise. Reassign two engineers to: (1) build a retention dashboard tracking D1/D7/D30 cohort curves, (2) conduct 25 user exit interviews with churned users, (3) redesign the product's re-engagement loop (weekly email digest of AI outputs, team collaboration features to increase stickiness).

**Result:**  
Exit interviews reveal users don't have a repeating use case — they try the tool for one campaign, then forget it. The team introduces a "content calendar" feature that creates a recurring weekly workflow. D30 retention climbs from 9% to 31% over 3 months. They revisit the raise with honest metrics and close a $1.5M pre-seed SAFE with a $6M cap — lower valuation, but on solid ground.

**Anti-Example:**  
The CEO ignores the advisor's pushback, raises the $2M at a $10M valuation citing "explosive user growth," and hires 8 people. Three months later, D30 retention is still 9%, paid conversion is 0.4%, and the burn multiple is 11x. They run out of runway in 14 months, are unable to raise a Series A, and shut down — a case study in vanity-metric-driven destruction.

### Scenario C: Consulting Platform Testing Freemium vs. Paid-Only Model

**Context:** A platform connecting independent IT consultants with mid-market companies. 8 months old. Currently charging companies $299/month for unlimited consultant discovery and outreach. Low top-of-funnel: 40 paying companies, $12K MRR.

**Trigger:**  
Top-of-funnel is the bottleneck — companies don't trust the platform before experiencing it. The team hypothesizes that a freemium tier (3 consultant searches/month for free) will drive sign-ups and then convert to paid. They design a 60-day experiment.

**Analysis:**  
Experiment design:
- Control: Paid-only ($299/month), current state
- Treatment: Freemium (3 searches free, unlimited at $299/month)
- Success metric: Paid conversion rate of freemium users within 60 days
- Secondary metric: CAC for freemium-converted customers vs. paid-only CAC

Results after 60 days:
- Freemium sign-ups: 280 companies
- Converted to paid: 17 companies (6.1% conversion — above the 2–5% benchmark)
- CAC freemium channel: $140 vs. paid-only CAC: $620
- Net new MRR from freemium converts: $5,083 (17 × $299)
- Cannibalization check: Zero existing paid customers downgraded to freemium

**Decision:**  
Adopt freemium permanently. Invest in onboarding automation triggered at Search #2 (when users see value) to accelerate conversion. Introduce annual plan ($2,490/year = 30% discount) to improve cash flow and reduce churn. Set 6-month review to ensure freemium tier doesn't become too generous.

**Result:**  
MRR grows from $12K to $28K within 4 months of full freemium rollout. CAC drops 55%. The 60-day cohort analysis confirms freemium customers churn at the same rate as original paid customers, confirming quality is not degraded. The platform closes a $750K angel round at a $4.5M SAFE cap.

**Anti-Example:**  
The team skips the 60-day experiment and makes freemium permanent immediately without measuring conversion. 500 companies join the freemium tier but only 8 convert (1.6% below benchmark). The free tier attracts price-sensitive companies who were never going to pay. The team spends 3 months optimizing freemium conversion before realizing they attracted the wrong ICP. They pivot back to paid-only but have damaged brand positioning with "budget" consultants. MRR declines to $9K due to churn from the confusion.

## 6. Practitioner Playbook

The following 12-step playbook covers the end-to-end journey from raw idea to a fundable seed round in the IT/AI/Product/Consulting sector.

**Step 1: Nail the Problem Statement (Weeks 1–2)**  
Write a 1-paragraph problem statement that specifies: Who suffers from the problem? What is the current workaround? What does the problem cost them (time, money, opportunity)? In AI startups, the problem must be painful enough that users accept imperfect AI output — "good enough AI" beats "perfect manual process." Do not mention your solution at this stage.

**Step 2: Validate the Problem with 20+ Customer Interviews (Weeks 2–6)**  
Use the Mom Test protocol: ask about past behaviour, not future intent. "Tell me about the last time you had to do [problem area]. What did you use? What was frustrating?" Talk to at least 20 target customers. Look for pattern: 3 or more interviewees unprompted mentioning the same pain is a signal. Record quotes verbatim — these become your marketing copy and investor proof points.

**Step 3: Define Your ICP and Segment (Week 6–7)**  
Based on interview data, define the ICP: company size, industry, role/title, current toolset, trigger event (what causes them to seek a solution now), and willingness to pay signal. In B2B IT/SaaS, the ICP should be narrow enough that a junior SDR can identify 1,000 exact-match companies using Apollo or LinkedIn Sales Navigator.

**Step 4: Draft the Business Model Canvas (Week 7)**  
Fill all 9 blocks. Focus on the Revenue Streams and Cost Structure blocks — this is where founders lie to themselves. In AI products, cost structure must include compute/GPU costs at scale, not just current development costs. Validate the revenue stream with at least one letter of intent or verbal commitment from an interviewee.

**Step 5: Build and Test a Landing Page (Weeks 7–9)**  
Create a one-page website with: a value proposition headline (outcome, not feature), 3 benefit bullets, social proof (quotes from interviews or advisors), and a CTA (email waitlist or "Book a Demo" button). Drive 500+ targeted visitors via LinkedIn content, cold email, or Reddit posts in relevant communities. Measure: email conversion rate (> 5% is signal), demo request rate, and bounce rate.

**Step 6: Ship the MVP in 4–6 Weeks (Weeks 9–15)**  
Define the single riskiest assumption your business rests on and build only what is needed to test that assumption. For an AI tool, the MVP can be a GPT-4 wrapper with a clean UX. For a consulting marketplace, it can be a spreadsheet + Calendly + Stripe. Measure activation: does the user reach the "aha moment" in the first session? If not, the MVP has failed its primary job.

**Step 7: Run a Closed Beta with 20–50 Users (Weeks 15–20)**  
Hand-pick beta users who match your ICP exactly. Do not optimize for volume — optimize for learning. Conduct weekly feedback calls. Track: activation rate, D7 retention, NPS, and the 3 most common feature requests. Look for users who use the product without prompting — these are your future champions.

**Step 8: Measure PMF Signals (Weeks 20–26)**  
Run the Sean Ellis 40% test. Track D30 retention (target > 40% for B2B SaaS). Calculate early LTV:CAC from beta pricing. Look for organic referrals — unprompted word-of-mouth is the strongest PMF signal. Define your north-star metric and verify that the team agrees it captures core user value delivery.

**Step 9: Set Pricing and Validate Willingness to Pay (Weeks 20–26)**  
Use the Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter: ask beta users 4 price questions (too cheap, cheap, expensive, too expensive) to find the acceptable range. In SaaS, aim for pricing at 10x the CAC you project at scale. Test annual vs. monthly plans — annual contracts improve cash flow and reduce churn risk dramatically. For AI tools with usage-based costs, identify the value metric (per seat, per output, per API call) that scales with customer value.

**Step 10: Build the First Sales Motion (Weeks 26–34)**  
Document the sales playbook from your beta wins: entry point (title/role), outreach message that worked, demo flow, objections and responses, and close sequence. Create 3–5 case studies from beta customers with quantified outcomes ("Saved 8 hours/week per consultant"). Hire one salesperson only after you (the founder) have closed 10 paying customers — this proves the motion is replicable.

**Step 11: Prepare the Seed Fundraise (Weeks 34–40)**  
Build the pitch deck (12 slides: Problem, Solution, Market Size, Product Demo, Traction, Business Model, GTM, Team, Competition, Financials, Ask, Appendix). Prepare a 3-year financial model with bottom-up MRR build, headcount plan, and 18-month runway post-funding. Target the raise when you have: $10K–$50K MRR, clear ICP, one repeatable sales motion, PMF signals documented, and at least 12 months of runway remaining. Use SAFEs with a valuation cap; avoid priced rounds at seed unless a strong lead investor insists.

**Step 12: Close the Seed Round and Operationalize (Weeks 40–52)**  
Close 3–5 investors maximum to keep the cap table clean. Prioritize investors who add network and domain expertise over pure capital. Post-raise: set up a proper data room (financials, contracts, IP assignments, cap table), implement a monthly investor update cadence (MRR, burn, runway, top wins, top risks, asks), hire only against the headcount plan, and run quarterly OKRs to stay aligned between the team and the board.

## 7. Content Critique & Depth Gaps

The Q&A file (Q1–Q70) covers foundational startup concepts adequately for an introductory MBA elective or undergraduate entrepreneurship course. However, for IIM/HBS MBA-level rigor, the following depth gaps represent significant analytical blind spots.

**Gap 1: Missing Causal Reasoning in Metric Chains**  
The Q&A defines metrics (CAC, LTV, churn, NPS) in isolation but does not establish causal models between them. At MBA depth, one must understand: Does improving activation rate causally reduce churn, or is it correlated because both are driven by ICP quality? The file presents no directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) or causal frameworks for metric intervention. For example, is low D30 retention caused by poor onboarding (fixable with UX), wrong ICP (requires repositioning), or product-market mismatch (requires pivot)? These have entirely different remedies. The Q&A's treatment of metrics as independent variables limits practitioner utility.

**Gap 2: No Failure Taxonomy or Post-Mortem Framework**  
The Q&A discusses pivots and churn but provides no systematic taxonomy of startup failure modes. CB Insights data consistently shows the top reasons startups fail: no market need (35%), ran out of cash (38%), wrong team (14%), out-competed (20%), pricing/cost issues (18%). An MBA-level treatment must cover: How do you distinguish "wrong market" from "wrong timing" from "wrong distribution"? What is the difference between a recoverable failure (fixable with a pivot) and a terminal failure (structural market absence)? The absence of a failure taxonomy means founders cannot use this material to diagnose their own situation.

**Gap 3: No Competitive Dynamics or Game Theory**  
The Q&A mentions competitive positioning superficially but includes no frameworks for competitive response modeling. At HBS depth, a startup strategy course must cover: First-mover vs. fast-follower trade-offs with sector-specific evidence (first-mover advantage is strong in network-effect businesses like Uber, weak in commodity SaaS where execution matters more than timing). Porter's Five Forces applied to SaaS/AI markets. Competitive moats: data moat (AI), switching costs (SaaS), network effects (marketplace), brand (consumer), regulatory (fintech). The Q&A mentions "first-mover advantage" in Q70 but treats it as a binary concept rather than a context-dependent strategic variable.

**Gap 4: No Financial Modeling Depth or Scenario Analysis**  
The Q&A lists formulas but provides no guidance on how to build a 3-statement financial model, stress-test assumptions, or run scenario analysis. At MBA level, founders must be able to model: Best/base/worst case MRR growth scenarios with explicit assumptions. The impact of increasing churn by 1% on LTV and fundraising timing. Break-even analysis under different pricing models (per-seat vs. usage-based). The Q&A's treatment of financials is definitional (what is runway?) rather than analytical (how do you model the decision between raising now vs. extending runway by cutting headcount?). This gap is particularly damaging for IT founders facing capital allocation decisions.

**Gap 5: Limited International and Emerging Market Context**  
All examples and benchmarks in the Q&A implicitly assume a US-centric startup ecosystem: SAFE notes (not used in most of Europe or India), Y Combinator benchmarks, US VC firm expectations. At IIM depth, the content must address: How do CAC benchmarks differ for Indian vs. US B2B SaaS (India SMB CAC can be 10x lower but LTV is also proportionally lower)? How do funding structures differ in EU (where Convertible Loans replace SAFEs)? How do regulatory environments (GDPR in Europe, data localization in India) affect the go-to-market for AI startups? The Q&A's framework is not portable to international markets without significant adaptation.

**Gap 6: No Equity Structure Nuance or Dilution Modeling**  
The Q&A defines ESOP, SAFE, convertible notes, and term sheets at a definitional level but does not cover: Pro-rata rights and their impact on founder dilution over multiple rounds. How a SAFE with a $5M cap and 20% discount converts in a priced round at different valuations (above cap, at cap, below cap). Anti-dilution provisions (broad-based weighted average vs. ratchet) and when they are triggered. The difference between pre-money and post-money SAFE (YC switched to post-money in 2018 — most founders don't understand the dilution difference). At HBS level, founders must be able to model a full cap table through 3 rounds and understand exactly what percentage they will own at IPO under different scenarios. This gap means founders walk into term sheet negotiations blind.

## 8. Quick-Recall Card

Core concepts every IT/AI/Product/Consulting practitioner must recall without notes:

- **Startup definition:** Temporary organization searching for a repeatable, scalable business model under uncertainty — not an SME executing a known model.
- **MVP purpose:** Test the single riskiest assumption with minimum effort — not ship the smallest product; the minimum is defined by learning, not features.
- **PMF signal:** > 40% of users "very disappointed" without the product (Sean Ellis test) + flat retention curve + organic referrals appearing without incentives.
- **North-star metric:** The one metric that captures core value delivered to users — everything else is either an input metric (leading) or a lag metric (trailing).
- **Unit economics health check:** LTV:CAC > 3:1, CAC payback < 12 months, gross margin > 70% (SaaS), net revenue retention > 100%.
- **Burn multiple:** Net burn ÷ net new ARR — the capital efficiency ratio; < 1.5x is healthy, > 3x is a red flag requiring immediate action.
- **Churn as the foundation:** Fix churn before scaling acquisition; 2% monthly churn = 22% annual churn = replacing a fifth of your customers every year.
- **Pivot trigger:** Pre-defined quantitative threshold set before the experiment starts — removes emotion and survivorship bias from the pivot decision.
- **Freemium economics:** 2–5% free-to-paid conversion is standard; gate collaboration and advanced analytics, not the core value; measure TTV relentlessly.
- **Funding ladder:** Bootstrapping → Friends & Family → Angel/SAFE → Seed (priced) → Series A — each stage requires a milestone gate, not just elapsed time.
- **Vanity vs. actionable metrics:** Registered users and page views are vanity; D30 retention, NPS, and ARR growth rate are actionable — they drive decisions.
- **ICP discipline:** The narrower your ICP, the faster you close, the lower your churn, and the more referral density you generate in a community.
- **Cap table hygiene:** Keep seed investors to < 10; use SAFEs with a lead to avoid coordination failure at Series A; model dilution through 3 rounds before signing.
- **Innovation accounting:** Measure progress with leading indicators (activation, retention cohort shape) during search phase — revenue is a lagging indicator.
- **Runway management:** Raise when you have 9–12 months remaining; default-alive analysis tells you whether you can grow to cash-flow positive without raising.
