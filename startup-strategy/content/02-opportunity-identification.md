# Opportunity Identification

## Overview

Opportunity identification is the process of spotting a problem worth solving and a group of people willing to pay for the solution. It is not about chasing trends — it is about finding a gap between what customers want and what the market delivers today. The best opportunities are specific, urgent, and reachable with the resources you have.

---

## Why It Matters

Most startups fail because they build something nobody needs. A disciplined search for real problems — not clever ideas — saves years of wasted effort and capital. Managers who can frame an opportunity clearly attract better talent, sharper investors, and faster first customers.

## Key Principles

- Start with an unmet need, not a technology or a product you like.
- Look at gaps, frustrations, workarounds, and costly manual processes.
- Check that the pain is frequent, expensive, and hard to ignore.
- Size the customer group before falling in love with the idea.
- Validate by talking to real users, not by reading reports.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Problem-solution fit** | Evidence that a real problem exists and your solution addresses it. |
| **Total Addressable Market (TAM)** | The full revenue opportunity if you served every possible customer. |
| **Pain point** | A specific, repeatable frustration a customer will pay to remove. |
| **Unfair advantage** | Something you uniquely have — skill, access, data — that competitors cannot copy quickly. |

## Use Case

A software engineer notices her parents struggle to book hospital appointments. She interviews 25 senior citizens, finds the same pattern, and designs a phone-call-based booking service instead of another app.

He rode along with three salesmen, confirmed the pattern, and launched a direct-to-store ordering service. ## Jargon Buster

| Term | Plain-English Definition | Why It Matters in Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Problem-Solution Fit | The confirmation that a specific solution meaningfully resolves a specific, validated pain — not just in theory, but as experienced by actual users | Without this, every subsequent investment — engineering, marketing, hiring — is built on sand. IT/AI products routinely ship elegant solutions to problems nobody urgently has. |
| Total Addressable Market (TAM) | The total annual revenue opportunity if your solution captured 100% of the eligible customers in the target segment, globally | TAM tells you whether the ceiling is worth chasing. A consulting firm pitching an AI audit tool must confirm TAM is large enough to justify partner attention and platform investment. |
| Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM) | The slice of TAM you can realistically reach given your current distribution channels, geography, and product scope | SAM grounds the business case. A product team in Bengaluru cannot realistically serve the entire global compliance market on day one; SAM defines the achievable footprint. |
| Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) | The realistic share of SAM you can capture in a defined horizon (typically 3–5 years), accounting for competitive intensity and execution capacity | SOM is what investors actually stress-test. It forces the team to defend win rates, sales cycles, and churn assumptions rather than waving at a large TAM. |
| Pain Point | A recurring friction, inefficiency, or cost — financial, emotional, or time-based — that a customer experiences and actively tries to work around | Frequency and severity together determine whether a pain point can anchor a business. An occasional minor inconvenience is not a viable pain point; a weekly, costly, embarrassing failure is. |
| Unfair Advantage | A resource, insight, relationship, or capability that competitors cannot easily replicate — the moat that makes your solution defensible | In AI/product consulting, unfair advantages include proprietary datasets, deep domain expertise, exclusive partnerships, or a distribution channel a competitor must build from scratch. |
| Jobs-to-Be-Done (JTBD) | A framework that reframes customer behavior as hiring a product to accomplish a functional, social, or emotional job, rather than buying a feature set | JTBD prevents feature creep. A compliance officer is not buying "an AI dashboard"; she is hiring a tool to confidently answer regulators in under two hours. The job defines the spec. |
| Opportunity Sizing | The structured process of estimating the number of addressable customers, their willingness to pay, and the resulting revenue potential before committing resources | Sizing prevents the common trap of building for a segment too small to sustain a business, or too diffuse to reach efficiently. It converts intuition into a falsifiable forecast. |
| Workaround | A manual, improvised, or off-label process a customer uses because no good solution exists — the digital equivalent of duct tape | Workarounds are treasure maps. If a team of 50 analysts exports data to Excel every Friday to reconcile two systems, that spreadsheet ritual is a product brief waiting to be written. |
| Beachhead Market | The narrow, specific, high-pain customer segment you target first to establish credibility, cash flow, and learnings before expanding | Startups and new consulting practices that chase the full market at launch spread resources fatally thin. Dominating a beachhead first creates references, pricing power, and a repeatable playbook. |
| Signal vs. Noise | The discipline of distinguishing customer feedback that reveals genuine, recurring problems (signal) from one-off complaints, feature requests, or polite encouragement (noise) | Without this skill, a product team builds a roadmap driven by the loudest customer, not the most representative one. Structured interview techniques separate the two. |
| Demand Validation | Concrete proof — pre-sales, letters of intent, pilot commitments, or paid prototypes — that customers will exchange money for the proposed solution before it is fully built | Demand validation is the difference between a hypothesis and a business. An AI consulting team that secures three signed pilots before writing a line of code has validated demand; one that builds first and hopes has not. |

## Frameworks & Mental Models

### 1. The Pain-Frequency-Willingness Matrix

Every candidate opportunity should be scored on three axes before committing resources: how severe the pain is, how often it occurs, and how much the customer is already paying (in time, money, or workarounds) to manage it. High scores on all three axes identify the opportunities worth pursuing.

```
                    HIGH FREQUENCY
                          |
  NICHE NUISANCE          |          SWEET SPOT
  (Severe but rare)       |       (Severe + frequent)
  Build only if WTP       |       Build fast; this is
  is extremely high       |       where companies are made
                          |
LOW SEVERITY -------------|------------- HIGH SEVERITY
                          |
  IGNORE                  |          CHRONIC BUT CHEAP
  (Low pain, low freq)    |       (Frequent but low WTP)
                          |       Automate at low cost or
                          |       pivot pricing model
                          |
                    LOW FREQUENCY

Axes: X = Pain Severity (customer cost in $, time, reputation)
      Y = Frequency (daily/weekly = high; annual = low)
      WTP = Willingness to Pay
```

Target zone: Top-right quadrant. For IT/AI products, the sweet spot is typically a process that runs weekly or more, causes measurable financial or compliance risk, and currently consumes skilled human hours.

### 2. The Jobs-to-Be-Done Demand Mapping Tree

JTBD maps the hierarchy from the high-level job the customer is trying to accomplish, down to the discrete sub-jobs, down to the specific pain that blocks execution. This prevents building for the wrong level of abstraction.

```

         |
         +---> SUB-JOB A: Collect data from 12 internal systems
         |          |

         |
         +---> SUB-JOB B: Flag anomalies before sign-off

                               CURRENT WORKAROUND: Junior analyst + 8 hours

OPPORTUNITY SIGNAL: Any sub-job where the workaround
costs > 10% of the value delivered is worth prototyping.
```

## Formulas, Thresholds & Rules of Thumb

### Market Sizing Formulas

**Bottom-Up TAM**
```
TAM = (Number of target customers) x (Annual spend per customer on the problem)

Example:
  Target: 8,000 mid-market Indian financial firms
  Annual spend on manual compliance processes: ~$40,000/firm
  TAM = 8,000 x $40,000 = $320M
```

**Top-Down TAM Sanity Check**
```
TAM (top-down) = Industry analyst market size x Relevant segment share

Example:
  Global compliance tech market: $48B
  India + SEA mid-market share: ~7%
  TAM = $48B x 0.07 = $3.36B  (compare with bottom-up; reconcile gap)
```

**Opportunity Score (Ulwick's Formula)**
```
Opportunity Score = Importance + MAX(Importance - Satisfaction, 0)

Where:
  Importance = Average customer rating of how important the job/outcome is (0-10)
  Satisfaction = Average customer rating of how satisfied they are with current solutions (0-10)

Score > 15: Strong opportunity — act now
Score 10-15: Moderate opportunity — validate further
Score < 10: Weak opportunity — deprioritize

Example (AI audit trail product):
  Importance of "instant audit-ready documentation" = 8.5
  Satisfaction with current solution = 3.0
  Score = 8.5 + MAX(8.5 - 3.0, 0) = 8.5 + 5.5 = 14.0  → Validate further
```

### Thresholds & Rules of Thumb

| Rule | Threshold | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency threshold | Pain occurs >= weekly | Below weekly, urgency rarely justifies a SaaS subscription |
| Interview minimum | Talk to >= 20 people in ICP before conviction | Fewer than 20 creates selection bias; patterns need repetition |
| WTP proxy | Customer currently spends >= 10x your planned price on workarounds | If your tool costs $500/mo and workaround costs $200/mo, urgency is low |
| Beachhead size rule | Beachhead SOM should be winnable with < 18 months and < $2M burn | Larger = too diffuse; smaller = insufficient reference base |
| Signal confirmation | >= 3 unprompted, independent users describe pain in same language | Same words without prompting = genuine, shared mental model of the problem |
| Pilot conversion | >= 40% of discovery calls convert to paid pilot | Below 40% signals messaging mismatch or pain severity overestimation |
| NPS as signal filter | Interview customers with NPS <= 6 on current solutions first | Detractors of existing tools are the highest-signal interview subjects |

## Do / Don't

### Do

1. **Start with observed behavior, not stated preferences.** Watch what people actually do — the spreadsheets they build, the Slack hacks they use, the reports they ignore — before asking them what they want.
2. **Interview across the buying committee.** In B2B IT/AI, the user, the buyer, the champion, and the blocker are often four different people. Map pain at each level.
3. **Quantify the pain in the customer's own currency.** "It takes our team 14 hours a week" is more actionable and persuasive than "it is frustrating."
4. **Use the five-whys discipline.** Surface-level pain (slow reporting) almost always has a deeper root cause (data locked in legacy ERP). Solve the root, not the symptom.
5. **Validate demand with money or commitment, not enthusiasm.** A signed letter of intent, a paid pilot, or a deposit is signal. "This sounds great, keep me posted" is noise.
6. **Size your beachhead before your total market.** Investors and boards respect a founder who can say "we will own 30% of the 400-firm BFSI compliance segment in India in 24 months" over one who cites a $10B global market.
7. **Document your invalidated hypotheses.** Knowing what you ruled out and why is as strategically valuable as knowing what you pursued.
8. **Map the workaround ecosystem.** The tools, hacks, and manual processes customers use today tell you what the MVP must replace — and set the usability bar.
9. **Stress-test your ICP definition quarterly.** As you learn, the Ideal Customer Profile sharpens. Revisit assumptions every 90 days or after every 10 interviews.
10. **Triangulate across data sources.** Interview data, support tickets, product usage logs, and industry analyst reports should converge. When they diverge, investigate before committing.

### Don't

1. **Don't build for a problem you personally had once.** Personal experience is a hypothesis, not a validated opportunity. The question is whether hundreds of others share it with equal severity.
2. **Don't confuse feature requests with opportunity signals.** "Can you add a PDF export button?" is a feature request. "Our compliance team manually reformats reports for four different regulators every quarter" is an opportunity.
3. **Don't use surveys as the primary discovery tool.** Surveys confirm hypotheses you already have; they rarely surface the unexpected insight that reframes the opportunity.
4. **Don't let the TAM number do the work.** A $50B market with 20 well-funded incumbents and a 3-year sales cycle is less attractive than a $500M market with fragmented, slow incumbents and a 30-day sales cycle.
5. **Don't skip the buyer-user distinction.** In enterprise IT, the person who experiences the pain and the person who signs the contract are rarely the same. An AI tool that delights engineers but cannot articulate ROI to a CFO will stall in procurement.
6. **Don't fall in love with the solution before validating the problem.** "We built an amazing NLP pipeline" is not an opportunity. "CFOs cannot get plain-language answers from their data warehouses without a SQL developer" is.
7. **Don't conflate market trend with market opportunity.** "AI is hot" is a trend. "Mid-market manufacturers cannot afford a data science team but need predictive maintenance" is an opportunity with a defined buyer and a quantifiable pain.
8. **Don't over-index on early adopters.** Early adopters tolerate friction and incomplete products that mainstream buyers will reject. Validate that the core pain exists in the mainstream segment, not just among enthusiasts.
9. **Don't treat competitor existence as validation.** Competitors prove a market exists but tell you nothing about whether your differentiation is meaningful or your go-to-market is viable.
10. **Don't skip opportunity scoring before committing to a build.** Apply Ulwick's formula or an equivalent prioritization model. Gut feel at this stage is expensive to undo at the roadmap stage.

## Metric-Driven Scenarios with Anti-Examples

### Scenario 1: AI-Powered Audit Trail for BFSI Compliance Teams

**Trigger:** A consulting partner notices that three separate BFSI clients mention, unprompted, that audit preparation consumes 200+ analyst-hours per quarter. Each team uses a different combination of Excel, SharePoint, and emailed PDFs. The partner commissions a structured discovery sprint.

**Analysis:**
- 22 interviews conducted across compliance leads, internal auditors, and CFOs at 8 firms
- Pain frequency: Quarterly regulatory submission cycle (4x/year) + ad hoc regulator queries (avg 6x/year = effectively monthly)
- Quantified pain: Average 210 analyst-hours/quarter at blended cost of $65/hour = $54,600/quarter = $218,400/year per firm
- Current workaround cost: 2 FTEs dedicated to documentation reconciliation
- Opportunity Score: Importance = 9.1, Satisfaction with current process = 2.4 → Score = 9.1 + 6.7 = 15.8 (Strong opportunity)
- SAM: 1,200 mid-to-large BFSI firms in India + SEA with compliance teams of 5+ people
- SAM value: 1,200 x $80,000 ACV = $96M

**Decision:** Commission a 6-week paid pilot with 3 firms at $8,000/pilot. Success metric: Reduce audit prep time by >= 50% within one quarter.

**Result:** All three pilots achieved 58–67% time reduction. Two firms signed annual contracts at $72,000 ACV within 90 days of pilot close. Third firm required an integration with a legacy system — added to roadmap as a dependency before broad rollout.

**Anti-Example:** A competing team skipped the discovery sprint and built an "AI audit dashboard" based on a single large client's brief. The dashboard solved one firm's idiosyncratic workflow. When pitched to eight other BFSI firms, seven said the interface assumed a data structure their systems did not produce. The team spent 9 months in custom integrations with no replicable product. By the time they pivoted, the consulting partner's paid pilots had generated enough signal to define a standardized data ingestion layer.

### Scenario 2: Direct-to-Engineer SaaS Tooling for DevOps Automation

**Trigger:** A product manager at an IT services firm notices that in five consecutive quarterly business reviews, engineering leads from different clients mention that release pipeline failures consume 30–40% of sprint capacity in debugging and rollback. NPS verbatims from the platform team's internal survey show 11 of 18 low-score responses mention "unpredictable deploy failures."

**Analysis:**
- Pain frequency: Every sprint cycle (bi-weekly for most clients) = 26x/year — extremely high
- Quantified pain: Average 35% of 5 senior engineer days per sprint = 1.75 engineer-days x $800/day = $1,400/sprint = $36,400/year per team
- Current workaround: Manual pre-deploy checklists, post-deploy monitoring Slack channels, on-call rotations that drain morale
- Interview finding (20 engineers, 8 DevOps leads): Root cause is not deploy tooling but inconsistent environment configuration drift between staging and production
- Pivot: The opportunity is not "better CI/CD" — multiple incumbents own that space. The opportunity is "environment configuration drift detection and auto-remediation" — an under-served sub-problem
- Opportunity Score: Importance = 8.8, Satisfaction = 3.1 → Score = 8.8 + 5.7 = 14.5 (Validate further)

**Decision:** Build a lightweight open-source drift detection CLI to generate demand signal and gather usage data before committing to a paid SaaS. Measure: 500 downloads in 60 days as a green light for monetization.

**Result:** CLI reached 1,100 downloads in 45 days. GitHub issues revealed three enterprise use cases not in the original brief. Converted 14 paying teams at $400/month within 4 months of launch.

**Anti-Example:** A rival team read the same DevOps market reports and built a full-featured CI/CD orchestration platform, reasoning that "the $8B DevOps tooling market" validated the opportunity. After 14 months and $1.2M in development, they discovered that enterprise procurement for CI/CD replacements required an 18-month evaluation cycle and security review that a startup could not survive. They had sized the TAM but failed to size the sales cycle and switching cost — both fatal constraints invisible in the market report.

### Scenario 3: AI Meeting Intelligence for Consulting Firms

**Trigger:** An internal tools team at a management consulting firm observes that 60% of engagement managers spend 45–90 minutes after every client call manually summarizing notes, assigning action items, and updating the CRM. The observation comes from a time-tracking audit, not from interviews. The team initiates a discovery sprint to validate whether this is the real pain.

**Analysis:**
- Interviews: 18 engagement managers, 6 partners, 4 operations leads
- Surprising finding: Engagement managers do not mind note-taking — they find it forces synthesis. The real pain is downstream: action items assigned in meetings are tracked in at least 3 different tools (email, Teams, CRM) with no single source of truth, causing missed follow-ups
- Frequency: Every client engagement has 3–5 calls/week. Missed follow-up rate cited by 14 of 18 EMs: "at least one per week"
- Cost: A missed client commitment at a consulting firm carries reputational cost that is multiples of the billable hour lost
- Pivot: The product is not a transcription or summary tool — it is an action-item accountability layer that integrates with existing tools
- Opportunity Score: Importance = 9.3, Satisfaction = 2.8 → Score = 9.3 + 6.5 = 15.8 (Strong opportunity)

**Decision:** Run a 4-week internal pilot with 10 engagement managers using a lightweight integration between Teams and the firm's CRM. Success metric: >= 80% of action items logged automatically without manual entry.

**Result:** 91% automatic capture rate achieved. Missed follow-up rate dropped from "at least 1/week" to "roughly 1/month" per EM. Internal pilot expanded to 40 users. Product team filed for internal deployment budget and began assessing commercial licensing.

**Anti-Example:** A SaaS startup in the same space built a meeting transcription tool with AI-generated summaries, targeting consulting firms. They had not done structured interviews; their assumption was that "summarization saves time." When pitched to the same consulting firm, EMs said summaries were "nice to have" but would not pay $30/seat for them — their firms already had junior analysts handling notes. The startup had solved the stated problem but not the actual pain. They pivoted 11 months into development after burning $600K.

## Practitioner Playbook

A structured, repeatable process for identifying and validating opportunities in IT/AI/Product/Consulting contexts.

1. **Define the observation trigger.** Identify the initial signal that prompted investigation: a support ticket cluster, a lost deal debrief, a client offhand comment, an NPS verbatim, a process audit finding, or a personal observation. Document it in one sentence. Do not act on a single data point.

2. **Form a falsifiable hypothesis.** Write the opportunity hypothesis in the form: "We believe that [ICP] struggle with [pain] [frequency] and are currently spending [cost] managing it with [workaround]. We will know this is true if [specific evidence from interviews]." A hypothesis you cannot falsify is not a hypothesis — it is a conviction, and convictions are dangerous at this stage.

3. **Define the Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) tightly.** Before interviewing anyone, specify the ICP by industry, firm size, role, geography, and current tech stack. Loose ICPs produce contradictory interview data that cannot be synthesized.

4. **Recruit interview subjects across the buying committee.** For B2B IT/AI opportunities, recruit: (a) the end user who experiences the pain daily, (b) the manager who owns the budget, (c) the champion who would advocate internally, and (d) the blocker who would veto the purchase. Each reveals a different dimension of the opportunity.

5. **Conduct a minimum of 20 discovery interviews.** Use a semi-structured interview guide. Open with context-setting questions ("Walk me through your last [relevant process]."), progress to pain exploration ("What does that cost you?"), and end with demand probing ("What would it be worth to solve this completely?"). Record with permission. Do not pitch during discovery.

6. **Synthesize across interviews using affinity clustering.** After every 5 interviews, map verbatims to an affinity diagram. Look for patterns that recur without prompting. Verbatims that appear in >= 60% of interviews with high emotional intensity are your candidate pain clusters.

7. **Quantify the pain in three dimensions.** For each pain cluster, calculate: (a) time cost (hours/week x loaded hourly rate), (b) financial cost (errors, penalties, lost revenue, rework), and (c) strategic cost (competitive disadvantage, regulatory exposure, team attrition). The most valuable opportunities score high on at least two of three.

8. **Run a TAM-SAM-SOM sizing exercise.** Use bottom-up sizing first (number of ICPs x annual spend on the problem). Then run top-down as a sanity check. Identify the three biggest constraints that shrink TAM to SAM (geography, regulation, channel, product maturity). Identify the three biggest constraints that shrink SAM to SOM (competitive win rate, sales cycle, implementation complexity). If SOM is below your minimum viable business threshold, the opportunity needs reframing or the beachhead needs tightening.

9. **Score each candidate opportunity using Ulwick's formula.** Assign Importance and Satisfaction scores based on interview data. Rank opportunities by score. Prioritize any opportunity scoring > 15 for immediate demand validation. Place opportunities scoring 10–15 in a secondary validation queue.

10. **Conduct a competitive landscape audit.** For the top-ranked opportunity, map: (a) direct competitors, (b) indirect substitutes, (c) the "do nothing" option (most underrated competitor). Assess each on their presence in your beachhead segment, pricing, switching cost, and strategic trajectory. Identify the gap your unfair advantage addresses.

11. **Design and run a demand validation experiment.** Do not build the product yet. Instead, create a demand artifact: a landing page, a one-page solution brief, a mockup, or a paid consulting engagement that proxies the product. Set a binary success criterion before running the experiment (e.g., "3 signed LOIs in 30 days" or "40% of demo calls request a pilot"). Run the experiment. Evaluate the result against the criterion without rationalizing a miss.

12. **Write the validated opportunity statement.** If the demand experiment clears the threshold, write the canonical opportunity statement: "For [ICP], who [pain with quantified cost], we offer [solution] that [specific outcome], unlike [current alternative], because [unfair advantage]." This statement becomes the north star for the product brief, the business model canvas, and the investor narrative.

13. **Document invalidated hypotheses.** Record every hypothesis that failed and the evidence that falsified it. This creates an institutional memory that prevents the team from relitigating dead ideas and reveals strategic insight about what the market does not want.

14. **Schedule a 90-day opportunity review.** Markets shift, ICPs evolve, and competitive landscapes change. Set a recurring 90-day review to stress-test the original opportunity statement against new data. Treat opportunity identification as a continuous process, not a one-time exercise.

## Content Critique & Depth Gaps

This section evaluates the source material against the analytical rigor expected at IIM Ahmedabad, ISB, Harvard Business School, and equivalent programs, and identifies what is missing for a practitioner operating at that level.

### What the Source Material Does Well
The source content correctly identifies the primacy of the problem over the solution, the role of workarounds as signal, and the need for direct customer engagement. The use case and scenario are grounded and concrete.

### Critical Depth Gaps

**1. Absence of competitive demand analysis.**
The source material treats opportunity identification as if the market is a blank canvas. At the MBA level, opportunity identification must include a rigorous analysis of why existing solutions fail — not just that they fail, but whether the failure is structural (incumbent incentive misalignment), technical (legacy architecture), or behavioral (customer inertia). Each failure type implies a different entry strategy.

**2. No treatment of timing risk (the "why now" question).**
A common MBA case study question is: if this is a real problem, why hasn't it been solved? The source does not address the "why now" framework — the confluence of enabling factors (regulatory shift, technology maturity, macro trend, distribution unlock) that makes this moment uniquely right for a given solution. Without this, even a well-validated opportunity may be premature or late.

**3. Missing demand curve analysis.**
The source correctly asks whether customers will pay but does not address price elasticity, willingness-to-pay segmentation, or the relationship between pain severity and price tolerance. At HBS, a case on opportunity identification would include a van Westendorp pricing analysis or conjoint study to determine the price range within which the opportunity is commercially viable.

**4. Regulatory and compliance dimension absent.**
For IT/AI/consulting contexts specifically, opportunities often live in regulated markets. The source does not address how to assess regulatory risk as part of opportunity scoring — a product that solves a $200M pain point but requires an RBI license or GDPR compliance layer may have a 24-month time-to-market that fundamentally changes the opportunity calculus.

**5. No treatment of network effects or platform potential.**
Some opportunities are valuable because they can evolve into platforms or two-sided markets. The source treats every opportunity as a point solution. At the MBA level, students must evaluate whether the initial problem is a wedge into a larger platform play — and whether network effects are available as a defensive moat.

**6. Stakeholder ecosystem mapping is absent.**
In enterprise IT and consulting, the opportunity is often shaped as much by the political economy inside the buyer's organization as by the technical merit of the solution. The source does not address stakeholder mapping — identifying who benefits, who loses power, and who controls the veto — as part of the opportunity assessment.

**7. Ethnographic and observational methods are underrepresented.**
The source mentions "shadowing nurses" in an example but does not treat observational research as a method in its own right. At this level, students should understand contextual inquiry, day-in-the-life shadowing, process archaeology (reconstructing what actually happens vs. what the process map says), and diary studies as distinct tools with different signal types.

**8. No framework for opportunity portfolio management.**
Organizations and consulting firms do not evaluate one opportunity at a time; they manage a portfolio. The source does not address how to prioritize across multiple candidate opportunities using a structured framework (e.g., a 2x2 of strategic fit vs. market size, or a scoring matrix weighted by time-to-revenue and competitive intensity).

## Quick-Recall Card

- Opportunity identification is not brainstorming — it is disciplined evidence collection against a falsifiable hypothesis
- The pain must be frequent (>= weekly), severe (quantifiable in dollars or hours), and inadequately served by current alternatives
- Workarounds are the most reliable signal: if customers are already paying — in time, money, or pain — to manage a problem, the problem is real
- Use the JTBD lens to identify the actual job, not the surface-level complaint; solve the root, not the symptom
- TAM tells you the ceiling; SAM tells you the reachable market; SOM tells you what you can win — never conflate the three
- Bottom-up sizing (number of ICPs x annual pain cost) is more credible than top-down (industry report x share percentage); use both and reconcile the gap
- Ulwick's Opportunity Score: Importance + MAX(Importance - Satisfaction, 0); scores above 15 are strong opportunities, scores below 10 are deprioritized
- Demand validation requires a commitment artifact — signed LOI, paid pilot, deposit — not a verbal "sounds interesting"
- Interview a minimum of 20 people in the ICP before forming conviction; patterns need repetition across independent voices to be signal rather than noise
- The "why now" question is as important as the "what" — identify the enabling conditions (technology shift, regulatory change, distribution unlock) that make this moment the right moment
- An unfair advantage is not a feature; it is a structural asymmetry — proprietary data, domain expertise, distribution, or a regulatory position — that a competitor cannot replicate quickly
- Document invalidated hypotheses; institutional memory of what does not work is as strategically valuable as knowledge of what does
- Beachhead first, then expansion: win one narrow segment deeply before claiming a broad market

As a PM/Consultant/AI Lead, the one question to answer with this framework is: "Is this pain real, recurring, expensive enough to buy against, and does our unfair advantage let us solve it better than any current or emerging alternative?"
