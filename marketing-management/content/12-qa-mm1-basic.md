# Practice Q&A — MM-I Basic

Foundational questions covering Marketing Management I concepts. Q1 – Q70

---

## Q1. What is marketing in simple terms?

**Level:** Basic

Marketing is the process of understanding what people need, creating a product or service that fits those needs, and communicating its value so people choose to buy it. It includes activities like research, pricing, promotion, and distribution to satisfy customers and achieve business goals.

**Real-life applications:**

- Marketing helps a bakery decide what flavors customers want.
- It guides how the bakery prices cakes and where it sells them.
- It also shapes posters, social media posts, and offers.
- In daily life, it explains why certain brands attract you more.

**Key concepts:** `Marketing` `Customer needs` `Value` `Exchange` `Satisfaction`

---

## Q2. Define marketing management.

**Level:** Basic

Marketing management means planning and controlling marketing activities to reach business goals. It involves choosing target customers, designing offerings, setting prices, selecting channels, and deciding promotions to create value and build customer relationships.

**Real-life applications:**

- A mobile company uses marketing management to plan a new model launch.
- It decides the target audience, pricing, and ad message.
- It selects online and retail stores for sales.
- It tracks results and adjusts strategy if sales are low.

**Key concepts:** `Marketing management` `Planning` `Implementation` `Control` `Customer value`

---

## Q3. What is a market?

**Level:** Basic

A market is a group of people or organizations that have a similar need or problem, have the ability to pay, and are willing to buy a product or service to satisfy that need.

**Real-life applications:**

- Students needing affordable meals form a market for budget cafeterias.
- Companies design low-cost menus for them.
- Online learning platforms target learners as a market too.
- Governments also study markets to support small businesses.

**Key concepts:** `Market` `Buyers` `Needs` `Demand` `Willingness to pay`

---

## Q4. What is the difference between needs, wants, and demands?

**Level:** Basic

Needs are basic requirements like safety, food, or comfort. Wants are specific ways people choose to satisfy needs, shaped by culture and taste. Demands are wants supported by the ability and willingness to pay.

**Real-life applications:**

- People need transport, want a scooter, and demand a specific model if they can afford it.
- Brands design products for different income levels.
- This helps businesses set versions and prices.
- It also explains why premium products sell to some groups.

**Key concepts:** `Needs` `Wants` `Demands` `Purchasing power` `Preference`

---

## Q5. What is customer value?

**Level:** Basic

Customer value is what customers feel they receive compared to what they give up. It includes product benefits, service, quality, and experience versus money, time, and effort spent.

**Real-life applications:**

- A fast-delivery grocery app creates value by saving time.
- A low-price store creates value by reducing cost.
- A hospital creates value through trust and care quality.
- Students choose courses that feel worth the fees and effort.

**Key concepts:** `Customer value` `Benefits` `Costs` `Perceived value` `Experience`

---

## Q6. What is customer satisfaction?

**Level:** Basic

Customer satisfaction is how happy a customer feels after using a product or service compared to what they expected. If performance meets or exceeds expectations, satisfaction increases; if it falls short, dissatisfaction happens.

**Real-life applications:**

- Restaurants track satisfaction through reviews and repeat visits.
- Schools measure satisfaction through feedback surveys.
- Companies improve quality and service to reduce complaints.
- Satisfied customers often recommend the brand to others.

**Key concepts:** `Satisfaction` `Expectations` `Performance` `Experience` `Loyalty`

---

## Q7. What is the marketing concept?

**Level:** Basic

The marketing concept is the idea that a company should start by understanding customer needs and then create products and services that satisfy those needs better than competitors. Customer satisfaction leads to long-term success.

**Real-life applications:**

- A phone brand studies what users want before adding features.
- A college designs programs based on student career needs.
- Hospitals improve patient care based on feedback.
- Businesses gain loyalty by focusing on customer benefit.

**Key concepts:** `Marketing concept` `Customer orientation` `Needs` `Satisfaction` `Long-term profit`

---

## Q8. What is the selling concept?

**Level:** Basic

The selling concept assumes customers will not buy enough unless the company uses aggressive selling and promotion. The focus is on pushing products, not deeply understanding customer needs.

**Real-life applications:**

- Some insurance sellers push policies through constant calls.
- Tele-shopping channels rely heavily on persuasion.
- This approach may work for low-involvement or unwanted products.
- But it can reduce trust if customers feel pressured.

**Key concepts:** `Selling concept` `Promotion` `Persuasion` `Push strategy` `Short-term sales`

---

## Q9. What is the production concept?

**Level:** Basic

The production concept says customers prefer products that are widely available and affordable. Companies focusing on this aim to improve efficiency, reduce costs, and increase distribution.

**Real-life applications:**

- Budget brands focus on low cost and wide availability.
- Public transport services aim for frequent availability.
- Retail chains optimize supply to keep prices low.
- This is common where demand is high and options are limited.

**Key concepts:** `Production concept` `Efficiency` `Availability` `Low cost` `Scale`

---

## Q10. What is the product concept?

**Level:** Basic

The product concept suggests customers prefer products with the best quality, performance, and features. Companies focus on continuous product improvement, but risk ignoring what customers truly value.

**Real-life applications:**

- A tech firm may add many features that users never use.
- Camera brands may focus only on specs, not ease of use.
- Companies must balance innovation with customer simplicity.
- In education, too many features in an app can confuse learners.

**Key concepts:** `Product concept` `Quality` `Features` `Innovation` `Marketing myopia risk`

---

## Q11. What is holistic marketing?

**Level:** Basic

Holistic marketing is the view that marketing should consider the whole business system—customers, employees, partners, society, and performance. It includes relationship marketing, integrated marketing, internal marketing, and socially responsible marketing.

**Real-life applications:**

- A bank aligns branches, app, and call center to give consistent service.
- Companies train employees so service matches brand promise.
- Brands also consider environmental and social impacts.
- This improves trust and long-term reputation.

**Key concepts:** `Holistic marketing` `Integrated marketing` `Internal marketing` `Relationship marketing` `Performance marketing`

## Q12. What are the 4Ps of marketing?

**Level:** Basic

The 4Ps are Product, Price, Place, and Promotion. They represent the main tools companies use to deliver value: what they sell, how much they charge, where they sell, and how they communicate to customers.

**Real-life applications:**

- A new café designs menu items (product).
- It sets affordable rates (price).
- It chooses a busy location and delivery apps (place).
- It runs ads and offers (promotion).

**Key concepts:** `4Ps` `Product` `Price` `Place` `Promotion`

---

## Q13. What is a value proposition?

**Level:** Basic

A value proposition is a clear statement of what benefit a product offers, to whom it is meant, and why it is better than alternatives. It answers, “Why should customers choose us?”

**Real-life applications:**

- A tutoring app promises “easy learning in 15 minutes daily.”A hospital promises “trusted care with quick appointments.”Companies use it to guide advertising messages.
- It also helps sales teams explain benefits simply.

**Key concepts:** `Value proposition` `Benefits` `Target customer` `Differentiation` `Positioning`

## Q14. What is market segmentation?

**Level:** Basic

Market segmentation is dividing a broad market into smaller groups of customers who have similar needs, behaviors, or characteristics. It helps companies serve customers more effectively.

**Real-life applications:**

- A soap brand makes variants for dry skin and oily skin.
- A university targets different programs for working professionals and fresh graduates.
- Retailers create offers for students and families.
- Segmentation reduces wasted marketing spending.

**Key concepts:** `Segmentation` `Customer groups` `Similar needs` `Segmentation bases` `Focus`

## Q15. What is target marketing?

**Level:** Basic

Target marketing means selecting one or more market segments to focus on. The company then designs its product and marketing activities for that specific group instead of trying to serve everyone.

**Real-life applications:**

- A sports brand targets runners with running shoes.
- A coaching center targets competitive exam students.
- A clinic targets senior citizens with health packages.
- Targeting improves message relevance and conversion.

**Key concepts:** `Targeting` `Target market` `Segment selection` `Focus strategy` `Customer fit`

## Q16. What is positioning?

**Level:** Basic

Positioning is how a brand wants customers to think about it compared to competitors. It focuses on creating a clear image in the customer’s mind, such as “most affordable,” “most reliable,” or “best quality.”

**Real-life applications:**

- A toothpaste brand positions as “sensitive teeth protection.”A budget airline positions as “low fares, on-time.”Students choose colleges based on perceived positioning like “best placements.”Positioning guides advertising and product decisions.

**Key concepts:** `Positioning` `Brand image` `Customer perception` `Differentiation` `Competitive comparison`

## Q17. What is marketing myopia?

**Level:** Basic

Marketing myopia is the mistake of focusing too much on the product and too little on customer needs. Companies fail when they define their business narrowly and ignore changing customer preferences.

**Real-life applications:**

- A DVD rental store ignoring streaming demand can collapse.
- A taxi company ignoring app-based booking loses customers.
- Schools ignoring online learning needs may lose enrollments.
- Customer-focused thinking helps avoid myopia.

**Key concepts:** `Marketing myopia` `Customer needs` `Narrow definition` `Change` `Opportunity analysis`

---

## Q18. What is a SWOT analysis?

**Level:** Basic

SWOT analysis is a tool to understand a business situation by listing internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats. It helps plan strategies based on reality.

**Real-life applications:**

- A café lists strength as taste, weakness as small space.
- Opportunity could be office crowd nearby, threat could be new competitors.
- It helps decide whether to expand or change prices.
- Even students can use SWOT for career planning.

**Key concepts:** `SWOT` `Strengths` `Weaknesses` `Opportunities` `Threats`

---

## Q19. What is the macro environment in marketing?

**Level:** Basic

The macro environment includes large external forces that affect all companies, such as economic conditions, technology changes, social trends, laws, and environmental issues. Businesses cannot control these but must adapt.

**Real-life applications:**

- Inflation affects how much customers can spend.
- New technology changes how people shop.
- New laws can restrict ads or packaging.
- Companies scan macro factors to avoid surprises.

**Key concepts:** `Macro environment` `PESTEL` `External forces` `Trends` `Adaptation`

---

## Q20. What is the micro environment in marketing?

**Level:** Basic

The micro environment includes factors close to the company that affect its ability to serve customers, such as customers, competitors, suppliers, distributors, and the company itself.

**Real-life applications:**

- Supplier delays can cause stock shortages.
- Competitors’ discounts can reduce your sales.
- Retailers influence how your product is displayed.
- Understanding micro factors improves daily marketing decisions.

**Key concepts:** `Micro environment` `Customers` `Competitors` `Suppliers` `Intermediaries`

**Real-life applications:**

## Q28. What is Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)?

**Level:** Basic

Customer Lifetime Value is the total profit a business expects from a customer over the entire relationship. It highlights why retaining good customers can be more valuable than constantly finding new ones.

**Real-life applications:**

- Telecom companies offer retention deals to valuable customers.
- Apps give rewards to keep users active.
- A coaching center focuses on long-term students.
- CLV guides spending on service and loyalty programs.

**Key concepts:** `CLV` `Retention` `Profitability` `Loyalty` `Long-term value`

## Q29. What is the consumer buying decision process?

**Level:** Basic

It is the common set of steps consumers follow: recognizing a need, searching information, evaluating options, making a purchase, and then experiencing post-purchase satisfaction or regret.

**Real-life applications:**

- A student needs a laptop, checks reviews, compares prices, buys, then shares feedback.
- Brands create content for each step.
- Stores use offers at purchase stage.
- After-sales service reduces regret and builds loyalty.

**Key concepts:** `Need recognition` `Information search` `Evaluation` `Purchase` `Post-purchase behavior`

## Q34. What is a buying center?

**Level:** Basic

A buying center is the group of people in an organization who participate in a purchase decision. It may include users, influencers, buyers, deciders, and gatekeepers.

**Real-life applications:**

- For software purchase, IT influences, finance approves, and users test.
- Sales teams must convince multiple roles.

## Q35. What is the role of a “decider” in B2B purchases?

**Level:** Basic

A decider is the person who has the final authority to choose whether to buy and which supplier to select. The decider may be a CEO, department head, or procurement leader depending on the purchase.

**Real-life applications:**

- A CEO may decide on a large software contract.
- A school principal may decide on new lab equipment.
- Suppliers tailor proposals to match decider priorities.
- Understanding the decider improves closing chances.

**Key concepts:** `Decider` `Authority` `Buying center` `Approval` `Final selection`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### STP and 4Ps
**Purpose:** Connect segmentation and targeting to a concrete marketing mix.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Segmentation -> Targeting -> Positioning -> 4Ps
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Segmentation - group customers with similar needs.
Component 2: Targeting - choose which segments to serve.
Component 3: Positioning - define the promise in the customer mind.
Component 4: 4Ps - product, price, place, promotion.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A fintech app segments by income and behavior, targets salaried users, positions itself as fast and trustworthy, and then sets the product, pricing, retail channel, and promotion accordingly.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the message, offer, and channel are not aligned.

### Marketing Orientation Ladder
**Purpose:** Show the difference between pushing products and building customer value.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Selling -> Production -> Product -> Marketing -> Holistic
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Selling concept - push what you already made.
Component 2: Production concept - optimize availability and cost.
Component 3: Product concept - optimize features and quality.
Component 4: Marketing concept - start from customer needs.
Component 5: Holistic marketing - align all touchpoints and relationships.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A brand can have a great feature set but still fail if it ignores positioning, service, and channel consistency.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is talking about output more than customer value.

### SWOT / PESTEL Map
**Purpose:** Separate internal strengths and weaknesses from external opportunities and threats.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Internal: Strengths / Weaknesses
External: Opportunities / Threats
PESTEL: macro factors around the firm
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: SWOT - a compact strategic scan.
Component 2: PESTEL - the wider macro-environment lens.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A software brand may have a strength in trust, a weakness in price, an opportunity in remote work trends, and a threat from aggressive competitors.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When strategy needs a structured context scan before decisions.

### Consumer Decision Funnel
**Purpose:** Track the steps from need recognition to post-purchase behavior.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Need -> Search -> Evaluate -> Purchase -> Post-purchase
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Need recognition - the customer feels a gap.
Component 2: Information search - the customer learns options.
Component 3: Evaluation - the customer compares alternatives.
Component 4: Purchase and post-purchase - the customer buys, uses, and reacts.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A buyer of enterprise CRM first notices process pain, then shortlists vendors, then compares demos and references before signing.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When conversion is weak and the team needs to locate the drop-off.

### B2B Buying Center / DMU
**Purpose:** Map the people who influence and approve a complex purchase.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Influencers -> Deciders -> Approvers -> Final selection
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Buying center - the group involved in the purchase.
Component 2: DMU - the decision-making unit inside the customer.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cybersecurity vendor must sell to IT, finance, procurement, and legal, not just the end user.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a B2B sale is stalling after the first demo.

### IMC and Channel System
**Purpose:** Keep messages and channels coordinated.

```text
Advertising + PR + Sales promo + Personal selling + Direct marketing
                    -> Consistent brand message
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Awareness - paid and owned media.
Component 2: Credibility - PR, reviews, and word of mouth.
Component 3: Response - offers, incentives, and direct channels.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product launch uses the same promise in ads, sales decks, onboarding emails, and retail displays so the market hears one story.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When campaigns are fragmented across channels.

## 4. Formulas
The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source]; the source names the concepts but does not state the math explicitly.

Formula: Customer lifetime value = Average purchase value x Purchase frequency x Customer lifespan
Variables:
Average purchase value = typical spend per purchase.
Purchase frequency = how often the customer buys.
Customer lifespan = length of the relationship.
Why this formula exists: It estimates how much value a customer contributes over time.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> the business needs better retention or upsell.
Value moderate -> the relationship is healthy but improvable.
Value high -> the brand can spend more to acquire and keep the customer.
Worked example with numbers: If average purchase value is $50, frequency is 6 times per year, and lifespan is 4 years, CLV is $1,200 before discounting.

Formula: Break-even units = Fixed costs / (Price - Variable cost per unit)
Variables:
Fixed costs = costs that do not vary with volume.
Price = price per unit charged to the customer.
Variable cost per unit = incremental cost of serving one unit.
Why this formula exists: It tells you the volume needed to cover the cost base.
How to interpret the output:
Value high -> the product needs scale or a higher price.
Value moderate -> manageable with normal demand.
Value low -> easier to launch and recover.
Worked example with numbers: If fixed costs are $80,000, price is $20, and variable cost is $12, break-even is 10,000 units.

Formula: Price elasticity = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price
Variables:
Quantity demanded = units sold after the price move.
Price = the change in price.
Why this formula exists: It shows whether customers are sensitive to price.
How to interpret the output:
Value > 1 -> demand is price-sensitive -> avoid aggressive hikes.
Value about 1 -> monitor carefully.
Value < 1 -> demand is less sensitive -> premium pricing is more feasible.
Worked example with numbers: If price rises 10% and demand falls 5%, elasticity is 0.5, so demand is relatively inelastic.

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: Brand repositioning for a SaaS product
Situation: A SaaS company has strong awareness but weak trust. Surveys show 68% awareness, but only 22% of respondents associate the brand with reliability.
Applicable framework/metric: STP, positioning, and brand equity.
Analysis: The issue is not awareness; it is brand image and proof. The company should not increase ad spend until the promise and the experience line up.
Decision rule: If awareness is high but trust is below 30%, fix product/service experience; if trust is above 50%, scale the campaign; if both are weak, rethink target market and message.
Action: Rewrite the positioning statement, improve onboarding, and use customer proof in the communication mix.

Scenario 2: B2B buying-center campaign
Situation: An enterprise AI vendor is losing deals after the demo phase. Sales data shows the technical user is positive, but procurement and finance stall the deal.
Applicable framework/metric: B2B buying center / DMU.
Analysis: The campaign is speaking to the user but not the deciders. The vendor needs materials for authority, approval, risk, and ROI, not just feature demos.
Decision rule: If technical approval is high but final selection lags, redesign the pitch for the DMU; if both technical and commercial approval are low, revisit the offer; if both are high, speed up contracting.
Action: Add ROI calculators, procurement-ready documents, and executive summaries to the sales motion.

Scenario 3: Product portfolio and pricing
Situation: A consumer brand is considering a premium line extension. The new variant would lift COGS by 18% but could raise willingness to pay by 30%.
Applicable framework/metric: Product mix, pricing, and break-even.
Analysis: The extension can work only if the premium promise is consistent and the channel can support the higher price. Otherwise it just adds complexity.
Decision rule: If willingness to pay exceeds added cost by at least 10 points and the channel supports it, launch; if not, keep the core line and revisit packaging or positioning.
Action: Pilot the new variant, test price elasticity, and compare repeat purchase versus the base product.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build a segment map showing who buys, why they buy, and what they value.
2. Write one positioning statement for each target segment and align every touchpoint to it.
3. Maintain one research dashboard with customer, competitor, channel, and market intelligence.
4. Review CLV, retention, churn, and repeat purchase by segment and channel.
5. Audit the communication mix so ads, PR, sales, and direct channels all tell the same story.
6. Run small pilots before committing to large-scale rollout or product expansion.
7. Track packaging, pricing, and distribution together, not in separate silos.
