
# Pricing Innovative/New Products

## Overview

New products don’t have a clear market price. Companies choose between charging high first or low first, depending on goals and competition.

## Why It Matters

Early pricing can decide success or failure. Price affects adoption speed, competitor reaction, and long-term profit.

## Key Principles

- Use skimming if product is unique and customers will pay more.
- Use penetration if you want fast market share.
- Educate customers if product is new to them.
- Adjust price over time as competition grows.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Price skimming** | High price first, then reduce later. |
| **Penetration pricing** | Low price first to grow fast. |
| **Adoption** | How quickly customers start using it. |
| **Reference price** | What customers expect to pay. |

## Use Case

A new health-tech gadget launches at a premium price for early adopters, then later offers a cheaper version.

## Scenario

> A company launches an AI-based learning app. They start with a low student plan to gain users quickly, then introduce premium features for higher price.

## Examples

- New smartphone launch: High launch price → later discounts.
- New OTT platform: Low subscription early → increases after gaining users.

# Promotional Pricing

## Overview

Promotional pricing is short-term discounts and deals to increase sales—like “Buy 1 Get 1,” festival sales, coupons, and limited-time offers.

## Why It Matters

Promotions can boost sales quickly, but too many promotions can train customers to wait for discounts and harm brand value.

## Key Principles

- Use promotions for specific goals (trial, inventory clearing, seasonal demand).
- Avoid frequent discounting for premium brands.
- Consider channel partner impact (retailers may face margin issues).
- Measure results (sales lift vs profit loss).

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Discount** | Reduced price for a period. |
| **Trade promotion** | Deals offered to retailers/distributors. |
| **Price off** | ₹X off or % off. |
| **Stockpiling** | Customers buy extra during discounts. |
| **Deal-prone customers** | People who buy only on discounts. |

## Use Case

A cookware brand runs a festival discount but ensures retailers are compensated so they don’t lose margin.

## Scenario

> A shampoo brand offers 20% off for 2 weeks. Sales jump, but next month sales drop because people stocked up. The brand then reduces frequency and uses bundle offers instead.

## Examples

- Big Billion Days / Great Indian Festival: Short-term sales boost and stock clearing.
- Restaurant happy hours: Drive demand in low-traffic time.

# Designing Channels of Distribution

## Overview

Distribution channels are the ways a product reaches customers—like wholesalers, retailers, company stores, or online platforms.

## Why It Matters

Even a great product fails if customers can’t find it easily. The right channel improves availability, convenience, and sales growth.

## Key Principles

- Choose channels based on customer buying habits.
- Balance coverage (more outlets) vs control (better experience).
- Ensure fast and reliable supply chain.
- Match channel type to product type (luxury vs mass market).

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Channel** | Path from producer to customer. |
| **Retailer** | Sells to final customer. |
| **Wholesaler/Distributor** | Buys in bulk, supplies retailers. |
| **Direct channel** | Company sells directly (website/store). |
| **Indirect channel** | Uses intermediaries (retailers). |

## Use Case

A premium perfume brand sells through select stores and its own website to maintain luxury image.

## Scenario

> A cement company creates small “company-backed shops” in towns to improve reach, branding, and trust where big retailers are limited.

## Examples

- Coca-Cola: Deep distribution—available everywhere via retailers.
- Nike: Mix of own stores + online + selected retailers.

# Channel Partners and Channel Conflict Management

## Overview

Channel partners are distributors, retailers, and online platforms that help sell products. Conflicts happen when partners feel treated unfairly (price differences, territory overlap, etc.).

## Why It Matters

Poor channel relationships can reduce availability, create price wars, and damage brand reputation in the market.

## Key Principles

- Set clear rules on pricing, territories, and roles.
- Communicate regularly and share incentives.
- Handle conflicts early with negotiation and data.
- Align goals (win-win partnerships).

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Channel partner** | Any intermediary helping distribution/sales. |
| **Channel conflict** | Disagreements between partners. |
| **Horizontal conflict** | Conflict at same level (two retailers). |
| **Vertical conflict** | Manufacturer vs distributor/retailer. |
| **Margin** | Profit share earned by partners. |

## Use Case

A brand sets online pricing rules so offline retailers don’t lose customers due to cheaper online prices.

## Scenario

> A company sells a TV online cheaper than in stores. Retailers protest. The company creates “exclusive bundles” for stores and keeps base price consistent across channels.

## Examples

- Offline vs online pricing conflict: Same product priced differently.
- Distributor territory conflict: Two distributors selling in same area.

# Online Channels and Omnichannel Retailing

## Overview

Online channels include websites, apps, marketplaces, and platforms. Omnichannel means customers get a smooth experience across online and offline (browse online, buy offline, return anywhere).

## Why It Matters

Customers want convenience. Businesses that combine online and offline well can grow faster and keep customers happier.

## Key Principles

- Ensure consistent pricing, product info, and service across channels.
- Manage inventory visibility (what’s available where).
- Balance needs of suppliers, platform, and customers.
- Focus on smooth delivery and returns.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **E-commerce** | Selling online. |
| **Marketplace** | Platform connecting buyers and sellers (e.g., Amazon). |
| **Two-sided platform** | Serves two groups (buyers and sellers). |
| **Omnichannel** | Integrated online + offline journey. |
| **Last-mile delivery** | Final step to customer doorstep. |

## Use Case

A fashion retailer lets customers order online and pick up in-store, reducing delivery cost and improving convenience.

## Scenario

> A customer checks shoe sizes online, visits store to try, orders online for a color not available in-store, and returns later at the store—no friction.

## Examples

- Reliance Retail / Tata Cliq style approach: Online + store integration.
- Click & Collect: Order online → pick up in store.

# Personal Selling and Sales Management

## Overview

Personal selling is when a salesperson directly talks to a customer to sell—common in high-value or complex products (B2B, appliances, insurance).

## Why It Matters

Many products need explanation and trust. A good sales process increases conversions, builds relationships, and improves customer satisfaction.

## Key Principles

- Understand customer needs before pitching.
- Build trust through honest information.
- Manage salesforce goals, training, and performance.
- Follow up after sale to reduce dissatisfaction.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Lead** | Potential customer. |
| **Prospecting** | Finding potential customers. |
| **Pitch** | Sales presentation. |
| **Objection handling** | Responding to customer doubts. |
| **CRM** | Tool to track customer interactions. |

## Use Case

A water purifier company uses door-to-door sales and demos to increase trust and close sales.

## Scenario

> A salesperson demonstrates a purifier, answers doubts about maintenance cost, offers a service plan, and closes the sale with a one-week installation guarantee.

## Examples

- Insurance agent: Builds trust, explains plans, closes based on needs.
- B2B software sales: Demos + negotiation + long-term contract.

# Marketing Communication (Traditional/Integrated)

## Overview

Marketing communication is how a company tells people about its product—using ads, TV, print, outdoor, PR, and more. The goal is to create awareness, interest, and action.

## Why It Matters

Even strong products need communication to be noticed. Clear communication builds brand image and influences buying decisions.

## Key Principles

- Choose the right message and target audience.
- Be consistent across channels (integrated communication).
- Use storytelling and benefits (not just features).
- Measure effectiveness (reach, recall, sales impact).

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **IMC** | Integrated Marketing Communications (consistent messaging). |
| **Reach** | Number of people who see the message. |
| **Frequency** | How often they see it. |
| **CTA** | Call-to-action (buy now, visit store). |
| **Positioning message** | Core idea communicated to customers. |

## Use Case

A weight-loss product uses TV ads for awareness and print for credibility, with a clear positioning message.

## Scenario

> A health drink launches: TV ad builds awareness, newspaper ad gives offer, and in-store posters push trial packs.

## Examples

- Festival campaigns: TV + hoardings + store branding.
- New FMCG launch: Sampling + print + radio for quick reach.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Launch Pricing Choice Set
**Purpose:** Decide whether the launch should prioritise premium capture or fast adoption.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
High price first  <->  Low price first
   skimming            penetration
```
Components explained:
- Skimming: start high when the product is unique or early adopters will pay.
- Penetration: start low when speed, share, and habit formation matter.
- Reference price: the mental anchor customers already expect to pay.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A new AI learning app can launch at a low student price to accelerate adoption and data collection, then add premium features later. If the product is genuinely differentiated, a skimming path can protect margin early.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the team is arguing about launch price without a clear growth objective.

### Promotional Pricing Control Loop
**Purpose:** Use discounts to create lift without training customers to wait for deals.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Promotion -> lift -> stockpiling / trial -> repeat behaviour -> margin check
```
Components explained:
- Discount depth: how much the price is reduced.
- Trade promotion: dealer or retailer deals.
- Deal-prone customers: buyers who only show up for discounts.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A consumer-tech brand runs a festival discount and sees strong sales lift, but also stockpiling. The decision is to shorten the promotion window and use bundles instead of repeated price cuts.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When sales are up but profit and brand strength are unclear.

### Channel Design Matrix
**Purpose:** Choose the right route-to-market based on coverage and control.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
            CONTROL
           low   high
COVERAGE  ---------------
 low      | niche | direct |
 high     | broad | hybrid |
```
Components explained:
- Coverage: how many customers/outlets you can reach.
- Control: how much you can shape price, experience, and service.
- Direct vs indirect: whether the firm sells itself or through intermediaries.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A premium perfume brand stays selective because too much coverage would damage the luxury signal. A mass-market brand goes broader and leans on retail reach.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the team must choose between reach and brand control.

### Channel Partner Conflict Management
**Purpose:** Prevent channel partners from fighting the brand or each other.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Manufacturer -> rules / pricing / territory / incentives -> partners
```
Components explained:
- Horizontal conflict: between partners at the same level.
- Vertical conflict: manufacturer versus retailer or distributor.
- Margin: the economic reason partners care.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** An online price cut causes retailers to complain because their margin is squeezed. The fix is consistent base pricing plus channel-specific bundles so the channel does not implode.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When channel partners start pushing back on price or territory.

### Omnichannel Journey Design
**Purpose:** Give customers a seamless online and offline experience.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
browse online -> buy online / buy offline -> pick up / return anywhere
```
Components explained:
- Omnichannel: the journey follows the customer, not the channel.
- Inventory visibility: customers need to know what is available.
- Last-mile delivery: the final step that often determines satisfaction.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A fashion retailer lets customers browse online, try in store, and return anywhere. That reduces friction and improves conversion while protecting the brand experience.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the business has both stores and digital touchpoints.

### Personal Selling and CRM Funnel
**Purpose:** Turn complex products into a structured sales process.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Lead -> Prospecting -> Pitch -> Objection handling -> Close -> CRM follow-up
```
Components explained:
- Lead: a possible buyer.
- Pitch: the sales conversation.
- Objection handling: resolving doubts before close.
- CRM: the system that tracks the relationship.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A B2B software rep uses CRM notes to handle objections about maintenance and implementation effort. The structured funnel raises conversion and prevents leads from being lost.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the selling motion is too dependent on individual memory or heroics.

### Integrated Marketing Communications
**Purpose:** Keep the message, audience, and call-to-action consistent across channels.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Awareness -> Interest -> Action
   |            |          |
  Reach      Frequency     CTA
```
Components explained:
- IMC: one consistent message across media.
- Reach: how many people see it.
- Frequency: how often they see it.
- CTA: the action you want them to take.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A health drink launch uses TV for awareness, print for credibility, and in-store posters for trial. The positioning message stays the same across all three so the brand does not confuse the customer.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When campaigns are fragmented and the market is not hearing one clear story.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| Don't | Do |
|-----|-----|
| Set launch price without deciding whether the goal is adoption or margin | Choose skimming or penetration based on the product's strategic role |
| Keep discounting to force sales while ignoring stockpiling and margin loss | Measure lift, repeat purchase, and profit impact together |
| Use every channel for every product | Match channel coverage to brand control and customer buying habits |
| Let online and offline pricing drift apart without a rule | Create channel pricing governance before conflict starts |
| Treat omnichannel as a website project | Design one customer journey across browse, buy, pickup, and return |
| Leave sales performance in spreadsheets and inboxes | Track the funnel in CRM and coach against the same pipeline facts |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: New Product Launch Pricing
**Situation:** A new AI learning app has a choice between launching at $5/month to maximise adoption or $20/month to signal premium value. The team estimates 10,000 users at $5 and 3,000 users at $20 in the first quarter.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Price skimming vs penetration pricing.

**Analysis:** If the product needs network effects, feedback data, or rapid habit formation, penetration pricing can be the better move even if the first-quarter revenue is lower. If the product is clearly differentiated and the target users expect a premium reference price, skimming may be smarter.

**Decision rule:** If adoption is the main goal and expected users at the lower price are 2x or more the higher-price base, choose penetration. If margin protection and brand signal are more important, choose skimming.

**Action:** Launch the low-price tier first, then add a premium tier after retention stabilises.

### Scenario 2: Channel Conflict in Retail
**Situation:** A brand sells both online and through retailers. Online pricing is 12% lower, retailer sell-through drops, and partners begin threatening to reduce shelf space.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Channel design matrix + channel conflict management.

**Analysis:** The issue is not just price; it is channel trust. If the brand keeps undercutting partners, the retail channel will ration support. The business needs a pricing rule, not ad hoc exceptions.

**Decision rule:** If retailer margin falls below the agreed floor, fix pricing or create channel-exclusive bundles. If partner complaints repeat across more than one region, formalise channel governance.

**Action:** Reset the price architecture, offer retailer-only bundles, and publish one source of truth for pricing rules.

### Scenario 3: B2B Sales Motion Improvement
**Situation:** A consulting firm has 500 leads in CRM, but only 40 convert to proposals and 8 to deals. Objection handling is inconsistent and follow-up depends on individual reps.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Personal selling and CRM funnel.

**Analysis:** The funnel is leaking between pitch and close. If the CRM is not being used consistently, leadership cannot see where objections are recurring or which lead sources actually convert.

**Decision rule:** If lead-to-proposal conversion is below 15%, fix prospecting and qualification. If proposal-to-close is below 25%, coach objection handling and value proposition. If follow-up is weak, enforce CRM hygiene.

**Action:** Build a sales playbook with objection templates, mandatory CRM stages, and weekly pipeline coaching.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Decide whether the launch objective is adoption, margin, or signal before setting the first price.
2. Define a promotion policy with guardrails on discount depth, frequency, and duration.
3. Map each product to its primary channel, secondary channel, and conflict risk.
4. Create one pricing rule for online and offline so the channel does not fight itself.
5. Tie every sales rep to a CRM stage model and a weekly pipeline review.
6. Measure channel health using sell-through, margin, conversion, and return rates, not just top-line sales.
7. Review the pricing-and-channel architecture quarterly and adjust it as competition and adoption evolve.
