
# Product Portfolio, Product Mix, Product Lines

## Overview

This is about what products a company sells and how they organize them. A company may sell many products, in different categories, and needs to decide which to keep, improve, add, or remove.

## Why It Matters

Companies have limited money and time. Managing the portfolio well helps them focus on products that bring profit and fit customer needs, instead of wasting effort on weak products.

## Key Principles

- Keep a balanced mix (high profit + growth + stable products).
- Decide which products to expand, maintain, or drop.
- Manage product line length (too many options can confuse customers).
- Use data on sales, profit, and customer demand.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Product portfolio** | All products a company sells. |
| **Product mix** | The full set of product lines and items. |
| **Product line** | A group of related products (e.g., shampoos). |
| **SKU** | A specific item/variant (size/flavor/color). |
| **Line extension** | Adding a new variant to an existing line. |

## Use Case

A packaged foods company checks which biscuits sell well and which don’t, then decides to remove slow sellers and add a healthier variant.

## Scenario

> A snack brand sells 25 chips flavors. Stores complain about shelf space. The brand studies sales and finds 8 flavors make most revenue. They reduce to 15, improve best-sellers, and launch 2 new spicy variants.

## Examples

- Smartphone brand: Keeps premium and mid-range models, removes low-demand budget model.
- Soap brand: Adds “lemon fresh” variant to the existing soap line (line extension).

# Brand Building and Brand Relationship Management

## Overview

A brand is not just a logo—it’s what people think and feel about a product. Brand management is about creating a clear identity, building trust, and making customers stay loyal.

## Why It Matters

Strong brands can charge better prices, gain loyal customers, and survive competition. People often buy brands they trust, even if alternatives are cheaper.

## Key Principles

- Create a clear brand promise (what you stand for).
- Be consistent in quality, message, and experience.
- Build emotional connection (trust, pride, belonging).
- Manage brand meaning across ads, packaging, service, and social media.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Brand equity** | Extra value a brand name adds. |
| **Brand identity** | How the company wants to be seen. |
| **Brand image** | How customers actually see it. |
| **Positioning** | The “space” a brand owns in the customer’s mind. |
| **Loyalty** | Customers repeatedly choose the brand. |

## Use Case

A clothing brand builds a “sustainable fashion” identity and uses eco-friendly packaging, storytelling, and consistent messaging.

## Scenario

> A new coffee chain wants to be known for “premium, friendly, fast service.” They train staff, standardize store design, and reward repeat customers with a loyalty program.

## Examples

- Apple: Brand built around design, simplicity, premium feel.
- Amul: Trust and “everyday Indian” connection through consistent communication.

# New Product Development (NPD)

## Overview

NPD is the process of creating a new product or improving an existing one—from idea to launch. It reduces guesswork by testing what customers actually want.

## Why It Matters

New products help companies grow and stay relevant. But many new products fail, so a structured process saves money and reduces risk.

## Key Principles

- Start with real customer problem/need.
- Generate many ideas, then filter them.
- Test prototypes and concepts before full launch.
- Plan launch: price, promotion, distribution, and feedback loop.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Idea generation** | Creating product ideas. |
| **Concept testing** | Checking if customers like the idea. |
| **Prototype** | Early sample version. |
| **MVP (Minimum Viable Product)** | Basic version to test quickly. |
| **Commercialization** | Launching in the market. |

## Use Case

A dairy brand launches a “high-protein yogurt” after testing demand among gym-goers.

## Scenario

> A chocolate company plans a “low-sugar dark chocolate.” They test 3 recipes with customers, choose the best one, design packaging, and launch first in metro cities before expanding.

## Examples

- Maggi oats noodles: Extension based on health trend (some succeeded, some didn’t).
- A new UPI-based payment feature: MVP release, gather feedback, then improve.

# Managing Services and Service Enterprises

## Overview

Services are activities, not physical products (like banking, salons, hospitals). Managing services means delivering a good experience every time, even though services can vary by person and situation.

## Why It Matters

In services, customers judge you strongly on experience. Good service creates repeat customers, positive word-of-mouth, and long-term profit.

## Key Principles

- Service quality depends on people + process + environment.
- Focus on customer experience at every touchpoint.
- Train employees and measure satisfaction regularly.
- Build loyalty using service recovery (fixing issues fast).

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Service** | An intangible offering (cannot be touched). |
| **Service quality** | How well service meets expectations. |
| **Service-profit chain** | Better employees → better service → loyal customers → profit. |
| **Customer lifetime value (CLV)** | Total value a customer brings over time. |
| **Service recovery** | Actions to fix a service failure. |

## Use Case

A restaurant improves service speed and complaint handling to increase repeat visits.

## Scenario

> A café receives complaints about slow orders. They redesign the order process, add staff at peak hours, and give a free drink coupon if orders are delayed.

## Examples

- Starbucks: Consistent store experience + friendly service builds loyalty.
- Banks: Faster complaint resolution improves retention.

# Pricing Products and Services

## Overview

Pricing is deciding how much to charge. Good pricing considers costs, customer value, competition, and business goals.

## Why It Matters

Price directly impacts revenue and profit. Even a small pricing mistake can cause big losses or make customers leave.

## Key Principles

- Understand customer’s willingness to pay.
- Consider cost, but don’t price only based on cost.
- Watch competitor pricing and market position.
- Use price to support your brand (premium vs affordable).

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Cost-based pricing** | Price = cost + margin. |
| **Value-based pricing** | Price based on perceived value. |
| **Break-even** | Sales needed to cover costs. |
| **Price sensitivity** | How strongly customers react to price changes. |
| **Price elasticity** | Demand change due to price change. |

## Use Case

A gym sets membership fees based on value (equipment, trainers, location) not just rent and salaries.

## Scenario

> A company selling water purifiers finds customers pay more for “health and safety.” They price higher than competitors but add free installation and service to justify value.

## Examples

- Premium salon: Charges more due to experience and brand.
- Budget airline: Lower price, fewer free add-ons.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Product Portfolio Balance Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether the portfolio has the right mix of high-growth, high-profit, and stable products.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
             Growth
     High  |  Invest
           |
Profit ----+----------------
           |  Harvest/Defend
     Low   |
           Stable / Mature
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Growth - products that need funding and attention.
Component 2: Profit - products that fund the portfolio.
Component 3: Stability - products that hold the base business.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A software company may keep one cash-cow product, one growth product, and one stable legacy product while dropping small SKUs that create operational drag.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the portfolio is too broad and no one can say which items matter most.

### Brand Equity Loop
**Purpose:** Show how brand identity turns into brand image and then into loyalty.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Promise -> Experience -> Image -> Loyalty -> Repeat
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Brand identity - the promise the company wants to own.
Component 2: Brand image - what customers actually believe.
Component 3: Brand equity - the extra value created by trust and meaning.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A premium SaaS brand that consistently ships simple onboarding, helpful support, and reliable uptime creates an image of trust, which supports higher pricing and renewal rates.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When brand feels like just a logo refresh instead of a business asset.

### NPD Stage-Gate Funnel
**Purpose:** Move product ideas from concept to launch with controlled funding.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Idea -> Concept Test -> Prototype -> MVP -> Launch
  |         |             |        |        |
 Kill      Go/Kill      Go/Kill  Go/Kill   Scale
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Idea generation - generate many possibilities.
Component 2: Concept testing - see whether customers like the idea.
Component 3: Prototype/MVP - build the smallest useful version.
Component 4: Commercialization - launch after evidence is good enough.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A food company tests a high-protein yogurt concept, pilots a prototype with gym-goers, and then launches region by region after the test data is strong.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When an idea is being funded before customer validation.

### Service-Profit Chain
**Purpose:** Connect employee experience to customer experience and profit.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Employee capability -> Service quality -> Loyalty -> Profit
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Employee capability - training, tools, and process.
Component 2: Service quality - speed, consistency, and recovery.
Component 3: Loyalty - repeat purchase and retention.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A support center that shortens wait time and improves complaint handling increases repeat use, which lifts CLV and reduces churn.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When service is being treated as a soft issue rather than a revenue driver.

### Pricing Decision Tree
**Purpose:** Pick a pricing method that fits the product and market.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Cost-based -> floor price
Value-based -> customer willingness to pay
Competitive -> market position
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Cost-based pricing - protect margin floor.
Component 2: Value-based pricing - charge for perceived benefit.
Component 3: Competitive pricing - stay in the market band.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A premium analytics platform may set a value-based price if it clearly saves labor hours, while a commodity offer may need competitive pricing to stay in the funnel.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is pricing by habit instead of strategy.

## 4. Formulas
The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source]; the source names the concepts but does not state the math explicitly.

Formula: Break-even units = Fixed costs / (Price per unit - Variable cost per unit)
Variables:
Fixed costs = costs that do not change with units sold.
Price per unit = customer price.
Variable cost per unit = incremental cost to make or serve one unit.
Why this formula exists: It shows the sales volume needed before the product starts contributing profit.
How to interpret the output:
Value high -> the product needs scale -> launch carefully or increase price.
Value moderate -> manageable -> validate demand.
Value low -> easier to recover -> scale faster if demand is real.
Worked example with numbers: If fixed costs are $100,000, price is $50, and variable cost is $30, break-even is 5,000 units.

Formula: Gross margin % = ((Revenue - Cost of goods sold) / Revenue) x 100
Variables:
Revenue = total sales.
COGS = direct cost of producing or serving the product.
Why this formula exists: It tells you whether the product can fund marketing, support, and growth.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 20% -> margin pressure -> reconsider price or cost structure.
Value 20%-50% -> workable for many businesses -> monitor efficiency.
Value > 50% -> strong margin -> invest in growth and brand.
Worked example with numbers: If revenue is $200 and COGS is $120, gross margin = 40%.

Formula: Price elasticity = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price
Variables:
% change in quantity demanded = demand shift after price change.
% change in price = relative change in price.
Why this formula exists: It shows how sensitive customers are to price moves.
How to interpret the output:
Elasticity > 1 -> demand is sensitive -> price changes have big effects.
Elasticity around 1 -> balanced sensitivity -> test carefully.
Elasticity < 1 -> demand is less sensitive -> premium pricing may hold.
Worked example with numbers: If price rises 10% and units fall 5%, elasticity is 0.5, which means demand is relatively inelastic.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Keep every SKU because it has historical sales. | Prune weak products and protect the items that drive profit or growth. |
| Treat brand as a design exercise only. | Manage identity, image, experience, and trust as business assets. |
| Launch a new product without concept testing. | Test the idea, prototype, and MVP before scaling spend. |
| Price from cost alone. | Combine cost floor, perceived value, and competition in the pricing decision. |
| Ignore service recovery after a bad customer experience. | Fix the problem fast, because service recovery often determines loyalty. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: Portfolio cleanup
Situation: A snack brand has 25 SKUs, but 8 SKUs generate 78% of revenue and only 5 SKUs generate most of the gross margin. Retailers are complaining about shelf clutter.
Applicable framework/metric: Product portfolio balance matrix.
Analysis: The brand is carrying too many low-value variants. If the long tail consumes shelf space, production complexity, and promo spend without creating profit, it is a drag on the portfolio.
Decision rule: If a SKU contributes less than 2% of revenue and less than 1% of gross margin, consider dropping it; if it drives a segment or channel, keep it; if uncertain, test a line extension instead of a full launch.
Action: Cut the weakest SKUs, reinforce the winners, and introduce one high-potential variant with clean demand tracking.

Scenario 2: Brand repositioning
Situation: A coffee chain wants to move from "cheap and fast" to "premium, friendly, reliable." Customer survey data shows awareness is high, but image scores are inconsistent across stores.
Applicable framework/metric: Brand equity loop.
Analysis: The gap is not awareness; it is execution consistency. If the promise is premium but the service is variable, brand image will not support pricing power.
Decision rule: If image and promise match, raise price carefully; if image lags promise, fix the service experience before rebranding; if loyalty is weak, add retention programs.
Action: Standardize store experience, retrain staff, and align packaging, messaging, and service with the new positioning.

Scenario 3: NPD launch decision
Situation: A dairy company tests a high-protein yogurt with 3 prototypes. The best prototype scores 72% concept approval, 61% repeat intent, and a projected break-even of 40,000 units.
Applicable framework/metric: NPD stage-gate and break-even.
Analysis: The concept looks viable, but launch should stay phased if first-round channel orders are not strong enough to clear the break-even threshold.
Decision rule: If concept approval is above 65% and repeat intent above 55%, proceed to pilot; if concept approval is below 50%, stop; if approval is 50%-65%, redesign and retest.
Action: Pilot in metro stores, collect feedback, and only scale after repeat purchase and margin hold.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build a portfolio table showing each product, its line, its SKU count, and its profit contribution.
2. Set a review cadence for product keep, improve, extend, or drop decisions.
3. Write a brand promise statement and audit every touchpoint against it.
4. Run concept tests and MVP pilots before any major product launch.
5. Track service quality, service recovery, CLV, and complaint closure time.
6. Use a pricing worksheet that compares cost floor, value ceiling, and competitor bands.
7. Maintain one dashboard for portfolio, brand, NPD, service, and pricing decisions.
