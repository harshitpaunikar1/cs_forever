
# Consumer Behavior

## Overview

Consumer behavior explains why people buy—influenced by culture, friends, personality, emotions, and marketing.

## Why It Matters

If you know why customers choose a product, you can design better products and better messages.

## Key Principles

- People don’t always buy logically; emotions matter
- Social influence affects choices (family, friends, trends)
- Buying usually follows steps: need → search → compare → buy → experience
- Post-purchase experience impacts repeat buying

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Perception** | How people interpret information |
| **Motivation** | What drives action |
| **Heuristics** | Mental shortcuts for decisions |
| **Framing** | How wording changes decisions |
| **Postpurchase behavior** | Feelings after buying |

## Use Case

A phone brand highlights camera and “status” because that motivates its target users.

## Scenario

> A student wants headphones. They check reviews, compare prices, buy, then share feedback online.

## Examples

- Discounts triggering impulse buying at supermarkets.
- “Limited stock” messages increasing urgency.

# Business Markets & B2B Buying

## Overview

B2B marketing is when businesses sell to other businesses (machines, software, raw material). Buying decisions involve multiple people and approvals.

## Why It Matters

B2B deals are often large and long-term. Strong relationships and trust matter a lot.

## Key Principles

- Understand the customer company’s goals and pain points
- Identify the buying center (who influences the decision)
- Provide clear value and ROI
- Build long-term partnerships and service support

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Buying center** | Group involved in purchase decision |
| **Procurement** | Company buying process |
| **RFQ/RFP** | Request for quote/proposal |
| **Systems selling** | Selling a complete solution |
| **Key account management** | Handling major clients |

## Use Case

A software company sells ERP to a manufacturing firm with demos and ROI calculations.

## Scenario

> A factory needs new machines. Engineering, finance, and procurement evaluate options before selecting a supplier.

## Examples

- Steel supplier selling to construction companies with contracts.
- Cloud provider selling to banks with security guarantees.

# Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning (STP)

## Overview

STP means: divide the market into groups (segmentation), choose which group to serve (targeting), and create a clear image in their mind (positioning).

## Why It Matters

You can’t sell to everyone the same way. STP helps focus on the right customers and beat competitors.

## Key Principles

- Segment by geography, age, lifestyle, behavior, etc.
- Choose target segments that are profitable and reachable
- Position with a clear difference (why you are better)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Segmentation** | Dividing customers into groups |
| **Targeting** | Selecting the best group(s) |
| **Positioning** | Desired brand image in customer’s mind |
| **POD** | Point of difference (unique benefit) |
| **POP** | Point of parity (must-have features) |

## Use Case

A skincare brand targets teens with acne solutions and positions as “gentle and effective.”

## Scenario

> A sports drink targets gym-goers and positions as “fast hydration + electrolytes.”

## Examples

- Tata Nano originally targeted value-seeking families.
- iPhone positioned as premium, simple, high-status.

# Brand Equity

## Overview

Brand equity is the extra power a brand name has—people trust it, pay more for it, and choose it faster.

## Why It Matters

Strong brand equity increases profit and protects the business during competition or crises.

## Key Principles

- Choose strong brand elements (name, logo, colors)
- Deliver consistent experience
- Build associations (quality, trust, lifestyle)
- Measure brand strength and improve over time

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Brand equity** | Value created by brand strength |
| **Brand extension** | Using brand name for new products |
| **Brand portfolio** | Collection of brands owned |
| **Internal branding** | Employees living the brand |
| **Brand community** | Loyal customer group around a brand |

## Use Case

A dairy brand extends into cheese and yogurt using its trusted brand name.

## Scenario

> A trusted baby-care brand launches baby wipes. Parents try it quickly because they trust the brand.

## Examples

- Amul trusted for quality across many dairy products.
- Nike’s brand equity enables premium pricing.

# Brand Positioning

## Overview

Positioning is deciding what you want customers to remember about you—like “safest,” “cheapest,” or “most premium.”

## Why It Matters

Clear positioning prevents confusion. It helps customers choose you quickly.

## Key Principles

- Pick a clear competitive frame (who you compete with)
- Define PODs and POPs
- Keep message simple and consistent
- Use a brand mantra (short internal guiding phrase)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Frame of reference** | Category you compete in |
| **POD** | Unique reason to buy |
| **POP** | Expected features of the category |
| **Brand mantra** | Short internal slogan (focus) |

## Use Case

A toothpaste positions as “complete cavity protection” for families.

## Scenario

> Two cafés exist nearby. One positions as “fast and affordable,” the other as “premium experience.” Customers pick based on their need.

## Examples

- Volvo: safety positioning.
- Domino’s: quick delivery positioning.

# Competitive Dynamics

## Overview

This is about how companies compete—leaders defend, challengers attack, followers copy, and nichers focus on small markets.

## Why It Matters

Competition decides pricing, promotions, product changes, and survival. Knowing competitor type helps choose the right strategy.

## Key Principles

- Leaders protect share and expand market
- Challengers differentiate or attack weak points
- Followers copy with lower risk
- Nichers serve a specific small segment well
- Use product life cycle strategies (intro, growth, maturity, decline)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Market share** | % of total sales in category |
| **PLC** | Product Life Cycle |
| **Cannibalization** | New product eats old product sales |

## Use Case

A small brand targets a niche (organic snacks) instead of fighting mass-market giants.

## Scenario

> A shampoo leader launches a new variant to defend share when a challenger introduces “herbal formula.”

## Examples

- Budget smartphone brands challenging premium leaders with value.
- Niche coffee brands focusing only on specialty beans.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Consumer Decision Journey
**Purpose:** Map how a buyer moves from trigger to repeat use.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Trigger -> Need recognition -> Search -> Compare -> Buy -> Experience -> Repeat / Advocate
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Trigger and need recognition - the event, pain, or emotion that starts the journey.
Component 2: Search and comparison - where heuristics, framing, reviews, and social influence shape choice.
Component 3: Purchase and experience - the product, service, and onboarding reality the customer sees.
Component 4: Postpurchase behavior - satisfaction, repeat buying, and word-of-mouth.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI meeting-notes tool sees high demo traffic but weak trial conversion. The journey map shows that the message is attracting curious users, but the comparison stage fails because buyers do not see enough workflow value or trust. The decision is to tighten the proof points, improve onboarding, and shift the positioning from "smart notes" to "faster follow-up and compliance-safe summaries."
When to pull this out in a meeting: When awareness is fine but conversion or repeat use is weak.

### B2B Buying Center Map
**Purpose:** Identify who influences an enterprise purchase and what each role needs to say yes.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Users -> Influencers -> Buyers -> Approvers -> Procurement
            \-> Gatekeepers / Security / Legal
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Users - care about workflow fit, speed, and ease of adoption.
Component 2: Influencers - care about technical fit, security, integration, and ROI logic.
Component 3: Buyers and approvers - care about budget, risk, contracts, and governance.
Component 4: Procurement and gatekeepers - care about RFQ/RFP discipline, compliance, and vendor terms.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A mid-market bank evaluating an AI support assistant needs a pilot for end users, SOC 2 and data-residency proof for security, a business-case model for finance, and clean RFP responses for procurement. The decision is whether to build a tailored proposal or lose time with a generic pitch.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a deal stalls because "the decision maker" is actually a committee.

### STP Decision Funnel
**Purpose:** Turn a broad market into a focused customer choice and a clear message.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
All customers -> Segments -> Target segment(s) -> Positioning statement
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Segmentation - grouping by geography, need, behavior, use case, or firmographic fit.
Component 2: Targeting - selecting the segment with the best fit, value, and reach.
Component 3: Positioning - defining the frame of reference, POD, POP, and brand mantra.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A workflow automation product can segment by company size, compliance load, and process complexity. It may target regulated mid-market service firms and position itself as "secure automation with human control," which drives a security-first feature roadmap and a narrower sales motion.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the product is "for everyone" and the message is not landing.

### Brand Equity Flywheel
**Purpose:** Show how a promise becomes trust, loyalty, and pricing power.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Brand identity -> Consistent experience -> Brand image -> Loyalty -> Brand equity -> Premium / Extension
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Brand identity - the promise the company wants to own.
Component 2: Brand image - what customers actually believe after use and communication.
Component 3: Loyalty and trust - repeat buying, lower skepticism, and stronger advocacy.
Component 4: Brand extension and portfolio - whether the name can credibly stretch into new offers.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm with consistent delivery, reusable templates, and disciplined follow-up creates trust that makes it easier to launch adjacent advisory services. The decision is whether the brand is strong enough to extend or should stay focused to avoid dilution.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone treats branding as a design task instead of a commercial asset.

### Competitive Dynamics Ladder
**Purpose:** Choose the right competitive posture based on role and product life cycle.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Leader -> defends share
Challenger -> attacks weak spots
Follower -> copies efficiently
Nicher -> serves a narrow segment

PLC: Intro -> Growth -> Maturity -> Decline
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Leader - protects category position with innovation, distribution, and defense.
Component 2: Challenger - attacks on value, features, pricing, or service gaps.
Component 3: Follower - reduces risk by imitating with lower cost and fewer mistakes.
Component 4: Nicher - wins by depth in a small, specific segment.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS vendor facing a dominant incumbent can use a challenger offer with stronger onboarding and lower entry price, then build a niche compliance package for a regulated segment. The decision is whether to fight for broad share or win a narrow segment first.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When competitor moves force a response on pricing, product scope, or messaging.

## 4. Formulas
The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source]; the source names the concepts but does not state the math explicitly.

Formula: Market share % = (brand sales / category sales) x 100
Variables:
Brand sales = your sales in the category.
Category sales = total category sales.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether positioning and distribution are winning relative to competitors.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 10% -> niche or weak share -> sharpen the segment choice and improve distribution.
Value 10%-25% -> challenger share -> press a differentiated message and stronger sales motion.
Value > 25% -> leader share -> defend, refresh, and watch for cannibalization.
Worked example with numbers: If your AI tool sells $4 million in a $50 million category, market share is 8%. That says the business is still niche, so the team should focus on one segment instead of broadening too early.

Formula: Repeat purchase rate % = (repeat buyers / total buyers) x 100
Variables:
Repeat buyers = customers who bought again in the period.
Total buyers = all buyers in the same base period.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the customer experience and brand promise are strong enough to bring people back.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 20% -> weak loyalty -> fix onboarding, service, and product fit.
Value 20%-40% -> mixed loyalty -> improve postpurchase communication and use cases.
Value > 40% -> strong loyalty -> invest in referrals, upsell, and brand extension.
Worked example with numbers: If 300 of 900 customers repurchase, the repeat purchase rate is 33.3%. That suggests the offer has traction, but retention work is still needed before scaling acquisition spend.

Formula: Cannibalization rate % = (lost sales from old offer / sales of new offer) x 100
Variables:
Lost sales from old offer = sales shifted away from an existing product.
Sales of new offer = sales generated by the new product or variant.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether a launch is creating net growth or just moving demand around inside the portfolio.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 10% -> low cannibalization -> launch is likely additive.
Value 10%-30% -> manageable substitution -> monitor margin and channel mix.
Value > 30% -> high cannibalization -> redesign the offer or narrow the target.
Worked example with numbers: If a new premium plan creates $500,000 of sales but $180,000 comes from customers who would have stayed on the old plan, cannibalization is 36%. The team should test whether the new plan still improves total margin and retention.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Market to everyone with one generic pitch. | Segment by need, behavior, and firmographics, then target one valuable group first. |
| Treat the buyer as one person in B2B. | Map the buying center and tailor proof for users, influencers, approvers, and procurement. |
| Position only on features. | Anchor a clear frame of reference, POD, and acceptable POPs. |
| Treat brand equity as a logo or campaign. | Manage identity, image, service consistency, and internal branding as one system. |
| Launch a new variant without checking substitution. | Measure cannibalization and decide whether the net result is growth or self-competition. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: Enterprise AI procurement
Situation: A product team is selling an AI support assistant into a bank. The sales cycle is 9 weeks, 11 stakeholders are involved, and the pilot has a 72% user satisfaction score but a stalled security review.
Applicable framework/metric: B2B Buying Center Map and proposal-to-close conversion.
Analysis: The user case is strong, but the security gatekeeper has not been addressed. If stakeholder coverage is 11/11 but the security response is still pending, the deal is at risk even with good user feedback.
Decision rule: If the security review is open, fix the evidence pack first; if the pilot satisfaction is above 70% and procurement asks for pricing, move to commercial negotiation; if only users are positive, do not forecast the deal as won.
Action: Build a one-page security summary, a finance ROI sheet, and a role-specific proposal for users, IT, finance, and procurement.

Scenario 2: STP for a workflow SaaS launch
Situation: A startup has 3 possible customer groups: freelancers, SMB service firms, and regulated mid-market companies. Conversion is 1.2% for freelancers, 3.8% for SMB service firms, and 6.4% for regulated mid-market accounts.
Applicable framework/metric: STP Decision Funnel and conversion rate.
Analysis: The regulated segment converts 5.3x better than freelancers and 1.7x better than SMB service firms. That suggests the product and message are closer to the regulated use case than the broad market.
Decision rule: If a segment converts at least 2x better than the base segment and has acceptable CAC payback, prioritize it; if it converts only marginally better, keep testing; if it underperforms, stop spending on that segment.
Action: Narrow the GTM message to compliance-heavy teams, add a security proof point, and build one case study that matches the target segment.

Scenario 3: Brand defense against a cheaper challenger
Situation: A software category leader has 31% market share and 46% repeat purchase rate. A challenger launches a cheaper plan that is causing 14% churn in one legacy tier.
Applicable framework/metric: Competitive Dynamics Ladder, market share, repeat purchase rate, and cannibalization.
Analysis: The leader is healthy overall, but the churn in one tier shows a local weakness. If the cheaper plan is taking customers from the old tier rather than growing the category, the team is fighting substitution, not just competition.
Decision rule: If churn in one tier is above 10%, respond with packaging or retention changes; if repeat purchase stays above 40%, protect the base and up-sell; if cannibalization rises above 30% on a new plan, redesign the offer.
Action: Tighten packaging, improve renewal offers, and monitor the churned tier separately from the rest of the portfolio.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build a customer evidence file that captures trigger, pain point, objection, and postpurchase behavior for each target segment.
2. Map the buying center for every enterprise opportunity and attach one proof artifact to each stakeholder role.
3. Write one segmentation worksheet with segment size, urgency, fit, CAC, and expected repeat rate.
4. Draft a positioning statement with frame of reference, POD, POP, and a short brand mantra.
5. Set a brand dashboard that tracks awareness, trust, repeat purchase, and message consistency across touchpoints.
6. Add a competition tracker for leader, challenger, follower, and nicher moves, plus any launch-induced cannibalization.
7. Review pricing, packaging, and product extension decisions against market share and retention before scaling spend.
