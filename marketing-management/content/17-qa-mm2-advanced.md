## Q142. Analyze the ethical risks of using behavioral targeting and personalization in digital marketing.

**Level:** Advanced

Behavioral targeting can improve relevance, but it raises ethical risks like privacy invasion, manipulation, discrimination, and lack of informed consent. When users do not understand how data is collected or used, trust weakens. Ethical practice requires transparency, limited data collection, fairness, and meaningful user control. Over-personalization can also create “filter bubbles” that restrict exposure to alternatives.

**Real-life applications:**

- Platforms must avoid targeting vulnerable users with harmful products.
- Banks and insurers must prevent discriminatory targeting based on sensitive traits.
- Universities should not misuse student data for aggressive remarketing.
- Governments must ensure citizen data is not used to influence behavior unfairly.

**Key concepts:** `Behavioral Targeting` `Privacy` `Consent` `Manipulation` `Fairness`

## Q143. Evaluate the limitations of SWOT analysis for making strategic marketing decisions.

**Level:** Advanced

SWOT is useful for organizing thinking, but it can be subjective and oversimplify complex realities. It often lists factors without showing their importance, trade-offs, or how they interact. SWOT can also encourage “wishful thinking” if not supported by evidence. Strong strategy requires deeper analysis, prioritization, and clear choices, not just a long SWOT list.

**Real-life applications:**

- Startups may overestimate strengths without market proof.
- Retailers may list “competition” as a threat but ignore specific competitor advantages.
- Universities may assume reputation strength without measuring applicant perceptions.
- Public programs may list opportunities but underestimate operational constraints and politics.

**Key concepts:** `SWOT Limitations` `Subjectivity` `Prioritization` `Strategy Trade-offs` `Evidence-Based Planning`

## Q144. How can firms measure brand equity beyond awareness and recall, and why is that important?

**Level:** Advanced

Beyond awareness, firms measure brand equity through price premium willingness, loyalty, perceived quality, strong associations, repeat purchase, advocacy, and resilience during crises. This is important because awareness alone does not guarantee preference or profitability. A widely known brand can still be weak if customers do not trust it or feel it delivers value.

**Real-life applications:**

- Brands compare price premium vs generic alternatives.
- They track repeat purchase and referral rates as equity signals.
- Universities track employer preference and alumni advocacy.
- Public institutions measure trust and compliance, not just visibility of campaigns.

**Key concepts:** `Brand Equity` `Price Premium` `Associations` `Loyalty` `Brand Resilience`

## Q145. Assess the strategic risks of brand extensions when a brand’s meaning is unclear or too broad.

**Level:** Advanced

When brand meaning is unclear, extensions may confuse customers and dilute brand identity. If the brand stands for “everything,” it may stand for “nothing,” weakening differentiation. Broad extensions can also create quality doubts and reduce trust in the core product. Successful extension requires clear associations, credible fit, and consistent quality across offerings.

**Real-life applications:**

- A premium brand launching too many unrelated low-price items can lose status.
- A trusted food brand extending into unrelated categories may reduce confidence.
- Universities adding unrelated programs can weaken core academic identity.
- Public portals adding too many features without clarity can reduce usability and trust.

**Key concepts:** `Brand Extension Risk` `Brand Dilution` `Fit` `Brand Meaning` `Confusion`

## Q146. Debate whether price should be treated mainly as a “revenue tool” or a “brand signal.”

**Level:** Advanced

Price is both a revenue tool and a brand signal. As a revenue tool, it must cover costs and support profit objectives. As a brand signal, it influences perceived quality, fairness, and positioning. Over-focusing on revenue can harm trust if customers feel exploited, while over-focusing on signaling can reduce competitiveness. Strong pricing balances financial goals with long-term brand meaning and customer value perception.

**Real-life applications:**

- Luxury brands maintain high prices to protect status.
- Budget brands use low pricing to signal affordability and mass access.
- Universities signal prestige through pricing but must justify outcomes and value.
- Public services balance affordability with sustainability and fairness perceptions.

**Key concepts:** `Pricing Signal` `Revenue Management` `Positioning` `Perceived Value` `Price Fairness`

## Q147. Critically analyze promotional pricing as a strategic tool: when does it create value and when does it destroy it?

**Level:** Advanced

Promotional pricing creates value when it supports trial, clears excess inventory, or stimulates demand without damaging long-term perception. It destroys value when it becomes frequent, trains customers to wait for discounts, reduces margins, and weakens brand quality cues. The key is strategic use with clear objectives, controlled frequency, and measurement of profit impact, not just sales volume.

**Real-life applications:**

- New products use introductory discounts to encourage trial.
- Retailers use promotions to move seasonal stock before expiry.
- Universities may use scholarships strategically, but constant fee discounting harms credibility.
- Public incentives must avoid dependency and ensure equitable access.

**Key concepts:** `Promotional Pricing` `Brand Dilution` `Stockpiling` `Margin Erosion` `Trial Strategy`

## Q149. Analyze how platform-based business models shift traditional STP (segmentation, targeting, positioning) logic.

**Level:** Advanced

Platforms serve multiple user groups, so STP must consider both sides and the interactions between them. Targeting one group without attracting the other can fail. Positioning must communicate trust, safety, and value for each side. Platform strategy often prioritizes building network effects first, then refining segmentation and monetization later.

**Real-life applications:**

- Ride apps must attract drivers and riders simultaneously.
- Marketplaces must target reliable sellers and value-seeking buyers.
- Job platforms must balance employer needs and candidate experience.
- Public platforms must balance citizen convenience and departmental compliance requirements.

**Key concepts:** `Platforms` `Two-Sided Markets` `Network Effects` `Multi-Segment Positioning` `Ecosystems`

## Q150. Critique the idea that “more channels always mean more sales.”

**Level:** Advanced

More channels can increase reach, but they can also create complexity, higher costs, inconsistent experiences, and channel conflict. If pricing and service differ across channels, trust can drop. Sales may shift from one channel to another without increasing total demand, causing cannibalization. Channel strategy should prioritize customer experience, profitability, and alignment, not just channel count.

**Real-life applications:**

- Brands lose retailer support when online undercuts store pricing.
- Customers get frustrated when returns are not accepted across channels.
- Universities confuse applicants when agents and websites provide conflicting information.
- Governments reduce adoption when portals and help centers give different guidance.

**Key concepts:** `Multichannel Risk` `Cannibalization` `Channel Conflict` `Consistency` `Profitability`

## Q151. How can firms manage channel conflict without harming customer convenience?

**Level:** Advanced

Firms can manage conflict by setting clear channel roles, ensuring consistent pricing policies, using exclusive bundles for specific channels, sharing incentives fairly, and integrating customer service across channels. The goal is to protect partner economics while still offering customer-friendly options like returns, warranties, and support. Strong communication and data sharing reduce misunderstandings.

**Real-life applications:**

- Electronics brands offer store-only service packages while maintaining online availability.
- Retailers receive co-op advertising support to stay motivated.
- Universities standardize messaging across agents and official channels.
- Governments unify grievance tracking across online and offline service points.

**Key concepts:** `Channel Governance` `Role Clarity` `Incentives` `Policy Consistency` `Omnichannel Service`

## Q152. Evaluate whether “service standardization” is always desirable in service marketing.

**Level:** Advanced

Standardization improves reliability and reduces variability, but too much standardization can reduce personalization and empathy, which many services require. The best approach standardizes core processes (safety, hygiene, timelines) while allowing flexibility in human interaction and problem-solving. Service excellence often combines consistent systems with personalized care.

**Real-life applications:**

- Hospitals standardize safety protocols but personalize patient communication.
- Hotels standardize check-in but adapt to guest needs.
- Universities standardize evaluation rules but offer personalized mentoring.
- Public offices standardize procedures while allowing special support for vulnerable citizens.

**Key concepts:** `Service Standardization` `Variability` `Personalization` `Service Quality` `Process Design`

## Q154. Critically evaluate Net Promoter Score (NPS) as a loyalty metric.

**Level:** Advanced

NPS is simple and useful for tracking advocacy trends, but it can oversimplify loyalty and ignore context. Customers may recommend a brand for one reason yet still switch later, and cultural differences can affect scoring. NPS should be combined with behavioral data like repeat purchases, churn rates, and complaint patterns for a fuller view.

**Real-life applications:**

- Service firms track NPS but also monitor retention and complaint resolution time.
- Hospitals combine patient feedback with readmission and follow-up rates.
- Universities combine satisfaction surveys with alumni engagement and referrals.
- Public services combine citizen feedback with usage and completion rates.

**Key concepts:** `NPS` `Loyalty Measurement` `Advocacy` `Churn` `Metrics Limitations`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Ethical Targeting Guardrail
**Purpose:** Decide whether personalization is helpful, acceptable, and defensible.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Data collected -> Consent? -> Fair? -> Transparent? -> Target or stop
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Consent - whether the user actually agreed to the data use.
Component 2: Fairness - whether the tactic avoids discrimination or exploitation.
Component 3: Transparency - whether the customer can understand what is happening.
Component 4: Vulnerability - whether the target group needs extra protection.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A fintech app uses behavioral targeting to push credit offers to users showing cash-flow stress. If consent is weak and the model disproportionately targets vulnerable users, the tactic is not just inefficient, it is risky. The decision is to tighten opt-in rules, exclude sensitive inferences, and add a human review step before launch.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When personalization looks profitable but feels legally or ethically shaky.

## 4. Formulas
The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source]; the source names the concepts but does not state the math explicitly.

Formula: NPS = % promoters - % detractors
Variables:
Promoters = customers rating the brand highly and willing to recommend it.
Detractors = customers rating the brand poorly.
Why this formula exists: It gives a quick read on advocacy and loyalty sentiment.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> warning sign -> fix the product or service before scaling.
Value 0 to 30 -> mixed loyalty -> improve the experience and repeat purchase drivers.
Value > 30 -> strong advocacy -> use for growth, referrals, and community building.
Worked example with numbers: If 55% are promoters and 18% are detractors, NPS is 37. That is a healthy signal, but it still needs to be checked against churn and repeat behavior.

Formula: Churn rate % = (customers lost during period / customers at start) x 100
Variables:
Customers lost during period = accounts or users that left.
Customers at start = active base at the start of the same period.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether trust, value, or pricing is breaking down.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 5% -> strong retention -> protect and expand.
Value 5%-10% -> manageable leakage -> investigate by segment.
Value > 10% -> serious problem -> fix pricing, product, or service quickly.
Worked example with numbers: If a subscription business starts with 10,000 users and loses 700 in a quarter, churn is 7%. That suggests a retention issue that needs segment-level diagnosis, not just more acquisition spend.

Formula: Price premium % = ((brand price - reference price) / reference price) x 100
Variables:
Brand price = your price.
Reference price = the comparator or expected market price.
Why this formula exists: It answers how far the price sits above the customer's mental benchmark.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> below market -> bargain signal -> watch margin and positioning.
Value 0 to 15 -> moderate premium -> defend with clear value proof.
Value > 15 -> high premium -> ensure the brand can justify the gap.
Worked example with numbers: If the reference price is $100 and your brand price is $120, the premium is 20%. That only works if the customer sees a clear reason to pay more.

Scenario 2: Pricing a premium AI workflow product
Situation: A B2B software firm launches a secure AI assistant at a 22% premium to the nearest reference product. Early trial is decent, but sales keep hearing "too expensive" and churn appears in the smallest customer segment.
Applicable framework/metric: Price Signal And Fairness Map and price premium %.
Analysis: The premium is not automatically wrong; the issue is whether value proof is strong enough. If small customers see no extra benefit, the premium becomes a fairness problem rather than a brand signal.
Decision rule: If price premium exceeds 15% and the value case is not clear, simplify packaging or add proof points; if churn rises above 10% in the target segment, reassess segmentation or price tiering.
Action: Rework the plan architecture, create a concrete ROI story, and reserve the premium offer for segments that value security and workflow automation.

Scenario 3: Channel conflict and brand resilience
Situation: A consumer electronics brand sells via its site, marketplaces, and retail partners. Market share is up to 14%, but retailer complaints are increasing, the brand's NPS has fallen from 41 to 29, and a discount-heavy marketplace campaign is driving 12% cannibalization.
Applicable framework/metric: Channel Governance And Platform Power Matrix, Brand Resilience Flywheel, and NPS.
Analysis: The growth is not clean. If the channel conflict is causing partner erosion and the NPS decline is not offset by retention, the strategy is weakening long-term trust.
Decision rule: If cannibalization is above 10% and NPS falls below 30, re-price or reassign channel roles; if partners push back, tighten governance before expanding spend.
Action: Rationalize channel pricing, improve retailer economics, and shift more spend to channels that preserve trust and margin.
