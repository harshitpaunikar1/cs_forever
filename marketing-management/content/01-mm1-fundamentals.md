
# Marketing Management Process

## Overview

Marketing management is how a company finds customers, understands them, creates value for them, and keeps them satisfied—step by step.

## Why It Matters

If a company markets randomly, it wastes money and time. A clear process helps the business choose the right customers, offer the right product, and grow in a planned way.

## Key Principles

- Start with customer needs, not just the product
- Decide who you want to serve and how you will serve them
- Create value, deliver it, then improve based on feedback
- Marketing is ongoing, not a one-time activity

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Market** | Group of people with similar needs and ability to buy |
| **Value** | Benefits customers get compared to what they pay |
| **Exchange** | Giving something to get something (money for product) |
| **Marketing mix** | Tools used to market (4Ps) |

## Use Case

A startup launching a healthy snack uses a marketing process to decide the right audience, price, place to sell, and promotion plan.

## Scenario

> A new juice brand first studies what health-conscious customers want, then designs flavors, prices them reasonably, sells in gyms/online, and promotes with fitness influencers.

## Examples

- A mobile brand studies student needs → launches a budget phone with long battery.
- A bakery notices demand for eggless cakes → adds eggless options and promotes them.

# Opportunity Analysis (Marketing Myopia & Customer Focus)

## Overview

Opportunity analysis means finding a good business chance by understanding what customers really need and where the market is changing.

## Why It Matters

Many businesses fail because they focus only on their current product and ignore changing customer needs. Opportunity analysis helps a company stay relevant and discover growth areas.

## Key Principles

- Look for unmet needs and problems customers face
- Focus on customer benefit, not just your product category
- Check if the opportunity is big enough and profitable
- Watch trends (technology, lifestyle, income, etc.)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Marketing myopia** | Being too focused on your product, not customer needs |
| **Unmet need** | Problem customers still struggle with |
| **Value proposition** | Simple promise of value to customers |
| **Trend** | Long-term change in customer behavior or environment |

## Use Case

A taxi company sees people want convenience and cashless payment → builds an app-based booking system.

## Scenario

> A DVD rental store keeps pushing DVDs while customers shift to streaming. A smart opportunity analysis would shift them to digital subscriptions earlier.

## Examples

- A notebook company expands into “study productivity tools” (planners, apps).
- A gym adds online classes when customers want home workouts.

# Environment Scanning for Marketing Decisions (Macro + Micro)

## Overview

Environment scanning means watching what’s happening outside and inside the market so marketing decisions don’t become outdated.

## Why It Matters

Markets change fast—new laws, new tech, new competitors, new customer habits. Scanning helps a company avoid surprises and grab new opportunities early.

## Key Principles

- Scan macro environment (big forces) and micro environment (near forces)
- Use frameworks like PESTEL and 5Cs
- Identify threats early and prepare responses
- Update scanning regularly, not once a year

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Macro environment** | Big external forces (PESTEL) |
| **Micro environment** | Customers, competitors, suppliers, partners |
| **PESTEL** | Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal |
| **SWOT** | Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats |

## Use Case

A cosmetics company tracks regulations on chemicals and shifts to safer ingredients before competitors.

## Scenario

> A phone company notices rising import duties (macro) and supplier delays (micro), so it plans local sourcing and adjusts pricing.

## Examples

- A café sees a health trend → adds low-sugar drinks (social factor).
- A retailer sees competitor discounting → runs a loyalty program (competitive factor).

# Competitive Dynamics (Competitor Analysis & Strategies)

## Overview

Competitive dynamics is about understanding competitors and deciding how to win when others are trying to win the same customers.

## Why It Matters

Competitors can copy features, cut prices, or grab your customers. If you don’t plan for competition, your sales and brand can fall quickly.

## Key Principles

- Identify direct and indirect competitors
- Compare on price, quality, service, brand, distribution
- Choose a clear competitive approach (differentiate, focus, cost)
- Predict competitor reactions before launching big moves

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Competitor analysis** | Studying competitors’ strengths and moves |
| **Differentiation** | Being meaningfully different (not just “better”) |
| **Cost leadership** | Competing mainly with low cost/low price |
| **Market leader/challenger/follower/nicher** | Roles firms play in markets |

## Use Case

An airline uses competitor analysis to decide whether to compete on low price or better service.

## Scenario

> A new food delivery app enters a city where two big apps exist. It chooses a niche: “10-minute delivery for essentials” and partners with local stores.

## Examples

- A shampoo brand adds anti-dandruff + herbal positioning to stand apart.
- A budget airline reduces extras and competes on low fares.

# Understanding Customer Buying Behavior (B2C)

## Overview

B2C buying behavior explains how individual customers decide what to buy, why they buy, and what influences their choice.

## Why It Matters

If you know how customers decide, you can design better products, better ads, and better shopping experiences—leading to more sales and loyalty.

## Key Principles

- Customers go through steps: need → search → compare → buy → after-buy feelings
- Emotions and habits matter, not just logic
- Culture, family, friends, income, lifestyle influence decisions
- Post-purchase experience affects repeat buying and word-of-mouth

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Need recognition** | Customer realizes a problem |
| **Information search** | Looking for options (online/offline) |
| **Evaluation of alternatives** | Comparing brands |
| **Post-purchase behavior** | Satisfaction, regret, reviews |

## Use Case

A smartphone brand designs ads differently for first-time buyers vs upgrade buyers.

## Scenario

> A student’s phone breaks (need). They check YouTube reviews (search), compare models (evaluate), buy during a sale (purchase), then post an unboxing review (post-purchase).

## Examples

- Buying sneakers: influenced by friends, Instagram, comfort, price.
- Buying toothpaste: often habit-based unless a problem (sensitivity) happens.

# Understanding Customer Buying Behavior (B2B) + DMU

## Overview

B2B buying behavior explains how organizations buy (companies buying from companies). Decisions are usually made by a group, not one person.

## Why It Matters

B2B deals are often high value and long-term. If you don’t understand the decision-making unit (DMU), you may convince one person but still lose the deal.

## Key Principles

- B2B buying is more logical, detailed, and process-based
- Many roles influence the decision (users, buyers, finance, tech, boss)
- Relationships, trust, and service matter a lot
- Purchasing follows stages: need → specs → suppliers → proposals → selection

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **DMU / Buying center** | Group involved in purchase decision |
| **User** | People who will use the product |
| **Influencer** | People who set specs/requirements (e.g., IT team) |
| **Gatekeeper** | Controls access to decision makers (e.g., admin/procurement) |

## Use Case

A software company selling HR software maps the DMU: HR head (user), IT (influencer), finance (approver), CEO (final approval).

## Scenario

> A hospital wants new patient management software. Doctors want ease (users), IT wants security (influencers), procurement wants low price (buyers), management wants ROI (deciders).

## Examples

- A factory buys machines: engineers + purchase team + owner decide together.
- A company chooses an ad agency: marketing team + finance + CEO approve.

# Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning (STP) + Intro to 4Ps

## Overview

STP means: Segmentation: Split the market into groups Targeting: Choose which group(s) to serve Positioning: Decide what you want people to think about your brand Then you use 4Ps to deliver it: Product, Price, Place, Promotion.

## Why It Matters

Not everyone is your customer. STP helps you focus on the right people and stand out clearly in their minds—so your marketing becomes more effective and less wasteful.

## Key Principles

- Segments must be meaningful and reachable
- Target based on size, profitability, and fit with your strengths
- Position using clear benefits and difference vs competitors
- Make sure 4Ps match your chosen position

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Segment** | Customer group with similar needs |
| **Target market** | Segment you choose to serve |
| **Positioning statement** | Short line explaining who, what, why you’re different |
| **POD/POP** | Points-of-Difference / Points-of-Parity |

## Use Case

A smartwatch brand targets fitness beginners and positions as “simple health tracking at an affordable price,” then sets 4Ps accordingly.

## Scenario

> A skincare company splits market into teens (acne), adults (glow), older (anti-aging). It targets teens and positions as “fast acne control without harsh chemicals,” then uses influencer-led promotion and pharmacy distribution.

## Examples

- A car brand targets families → positions on safety + space → promotes family trips.
- A coffee chain targets working professionals → positions on “quick premium coffee” → places outlets near offices.

# Marketing Research + MR vs MIS

## Overview

Marketing research is a planned way to collect information so a company can make better marketing decisions (like what product to launch or what price to set).

## Why It Matters

Without research, decisions become guesses. Research reduces risk, helps understand customers better, and supports smarter planning.

## Key Principles

- Start with a clear problem/question
- Choose the right method (survey, interview, observation, experiments)
- Collect data carefully and avoid bias
- Convert findings into actionable decisions

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Primary data** | New data collected for your specific problem |
| **Secondary data** | Already available data (reports, articles, company records) |
| **Marketing Research (MR)** | Project-based study for a decision/problem |
| **Marketing Information System (MIS)** | Ongoing system that stores and shares marketing data |

## Use Case

A food brand tests two flavors with a survey and small market trial before full launch.

## Scenario

> A company sees falling sales. Research shows customers think the product is “too expensive for the value,” so the firm changes packaging size and runs a value-focused campaign.

## Examples

- A restaurant surveys customers → adds more vegetarian options.
- An e-commerce app A/B tests two homepages → keeps the one with higher conversion.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Framework 1: STP + 4Ps Alignment Matrix
**Purpose:** Ensure 4Ps match chosen positioning — or they cancel out.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 Segment (Target)          │ Positioning                    │ 4Ps must support
 ─────────────────────── ─┼────────────────────────────── ─┼──────────────────────
 Premium professionals     │ "Fastest AI coding assistant" │ Product: top model
                          │                               │ Price: premium
                          │                               │ Place: direct enterprise
                          │                               │ Promo: developer advocacy
 Mid-market SMBs           │ "Affordable AI productivity"  │ Product: simplified
                          │                               │ Price: freemium
                          │                               │ Place: self-serve
                          │                               │ Promo: content + community
 Price-sensitive students  │ "Free AI study tool"         │ Product: light version
                          │                               │ Price: free or ~$5/mo
                          │                               │ Place: app stores
                          │                               │ Promo: viral + campus

 Rule: each P consistent with positioning; inconsistent → confused customer.
```

Components:
- Align target × positioning × each of 4Ps
- Mismatches = wasted marketing

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** AI SaaS has premium positioning but freemium pricing. Wealthy enterprise buyers confused — why is the "premium" product giving it away? Misalignment. Fix: tier clearly: free (students), Pro (prosumers), Enterprise (premium).

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** New-product launch; pricing reviews; positioning debates.

---

### Framework 2: 5Cs Marketing Environmental Scan
**Purpose:** Cover all 5 Cs to avoid blindspots.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 CUSTOMERS              COMPANY                  COMPETITORS
 - Segments             - Capabilities           - Direct
 - Needs / JTBD         - Resources              - Indirect
 - WTP                  - Brand equity           - Substitutes
 - Buying behaviour     - Strategy               - Positioning
                        
 COLLABORATORS          CONTEXT (PESTEL)
 - Channel partners     - Political
 - Suppliers            - Economic
 - Platform ecosystem   - Social
                        - Technological
                        - Environmental
                        - Legal
```

Components:
- 5 categories; miss any = blindspot
- More comprehensive than just "know your customers"

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** AI product scan:
- Customers: BFSI mid-market; need compliance-grade AI
- Company: 25 engineers; strong ML team; weak on enterprise security
- Competitors: 2 direct, 3 indirect, open-source substitutes
- Collaborators: Cloud provider MDF; system integrators
- Context: EU AI Act rising (Political/Legal); cost of inference falling (Tech)

Missing any of these 5 → bad GTM decisions.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Any new product / market entry planning.

### Framework 3: DMU Mapping (B2B)
**Purpose:** Influence every role in the buying centre.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 Role            │ What they care about          │ Marketing to them
 ──────────────┼──────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────
 Initiator       │ Solving a specific problem    │ Educational content
 User            │ Day-to-day ease of use        │ Product demos
 Influencer      │ Technical specs + fit         │ Whitepapers; eval docs
 Buyer            │ Price, contract terms         │ ROI calculators
 Decider          │ Strategic impact, ROI         │ Case studies; exec briefs
 Gatekeeper      │ Access control; compliance   │ Security documents

 Insight: most B2B losses = missed influencer or decider (not user).
```

Components:
- 6 roles; each has different interest
- Content and touchpoints tailored per role

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** AI SaaS selling to enterprise: user loves it (PLG trial); but security team (gatekeeper) blocks; CFO (decider) asks for ROI case. Win requires: security brief + ROI calculator + exec briefing alongside user love.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** B2B sales enablement; enterprise marketing design.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Segmentation Viability Test
**Formula:** Segment passes if: **Measurable + Accessible + Substantial + Differentiable + Actionable** (MADSA criteria)

**Worked example:**
- "AI-interested developers" — Measurable (GitHub tags), Accessible (ads, communities), Substantial (millions), Differentiable (vs general dev), Actionable (we can serve them). PASS.
- "Purple-car-owning yogis" — niche and un-targetable. FAIL.

**Data source:** Segment-design workshops.

---

### Formula 2: Perceptual Map Coordinates
**Formula:** Plot brands on 2 differentiation dimensions; find white space.

**Worked example:** Axes: Quality (high/low) × Price (high/low). Plot competitors. Find white space "high quality + reasonable price" → positioning opportunity.

**Data source:** Customer surveys; competitor pricing audit.

---

### Formula 3: Positioning Statement (Formula)
**Formula:** `For [target segment], our [product] is the [category] that [POD] because [reason to believe].`

**Worked example:** "For mid-market developers, our AI coding assistant is the only tool that runs fully on-premise without cloud data exposure because we use distilled open-source models deployed locally."

**Data source:** STP workshop; customer research.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Start with product | Start with customer need / JTBD |
| Target "everyone" | Pick specific segments; exclude others explicitly |
| Skip macro scan | PESTEL + 5Cs before strategy |
| Treat competitors as noise | Systematic competitor analysis |
| Focus only on decision-maker in B2B | Map + influence full DMU |
| Positioning without POD + POP | Both required for meaningful positioning |
| Use 4Ps inconsistently with positioning | STP dictates 4Ps — alignment matters |
| Rely only on MR (projects) | Build MIS (continuous infrastructure) |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: AI Product Launching with Misaligned 4Ps
**Situation:** AI startup priced enterprise but sold self-serve; result: confusion.

**Applicable framework/metric:** STP + 4Ps Alignment.

**Analysis:**
- Target: enterprise security teams
- Positioning: "audit-grade AI"
- Current: freemium pricing (inconsistent); no direct sales (inconsistent); community marketing only

Fix: move to direct-sales motion + enterprise pricing + security-conference presence; add compliance collateral.

**Decision rule:** All 4Ps must reinforce positioning.

**Action (Monday morning):** Audit 4Ps against positioning; realign within 30 days.

---

### Scenario 2: Consulting Firm Helping B2B SaaS Win Enterprise Deals
**Situation:** B2B SaaS wins SMB deals easily but loses enterprise.

**Applicable framework/metric:** DMU Mapping.

**Analysis:**
- SMB: 1-2 stakeholders (user + buyer) → easy
- Enterprise: 6-role DMU — losing on Influencer (security) and Decider (CFO)
- Missing content: security whitepaper, ROI calculator, executive briefs

**Decision rule:** Enterprise = full DMU content + in-person engagement.

**Action:** Build missing collateral; allocate enterprise sales specialist; track DMU contact coverage per deal.

---

### Scenario 3 (Anti-example): Marketing Myopia in Action
**Situation:** DVD retailer continues optimising DVD selection while customers migrate to streaming.

**Analysis (what goes wrong):**
- Product-focused (DVDs) rather than customer-focused (entertainment delivery)
- Failed to scan context (broadband + streaming rise)
- Out of business

**Cost of this mistake:** Entire industry extinction for non-adapters.

**Decision rule:** Re-define business by customer need, not product category.

**Action:** Re-define "What business are we in?"; pivot to streaming early.
