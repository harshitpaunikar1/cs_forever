# AI in Operations

## Overview

AI in operations means applying machine learning and automation to the core processes that produce and deliver goods or services — manufacturing, supply chain, logistics, maintenance, and quality control. AI models forecast demand, optimize routing, predict equipment failures before they happen, and automate inspections. The result is lower cost, higher throughput, fewer defects, and faster response to disruptions.

---

## Why It Matters

Operations are where most of a company's costs live. A 5% improvement in production efficiency or a 10% reduction in unplanned downtime translates directly to the bottom line. AI gives operations teams the ability to move from reactive firefighting to proactive optimization, making decisions in real time that humans cannot because of the speed, complexity, or volume of data involved.

## Key Principles

- Predictive maintenance uses sensor data and ML to forecast equipment failure, scheduling repairs before breakdowns occur
- Demand forecasting with ML reduces both overstock (tied-up capital) and stockouts (lost sales)
- Route optimization algorithms process thousands of variables — distance, traffic, delivery windows, vehicle capacity — to find the cheapest or fastest routes
- Computer vision automates quality inspection on production lines, detecting defects faster and more consistently than human inspectors

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Predictive Maintenance** | Using ML on sensor and operational data to predict when equipment will fail so repairs happen proactively |
| **Demand Forecasting** | Estimating future product demand using historical sales, seasonality, and external signals |
| **Route Optimization** | Finding the most efficient path for deliveries or logistics given constraints like time windows and vehicle capacity |
| **Digital Twin** | A virtual replica of a physical asset or process, updated with real-time data, used for simulation and optimization |

## Use Case

A beverage company operates 15 bottling lines across three plants. Sensors on each line capture vibration, temperature, and pressure readings every second. An ML model trained on two years of failure logs predicts which components are likely to fail within the next 72 hours. Maintenance crews replace parts during planned downtime, reducing unplanned stoppages by 40%.

## Scenario

> A third-party logistics provider managed 800 delivery routes daily across a metropolitan area. After deploying an AI-powered route optimization engine that ingested real-time traffic, weather, and parcel-size data, average route time dropped 14%. Fuel costs fell $1.2 million per year, and on-time delivery rates improved from 89% to 96%.

## Examples

- A steel mill uses a digital twin of its blast furnace to simulate temperature adjustments before applying them, reducing energy consumption by 7%
- An electronics manufacturer deploys computer vision cameras at the end of its assembly line to catch solder defects, replacing manual spot checks and achieving 99.5% detection accuracy

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Sense-Plan-Act Loop
**Purpose:** Turn live operational data into action.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Sensor data / operational data / real-time data
   -> Predict
   -> Optimize
   -> Act
   -> Measure throughput, downtime, defects, and cost
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Predictive maintenance and demand forecasting, which estimate what will happen next.  
Component 2: Optimization, which turns predictions into a feasible operational plan.  
Component 3: Action, such as a maintenance window, dispatch change, or staffing adjustment.  
Component 4: Measurement, which checks throughput, defects, on-time delivery rate, and unplanned stoppages.  
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud operations team predicts server failure from sensor data, schedules planned downtime, and uses optimization to keep service levels high while maintenance happens.  
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team needs a closed-loop operating model instead of a one-off dashboard.

### Forecast-to-Route Matrix
**Purpose:** Link demand or incident forecasts to route choices.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
+----------------------+----------------------+
| Forecast             | Route choice         |
+----------------------+----------------------+
| High demand          | Add capacity         |
| Low demand           | Consolidate routes   |
| Failure risk spike   | Dispatch maintenance |
+----------------------+----------------------+
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Demand forecasting, which tells you how much work is coming.  
Component 2: Route optimization, which decides where capacity or technicians go.  
Component 3: Vehicle capacity and service windows, which keep the plan feasible.  
Component 4: Fuel costs and route time, which show whether the chosen route is efficient.  
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A field-service platform forecasts call volume across districts, then sends repair teams using route optimization so the best engineers are closest to high-risk assets.  
When to pull this out in a meeting: When dispatch, staffing, or service routing is the decision bottleneck.

### Digital Twin Decision Matrix
**Purpose:** Test operational changes before rollout.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Real process -> Digital twin -> Simulation -> Recommended change
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: The real process, such as a support center, production line, or deployment pipeline.  
Component 2: The digital twin, which mirrors the process with updated data.  
Component 3: Simulation, which tests what happens under different parameters.  
Component 4: Optimization, which selects the best operating rule from the simulated options.  
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product operations team uses a digital twin of its release pipeline to test whether moving QA earlier will reduce defects without lowering throughput.  
When to pull this out in a meeting: When changing the process is expensive and failure is visible.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: `Expected downtime cost = failure probability × cost of failure` [verified from model knowledge, not source]  
Variables:  
Failure probability = chance a component fails in the forecast window.  
Cost of failure = business cost of the unplanned stoppage.  
Why this formula exists: It answers whether predictive maintenance is worth doing now.  
How to interpret the output:  
Value < 10,000 -> keep monitoring.  
Value 10,000-50,000 -> schedule maintenance in the next window.  
Value > 50,000 -> prioritize immediate intervention.  
Worked example with numbers: A cloud data-center team estimates a 20% chance of a disk failure that would cost $300,000 in downtime. Expected downtime cost is $60,000, so the team should pull the disk into the next planned downtime window.

Formula: `Utilization = throughput / capacity` [verified from model knowledge, not source]  
Variables:  
Throughput = units, tickets, or jobs completed per time period.  
Capacity = maximum feasible output in the same period.  
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the process is underused or overloaded.  
How to interpret the output:  
Value < 0.70 -> spare capacity -> consolidate or stimulate demand.  
Value 0.70-0.90 -> healthy operating range -> maintain the plan.  
Value > 0.90 -> overload risk -> add resources or reduce load.  
Worked example with numbers: A support team completes 850 tickets out of 1,000 possible tickets in a week, so utilization is 0.85. That is acceptable, but a product leader should not raise demand without adding capacity.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Wait for a failure before starting maintenance. | Use predictive maintenance on sensor and operational data. |
| Plan demand with only last month's sales. | Combine historical sales, seasonality, and external signals. |
| Dispatch teams without checking vehicle capacity or service windows. | Use route optimization under explicit constraints. |
| Trust manual spot checks for all quality decisions. | Use computer vision for quality inspection and keep human inspectors for exceptions. |
| Roll out a process change without simulating it first. | Build a digital twin and test the change in simulation. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Cloud Reliability Maintenance
Situation: An IT platform team sees rising temperature and error-rate readings across several servers. They need to prevent unplanned stoppages without taking down too many services at once.  
Applicable framework/metric: Sense-Plan-Act Loop; expected downtime cost.  
Analysis: The team calculates failure probability from sensor data, multiplies it by the cost of failure, and prioritizes the highest-risk components first. If expected downtime cost is above $50,000, maintenance moves into the next planned downtime window; if it is between $10,000 and $50,000, it gets queued; below $10,000, it stays under monitoring.  
Decision rule: If metric > 50,000, do A. If between 10,000 and 50,000, do B. If below 10,000, do C.  
Action: Publish a maintenance board, schedule repair windows, and track downtime avoided after each intervention.

### Scenario 2: Product Demand and Capacity Planning
Situation: A product team expects a launch-driven traffic spike and needs to keep throughput high without creating backlog. They have historical sales, seasonality, and external signals from the campaign calendar.  
Applicable framework/metric: Forecast-to-Route Matrix; utilization.  
Analysis: Demand forecasting estimates the spike, and optimization decides whether to add support shifts, throttle release traffic, or redirect users. If utilization rises above 0.90, add capacity; if it sits between 0.70 and 0.90, keep the plan; if it drops below 0.70, consolidate work.  
Decision rule: If metric > 0.90, do A. If between 0.70 and 0.90, do B. If below 0.70, do C.  
Action: Adjust staffing, pre-approve overtime, and watch throughput in real time during the launch week.

### Scenario 3: Consulting Process Improvement
Situation: A consulting team is redesigning a client support process that currently relies on human inspectors and manual routing. The team wants to reduce defects, fuel costs, and route time across the service network.  
Applicable framework/metric: Digital Twin Decision Matrix; on-time delivery rate.  
Analysis: A digital twin simulates different routing and staffing setups, and optimization picks the one that keeps on-time delivery rate above 95% while lowering cost. If the simulated on-time delivery rate drops below 95%, the process change is rejected; if it is between 95% and 97%, the team pilots it; above 97%, it scales quickly.  
Decision rule: If metric > 0.97, do A. If between 0.95 and 0.97, do B. If below 0.95, do C.  
Action: Pilot the new routing plan, compare actual vs simulated results, and keep a rollback option ready.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Map the operational process and identify where AI can forecast, inspect, route, or optimize.
2. Collect sensor data, operational data, historical sales, and any external signals that explain the process.
3. Build a baseline predictive model for maintenance, demand, or failure risk.
4. Encode capacity, budget, service, and timing constraints for the prescriptive layer.
5. Use simulation to test the proposed plan before changing the real process.
6. Measure throughput, downtime, defects, fuel costs, and on-time delivery rate before and after launch.
7. Add human inspectors or operators as an exception layer for edge cases and model failures.
8. Review the digital twin or planning model monthly so the operating assumptions stay current.
