# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and concepts covering Q1 to Q70.

## Q1. What is business valuation?

**Level:** Basic

Business valuation is the process of estimating what a company is economically worth. It combines financial analysis, market comparisons, and assumptions about future performance. The output is a value or value range used for deals, investments, taxes, or disputes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting price in a merger or acquisition
- Pricing shares for an IPO
- Negotiating a fundraising round
- Settling shareholder or divorce disputes
- Allocating purchase price in accounting

**Key concepts:** `value`, `pricing`, `analysis`, `dcf`, `comparables`

---

## Q2. What is a balance sheet?

**Level:** Basic

A balance sheet is a financial statement that shows what a company owns, owes, and the residual equity at a specific date. Assets must always equal liabilities plus equity. It is the snapshot of financial position at one moment.

**Real-life applications:**
- Checking liquidity before a loan application
- Computing book value for valuation
- Assessing solvency in bankruptcy cases
- Measuring working capital needs
- Supporting credit rating reviews

**Key concepts:** `assets`, `liabilities`, `equity`, `book-value`, `snapshot`

---

## Q3. Define an asset.

**Level:** Basic

An asset is any resource owned by a company that has economic value. Assets are expected to produce future benefits, such as cash or usage. They include cash, receivables, inventory, equipment, and intangibles.

**Real-life applications:**
- Classifying items on a balance sheet
- Valuing inventory for tax purposes
- Assessing collateral for a loan
- Capitalizing software development costs
- Auditing fixed asset registers

**Key concepts:** `resource`, `ownership`, `value`, `current`, `non-current`

---

## Q4. What is a liability?

**Level:** Basic

A liability is a financial obligation the company owes to others. Liabilities are settled by paying cash, delivering goods, or providing services. They include loans, trade payables, taxes, and pensions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing debt-to-equity ratios
- Planning working capital
- Disclosing off-balance-sheet items
- Meeting loan covenants
- Preparing for creditor negotiations

**Key concepts:** `obligation`, `debt`, `payable`, `covenants`, `solvency`

---

## Q5. What is equity in a company?

**Level:** Basic

Equity is the owners' stake in the business — what is left after liabilities are deducted from assets. It includes paid-in capital and retained earnings. Equity holders carry the highest risk and reward.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing book value per share
- Tracking retained earnings over time
- Issuing new shares for capital
- Paying dividends to owners
- Calculating return on equity

**Key concepts:** `ownership`, `capital`, `retained-earnings`, `shares`, `net-worth`

---

## Q6. What is working capital?

**Level:** Basic

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. It measures the short-term liquidity of a business. Positive working capital means the company can cover its near-term bills.

**Real-life applications:**
- Forecasting cash needs
- Judging supplier payment terms
- Sizing a working capital loan
- Managing inventory levels
- Monitoring receivables collection

**Key concepts:** `liquidity`, `current-assets`, `current-liabilities`, `cash`, `operations`

---

## Q7. What does EBITDA stand for?

**Level:** Basic

EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortization. It is a proxy for cash operating profit before capital structure and tax effects. Analysts use it to compare companies across tax regimes and leverage levels.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing EV/EBITDA multiples
- Setting lender covenants
- Comparing cross-border companies
- Benchmarking operational profitability
- Sizing private equity deals

**Key concepts:** `profit`, `cash-proxy`, `multiples`, `covenants`, `operations`

---

## Q8. What is depreciation?

**Level:** Basic

Depreciation is the accounting method of spreading the cost of a tangible asset over its useful life. It matches the expense of using an asset with the revenue it helps generate. It is a non-cash charge on the income statement.

**Real-life applications:**
- Spreading factory equipment costs
- Reducing taxable income
- Normalizing profit across periods
- Adding back in cash flow analysis
- Planning asset replacement

**Key concepts:** `non-cash`, `useful-life`, `matching`, `tangible`, `expense`

---

## Q9. What is amortization?

**Level:** Basic

Amortization is the gradual writing off of the cost of an intangible asset over its useful life. It also refers to scheduled repayment of a loan's principal. Like depreciation, the accounting version is a non-cash expense.

**Real-life applications:**
- Writing off acquired patents
- Spreading goodwill impairment
- Tracking loan principal paydown
- Matching software cost to benefit
- Adding back in EBITDA

**Key concepts:** `intangible`, `non-cash`, `useful-life`, `loan`, `ebitda`

---

## Q10. What is net income?

**Level:** Basic

Net income is the profit left after all expenses, interest, and taxes are deducted from revenue. It is the bottom line of the income statement. It belongs to the shareholders.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing earnings per share
- Tracking profitability trends
- Declaring dividends
- Setting executive bonuses
- Calculating return on equity

**Key concepts:** `profit`, `bottom-line`, `eps`, `dividends`, `roe`

---

## Q11. What is free cash flow?

**Level:** Basic

Free cash flow is the cash left after operating costs and capital expenditure. It represents the cash available to pay debt, dividends, or reinvest in growth. It is the most trusted measure of cash-generating ability.

**Real-life applications:**
- Discounting in a DCF model
- Judging dividend capacity
- Planning share buybacks
- Assessing debt repayment ability
- Stress-testing through downturns

**Key concepts:** `cash`, `capex`, `dcf`, `dividends`, `buyback`

---

## Q12. What is the time value of money?

**Level:** Basic

The time value of money says a rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow. Money in hand can be invested and earn a return. Future cash must be discounted to compare fairly with today's cash.

**Real-life applications:**
- Discounting in valuation models
- Comparing payment schedules
- Pricing bonds and loans
- Evaluating pension obligations
- Choosing between capex projects

**Key concepts:** `present-value`, `future-value`, `discounting`, `compounding`, `opportunity-cost`

---

## Q13. What is present value?

**Level:** Basic

Present value is today's worth of a future sum, discounted at a chosen rate. It answers: how much should I pay now for money I will get later? A higher discount rate or longer wait lowers the present value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing future cash flows in DCF
- Pricing lottery-style settlements
- Comparing annuity options
- Budgeting for retirement
- Evaluating infrastructure projects

**Key concepts:** `discount`, `future-cash`, `rate`, `npv`, `dcf`

---

## Q14. What is future value?

**Level:** Basic

Future value is what a sum today will be worth at a later date after earning a return. It is the result of compounding. Small differences in rate or time produce large differences in future value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Projecting retirement savings
- Forecasting sinking fund balances
- Planning bond maturities
- Setting compound-interest products
- Modelling endowment growth

**Key concepts:** `compounding`, `rate`, `time`, `savings`, `growth`

---

## Q15. What is a discount rate?

**Level:** Basic

A discount rate is the rate used to convert future cash flows into present value. It reflects the time value of money and the risk of the cash flows. Higher risk demands a higher discount rate.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting WACC for a DCF
- Pricing project bonds
- Evaluating capex proposals
- Choosing between lease terms
- Valuing long-term contracts

**Key concepts:** `wacc`, `risk`, `present-value`, `hurdle-rate`, `opportunity-cost`

---

## Q16. Define DCF in one sentence.

**Level:** Basic

DCF stands for discounted cash flow: a method that values a business as the sum of its future free cash flows discounted to today. It is the most common intrinsic valuation method. It is sensitive to forecast and discount rate assumptions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing companies for M&A
- Deciding on capital projects
- Valuing concessions or licenses
- Setting IPO price ranges
- Testing market prices for value

**Key concepts:** `dcf`, `free-cash-flow`, `wacc`, `terminal-value`, `valuation`

---

## Q17. What is WACC?

**Level:** Basic

WACC is the weighted average cost of capital — a blended rate combining the cost of debt and equity. It reflects what investors demand for bearing the risk of the business. It is used to discount free cash flow to the firm.

**Real-life applications:**
- Discounting FCFF in DCF models
- Setting internal hurdle rates
- Evaluating capex projects
- Pricing long-term contracts
- Judging acquisition targets

**Key concepts:** `cost-of-capital`, `cost-of-debt`, `cost-of-equity`, `weights`, `discount`

---

## Q18. What is cost of equity?

**Level:** Basic

Cost of equity is the return shareholders require for investing in a company's stock. It reflects the risk of owning that equity. It is typically estimated with the capital asset pricing model (CAPM).

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing WACC
- Evaluating stock investments
- Setting required return on projects
- Pricing rights issues
- Valuing private firms with peer betas

**Key concepts:** `capm`, `beta`, `risk-free-rate`, `equity-premium`, `shareholders`

---

## Q19. What is cost of debt?

**Level:** Basic

Cost of debt is the effective interest rate a company pays on its borrowings. Since interest is tax-deductible, the after-tax cost is used in WACC. Lower-rated companies pay higher costs of debt.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing after-tax WACC
- Pricing new bond issues
- Judging refinancing opportunities
- Comparing lender offers
- Monitoring credit rating impact

**Key concepts:** `interest`, `tax-shield`, `credit-rating`, `debt`, `wacc`

---

## Q20. What is beta in finance?

**Level:** Basic

Beta measures a stock's sensitivity to overall market movements. A beta of 1 moves with the market; above 1 is more volatile, below 1 is less. It is used in CAPM to compute cost of equity.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing cost of equity
- Building portfolio risk profiles
- Adjusting for leverage in peer comparison
- Testing stock hedges
- Measuring cyclicality

**Key concepts:** `capm`, `volatility`, `market`, `systematic-risk`, `cost-of-equity`

---

## Q21. What is the risk-free rate?

**Level:** Basic

The risk-free rate is the return on an investment considered to have zero default risk. Government bond yields, especially long-dated sovereigns, are typical proxies. It anchors the CAPM calculation.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing cost of equity
- Valuing risk-free annuities
- Pricing guarantees
- Benchmarking hurdle rates
- Measuring equity risk premium

**Key concepts:** `treasury`, `sovereign`, `capm`, `benchmark`, `default-free`

---

## Q22. What is equity risk premium?

**Level:** Basic

Equity risk premium is the extra return investors demand for holding stocks over the risk-free rate. It compensates for market risk. Estimates typically range from 4% to 7% depending on the market.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting cost of equity
- Comparing country risks
- Pricing equity-linked notes
- Calibrating investment policy
- Justifying stock allocations

**Key concepts:** `premium`, `risk-free-rate`, `capm`, `stocks`, `return`

---

## Q23. What is terminal value?

**Level:** Basic

Terminal value is the value of all cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period in a DCF. It is usually the largest component of the answer. It is computed using a perpetual growth rate or an exit multiple.

**Real-life applications:**
- Closing out a DCF model
- Running sensitivity checks
- Justifying long-term growth assumptions
- Cross-checking with exit multiples
- Valuing mature businesses

**Key concepts:** `gordon-growth`, `exit-multiple`, `perpetuity`, `dcf`, `long-term`

---

## Q24. What is enterprise value?

**Level:** Basic

Enterprise value (EV) is the total value of a company's operations to both debt and equity holders. It equals equity value plus net debt and other claims. EV is used with pre-interest metrics like EBITDA.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing EV/EBITDA multiples
- Pricing cash-free, debt-free deals
- Comparing capital-structure-neutral peers
- Building DCF outputs
- Sizing whole-company acquisitions

**Key concepts:** `ev`, `net-debt`, `equity-value`, `multiples`, `capital-structure`

---

## Q25. What is equity value?

**Level:** Basic

Equity value is the portion of enterprise value that belongs to shareholders. For listed companies, it is share price times shares outstanding. It matches post-interest metrics like net income and FCFE.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing P/E ratios
- Pricing share transactions
- Reporting market cap
- Allocating in SPAs
- Paying seller shareholders

**Key concepts:** `market-cap`, `shareholders`, `p/e`, `net-income`, `fcfe`

---

## Q26. What is net debt?

**Level:** Basic

Net debt is total debt minus cash and cash equivalents. It is the bridge between enterprise value and equity value. It captures the real leverage of the business.

**Real-life applications:**
- Bridging EV to equity value
- Setting lender leverage ratios
- Adjusting transaction price at closing
- Comparing peer balance sheets
- Estimating debt capacity

**Key concepts:** `debt`, `cash`, `leverage`, `ev-bridge`, `covenants`

---

## Q27. What is goodwill?

**Level:** Basic

Goodwill is the premium paid over the fair value of net assets in an acquisition. It represents brand, relationships, and other intangibles that cannot be separately identified. It sits on the buyer's balance sheet and is tested annually for impairment.

**Real-life applications:**
- Booking M&A purchase price
- Testing annual impairment
- Disclosing synergy claims
- Auditing goodwill additions
- Writing down after failed deals

**Key concepts:** `acquisition`, `premium`, `intangibles`, `impairment`, `m&a`

---

## Q28. What is book value?

**Level:** Basic

Book value is total assets minus total liabilities, as reported in the accounts. It is the accounting version of equity. It often differs from market value, especially when intangibles dominate.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing price-to-book ratios
- Valuing asset-heavy firms
- Assessing liquidation floors
- Comparing banks and financials
- Writing fair value adjustments

**Key concepts:** `accounting`, `equity`, `p/b`, `liquidation`, `fair-value`

---

## Q29. What is market value?

**Level:** Basic

Market value is the price at which a company or asset would change hands between willing parties. For listed firms, it equals share price times shares outstanding. It reflects the market's aggregate view of value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pricing listed investments
- Benchmarking private valuations
- Computing market capitalization
- Setting offer prices
- Reporting for wealth management

**Key concepts:** `market-cap`, `listed`, `willing-buyer`, `price`, `fair-value`

---

## Q30. What is a multiple in valuation?

**Level:** Basic

A multiple is a ratio that compares price to a financial metric — like EV/EBITDA or P/E. It standardizes value across companies of different sizes. Multiples are quick to compute and easy to compare.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running comparable company analysis
- Setting IPO price bands
- Pricing minority stakes quickly
- Cross-checking DCF outputs
- Negotiating quick deal math

**Key concepts:** `ratio`, `comparables`, `ev/ebitda`, `p/e`, `benchmark`

---

## Q31. What is EV/EBITDA?

**Level:** Basic

EV/EBITDA is enterprise value divided by EBITDA. It is the most widely used valuation multiple for comparing companies. It is capital-structure neutral because EV and EBITDA both ignore interest.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing peers across leverage levels
- Pricing M&A targets
- Setting LBO bid caps
- Benchmarking industry multiples
- Cross-border valuations

**Key concepts:** `ev`, `ebitda`, `multiples`, `capital-structure-neutral`, `peers`

---

## Q32. What is the P/E ratio?

**Level:** Basic

P/E ratio is share price divided by earnings per share. It shows how much investors pay per rupee of earnings. Higher P/Es usually imply higher expected growth or lower perceived risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- Quick equity screening
- Comparing growth vs. value stocks
- Judging market sentiment
- Justifying IPO pricing
- Tracking historical valuations

**Key concepts:** `price`, `earnings`, `multiple`, `growth`, `sentiment`

---

## Q33. What is comparable company analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Comparable company analysis values a business by looking at multiples of similar listed peers. It is a market-based method that reflects current investor sentiment. It is usually used alongside DCF and precedent transactions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pitching IPO price ranges
- Cross-checking DCF values
- Setting quick M&A price guidance
- Valuing minority stakes
- Reporting fair value to auditors

**Key concepts:** `peers`, `multiples`, `market-based`, `benchmark`, `dcf-cross-check`

---

## Q34. What is precedent transaction analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Precedent transaction analysis values a company using prices paid in past M&A deals for similar targets. Unlike trading comps, these prices include control premiums. It shows what acquirers will actually pay.

**Real-life applications:**
- Justifying a sell-side price
- Negotiating M&A offers
- Benchmarking control premiums
- Preparing fairness opinions
- Advising on exit strategy

**Key concepts:** `m&a`, `control-premium`, `transaction-multiples`, `acquirer`, `benchmark`

---

## Q35. What is a control premium?

**Level:** Basic

A control premium is the extra price a buyer pays above the market share price to gain control of a company. It compensates shareholders for giving up ownership. Premiums typically range from 20% to 40%.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting takeover offer prices
- Advising boards on fairness
- Explaining share price jumps
- Benchmarking deal terms
- Valuing majority stakes

**Key concepts:** `takeover`, `premium`, `majority`, `m&a`, `control`

---

## Q36. What is a liquidity discount?

**Level:** Basic

A liquidity discount is a valuation haircut for shares that cannot be easily sold. It applies to private companies and restricted shares. Typical discounts range from 15% to 35%.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing private company shares
- Pricing employee stock at pre-IPO
- Settling estate tax valuations
- Pricing restricted placements
- Applying to closely held stakes

**Key concepts:** `private`, `marketability`, `discount`, `restricted`, `pre-ipo`

---

## Q37. What is FCFF?

**Level:** Basic

FCFF stands for free cash flow to firm — cash available to both debt and equity holders before interest payments. It is discounted at WACC in a DCF. It is the most common cash flow measure in enterprise valuation.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building enterprise DCF models
- Evaluating capital projects
- Comparing firms across leverage levels
- Setting transfer prices
- Valuing whole-business sales

**Key concepts:** `firm`, `wacc`, `dcf`, `enterprise-value`, `capital-providers`

---

## Q38. What is FCFE?

**Level:** Basic

FCFE stands for free cash flow to equity — cash available to shareholders after debt service. It is discounted at the cost of equity. It gives equity value directly, not enterprise value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing banks and insurance firms
- Testing dividend capacity
- Projecting equity returns
- Valuing leveraged entities
- Pricing equity stakes directly

**Key concepts:** `equity`, `cost-of-equity`, `post-interest`, `dividends`, `shareholders`

---

## Q39. What is an income statement?

**Level:** Basic

An income statement reports revenues, expenses, and profit over a period — usually a quarter or a year. It shows how a company performed operationally and financially. It ends at net income.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking quarterly performance
- Computing earnings per share
- Setting manager incentives
- Supporting credit reviews
- Building valuation forecasts

**Key concepts:** `revenue`, `expenses`, `profit`, `period`, `performance`

---

## Q40. What is a cash flow statement?

**Level:** Basic

A cash flow statement reports actual cash movements during a period, split into operating, investing, and financing activities. It complements the income statement by showing cash reality. It is trusted more than profit by many analysts.

**Real-life applications:**
- Checking cash-generation ability
- Detecting accounting red flags
- Sizing dividend payouts
- Monitoring capex discipline
- Validating DCF projections

**Key concepts:** `operating`, `investing`, `financing`, `cash`, `reality-check`

---

## Q41. What is revenue?

**Level:** Basic

Revenue is the total income from selling goods or services before any costs are deducted. It is the top line of the income statement. Recurring, growing revenue is more valuable than one-off revenue.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking top-line growth
- Setting sales targets
- Valuing SaaS at ARR multiples
- Comparing market share
- Filing statutory disclosures

**Key concepts:** `top-line`, `sales`, `recurring`, `growth`, `arr`

---

## Q42. What is gross profit?

**Level:** Basic

Gross profit is revenue minus cost of goods sold. It shows the profit from producing or delivering the core product. Gross margin reveals product-level economics.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pricing new products
- Benchmarking unit economics
- Comparing manufacturing costs
- Monitoring procurement efficiency
- Judging brand power

**Key concepts:** `margin`, `cogs`, `unit-economics`, `pricing`, `revenue`

---

## Q43. What is operating profit?

**Level:** Basic

Operating profit is gross profit minus operating expenses like salaries, rent, and marketing. It shows profit from core business operations before interest and tax. It is also called EBIT.

**Real-life applications:**
- Evaluating management efficiency
- Setting manager bonuses
- Reporting segment performance
- Benchmarking against peers
- Computing EV/EBIT multiples

**Key concepts:** `ebit`, `core-operations`, `margin`, `overheads`, `management`

---

## Q44. What is net margin?

**Level:** Basic

Net margin is net income divided by revenue, expressed as a percentage. It shows how much profit the company keeps from every rupee of sales. Higher margins usually reflect pricing power or cost discipline.

**Real-life applications:**
- Benchmarking profitability
- Comparing peer quality
- Tracking margin trends
- Setting long-term growth targets
- Supporting valuation multiples

**Key concepts:** `bottom-line`, `margin`, `revenue`, `profit`, `benchmark`

---

## Q45. What is return on equity (ROE)?

**Level:** Basic

Return on equity is net income divided by shareholders' equity. It measures how efficiently a company uses shareholder money to generate profit. Consistently high ROE is a hallmark of quality businesses.

**Real-life applications:**
- Screening quality stocks
- Benchmarking bank performance
- Setting shareholder return targets
- Comparing across business units
- Deciding dividend policy

**Key concepts:** `equity`, `net-income`, `efficiency`, `quality`, `shareholders`

---

## Q46. What is return on assets (ROA)?

**Level:** Basic

Return on assets is net income divided by total assets. It shows how well a company generates profit from its asset base. It is useful for capital-heavy industries.

**Real-life applications:**
- Benchmarking banks and utilities
- Judging asset efficiency
- Tracking divisional performance
- Comparing competitors
- Setting capex discipline

**Key concepts:** `assets`, `net-income`, `efficiency`, `capital-heavy`, `roa`

---

## Q47. What is return on invested capital (ROIC)?

**Level:** Basic

ROIC is operating profit after tax divided by invested capital. It measures returns across all capital — debt and equity combined. A ROIC above WACC creates value; below, it destroys value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Deciding capex approvals
- Benchmarking management teams
- Setting transfer pricing standards
- Judging M&A success
- Communicating value creation

**Key concepts:** `invested-capital`, `value-creation`, `wacc`, `operations`, `post-tax`

---

## Q48. What is a capital structure?

**Level:** Basic

Capital structure is the mix of debt and equity a company uses to finance its operations. It affects risk, return, and valuation. Optimal capital structure minimizes WACC.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting leverage targets
- Deciding refinancing needs
- Issuing new equity or debt
- Pricing M&A transactions
- Managing credit ratings

**Key concepts:** `debt`, `equity`, `leverage`, `wacc`, `optimization`

---

## Q49. What is leverage?

**Level:** Basic

Leverage is the use of debt to finance assets or acquisitions. It amplifies returns — positive or negative — for equity holders. Too much leverage raises the risk of default.

**Real-life applications:**
- Structuring LBOs
- Setting lender covenants
- Managing credit ratings
- Amplifying ROE targets
- Monitoring risk exposure

**Key concepts:** `debt`, `amplification`, `risk`, `roe`, `default`

---

## Q50. What is an LBO?

**Level:** Basic

An LBO, or leveraged buyout, is an acquisition funded mostly by borrowed money. The target's own cash flows repay the debt over time. LBOs are typical private equity deals.

**Real-life applications:**
- Private equity take-private deals
- Management buyouts
- Carve-outs from conglomerates
- Public-to-private transactions
- Value investing strategies

**Key concepts:** `private-equity`, `leverage`, `debt`, `sponsor`, `acquisition`

---

## Q51. What is a sponsor in an LBO?

**Level:** Basic

A sponsor is the private equity firm leading a leveraged buyout. The sponsor raises equity, arranges debt, and manages the investment. It typically holds for 3–7 years before exit.

**Real-life applications:**
- Leading buyout negotiations
- Arranging acquisition financing
- Governing portfolio companies
- Driving operational improvement
- Planning exit timing

**Key concepts:** `private-equity`, `buyer`, `investor`, `lbo`, `hold-period`

---

## Q52. What is an IRR?

**Level:** Basic

IRR is the internal rate of return — the annualized return an investment generates over its life. It equates the present value of inflows to outflows. PE funds target IRRs of 20–25%.

**Real-life applications:**
- Evaluating investment proposals
- Comparing PE fund performance
- Pricing capital projects
- Setting fund benchmarks
- Judging LBO attractiveness

**Key concepts:** `return`, `annualized`, `npv`, `benchmark`, `pe`

---

## Q53. What is NPV?

**Level:** Basic

NPV stands for net present value — the sum of discounted cash inflows minus outflows. Positive NPV means the project creates value. Negative NPV means it destroys value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Approving capex projects
- Comparing investment options
- Valuing long-term contracts
- Pricing acquisitions
- Evaluating concessions

**Key concepts:** `present-value`, `project`, `value-creation`, `dcf`, `decision`

---

## Q54. What is a hurdle rate?

**Level:** Basic

A hurdle rate is the minimum acceptable return on an investment. It reflects the cost of capital plus a margin for risk. Projects below the hurdle rate are rejected.

**Real-life applications:**
- Approving capex plans
- Setting PE carry triggers
- Filtering M&A opportunities
- Pricing internal transfers
- Prioritizing investments

**Key concepts:** `minimum-return`, `wacc`, `capex`, `decision`, `filter`

---

## Q55. What is pre-money valuation?

**Level:** Basic

Pre-money valuation is the value agreed for a start-up before a new investment round comes in. It determines how much the new investor owns. The founders use it to measure dilution.

**Real-life applications:**
- Negotiating fundraising rounds
- Setting new investor stakes
- Calculating founder dilution
- Comparing valuation trends
- Issuing new shares

**Key concepts:** `startup`, `funding-round`, `dilution`, `cap-table`, `equity`

---

## Q56. What is post-money valuation?

**Level:** Basic

Post-money valuation is the company's value right after new investment is added. It equals pre-money valuation plus the new cash injected. Founders and investors track both.

**Real-life applications:**
- Reporting completed rounds
- Benchmarking valuation trends
- Setting employee option strike prices
- Updating cap tables
- Comparing round-on-round growth

**Key concepts:** `startup`, `after-round`, `pre-money`, `funding`, `equity`

---

## Q57. What is dilution?

**Level:** Basic

Dilution is the reduction in existing shareholders' ownership percentage when new shares are issued. Every funding round dilutes founders and early investors. Anti-dilution clauses can protect against harsh dilution.

**Real-life applications:**
- Negotiating funding terms
- Running cap-table simulations
- Calculating employee option pools
- Protecting with anti-dilution
- Planning long-term founder equity

**Key concepts:** `ownership`, `new-shares`, `founders`, `anti-dilution`, `cap-table`

---

## Q58. What is a cap table?

**Level:** Basic

A cap table lists all the owners of a company's equity and their stake. It shows founders, investors, and option-holders. It updates with every funding round.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking ownership across rounds
- Modelling dilution scenarios
- Preparing for exits
- Managing option grants
- Complying with disclosure

**Key concepts:** `ownership`, `shares`, `investors`, `founders`, `dilution`

---

## Q59. What is liquidation preference?

**Level:** Basic

A liquidation preference gives an investor the right to a minimum payout before common shareholders in an exit. It protects downside for preferred shareholders. Higher multiples make the term more founder-unfriendly.

**Real-life applications:**
- Structuring VC term sheets

**Key concepts:** `preferred`, `exit`, `waterfall`, `protection`, `downside`

## Q60. What is ARR?

**Level:** Basic

ARR stands for annualized recurring revenue — a SaaS-style metric showing the run-rate of subscription revenue. Investors use it to value recurring-revenue businesses. ARR is more valuable than one-off revenue.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing SaaS start-ups
- Monitoring SaaS growth
- Projecting long-term revenue
- Setting investor reporting
- Benchmarking peer multiples

**Key concepts:** `saas`, `recurring`, `subscription`, `growth`, `valuation`

## Q61. What is a synergy in M&A?

**Level:** Basic

A synergy is extra value created when two companies combine — from cost savings, revenue gains, or tax benefits. It is the justification for paying a control premium. Many deals overestimate synergies.

**Real-life applications:**
- Justifying M&A premiums
- Planning integration programs
- Tracking post-deal performance
- Reporting to boards
- Negotiating earn-outs

**Key concepts:** `m&a`, `cost-savings`, `revenue`, `tax`, `integration`

## Q62. What is integration in M&A?

**Level:** Basic

Integration is the process of combining two businesses after a deal closes. It covers systems, people, processes, and culture. Poor integration destroys synergy value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running post-deal integration offices
- Merging IT systems
- Combining sales forces
- Aligning compensation
- Managing cultural fit

**Key concepts:** `post-merger`, `systems`, `people`, `culture`, `synergy`

## Q63. What is a merger?

**Level:** Basic

A merger is the combination of two companies into a single new entity. Shareholders of both get shares in the combined company. It is sometimes called a merger of equals when parties are similar in size.

**Real-life applications:**
- Combining competitors to gain scale
- Forming cross-border champions
- Rationalizing conglomerate structures
- Joining complementary products
- Defending against takeovers

**Key concepts:** `combination`, `merger-of-equals`, `consolidation`, `scale`, `shareholders`

## Q64. What is an acquisition?

**Level:** Basic

An acquisition is when one company buys another and takes control. The target becomes a subsidiary or is absorbed entirely. Acquirers usually pay a premium over market price.

**Real-life applications:**
- Buying growth
- Acquiring technology or talent
- Entering new markets
- Consolidating industries
- Executing portfolio strategy

**Key concepts:** `takeover`, `target`, `buyer`, `premium`, `subsidiary`

## Q65. What is a spin-off?

**Level:** Basic

A spin-off is when a parent company separates a subsidiary into a new independent listed entity. Existing shareholders get shares in both entities. It can unlock hidden value by letting each unit trade on its own merits.

**Real-life applications:**
- Separating conglomerate units
- Unlocking hidden value
- Focusing management teams
- Enabling strategic flexibility
- Avoiding conglomerate discounts

**Key concepts:** `separation`, `parent`, `subsidiary`, `unlock-value`, `listed`

## Q66. What is a private company?

**Level:** Basic

A private company has shares that are not traded on a public stock exchange. Its ownership is typically held by founders, employees, and private investors. Valuing private companies requires extra adjustments for illiquidity.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing for fundraising
- Pricing for sale to PE
- Settling shareholder disputes
- Estate planning
- Employee stock programs

**Key concepts:** `unlisted`, `illiquidity`, `closely-held`, `private-equity`, `founders`

## Q67. What is a listed company?

**Level:** Basic

A listed company has shares traded on a public stock exchange. Its market price gives a daily indication of value. Listed firms must meet disclosure and governance standards.

**Real-life applications:**

- Enabling investor exits

## Q68. What is an IPO?

**Level:** Basic

An IPO — initial public offering — is the first sale of shares of a private company to the public. It converts private equity into listed stock. The IPO price is typically set using comps and DCF.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q69. What is fair value?

**Level:** Basic

Fair value is the price at which an asset would change hands between willing parties in an orderly transaction. It is the accounting standard used for many balance sheet items. It may differ from book value.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### 3.1 Three-Statement Flow
**Purpose:** See how BS/IS/CF feed each other and feed valuation inputs.
```
  Income Statement                 Cash Flow Statement              Balance Sheet
  +-----------------+              +------------------+             +-----------------+
  | Revenue         |              | CFO (from NI)    | ---+------> | Cash            |
  | - COGS          | --- NI ----> | +D&A, -dWC       |    |        | PP&E (Capex)    |
  | = Gross Profit  |              | CFI (Capex)      | ---+------> | Debt (CFF)      |
  | - Opex          |              | CFF (Debt,Div)   | ---+------> | Retained Earn.  |
  | = EBIT          |              +------------------+             +-----------------+
  | - Int, Tax      |                     |                                 |
  | = Net Income    | ---+                v                                 v
  +-----------------+    |             FCFF/FCFE  ---> DCF ---> Enterprise Value
                         +---> Retained Earnings (equity)
```
**Components:** NI flows to CFO and to Retained Earnings; Capex hits CFI and PP&E; dWC bridges IS accruals to CFO.
**Worked example (AI-SaaS):** An AI-SaaS firm books $100M ARR, $20M NI, $8M D&A, $5M dWC build, $10M Capex - FCFF approx $20M + $8M - $5M - $10M = $13M unlevered.
**Trigger:** Any time a candidate is asked "how do the three statements connect" in round 1.

### 3.2 Valuation Methods Triangle (Football Field)
**Purpose:** Triangulate value across DCF / Trading Comps / Precedents / LBO.
```
                           DCF
                          /    \
                         /      \
                        /  FAIR  \
                       / VALUE   \
                      /  RANGE    \
           Comps ----/------------\---- Precedents
                    \              /
                     \            /
                      \   LBO    /
                       \________/
          $  |---DCF---|
             |----Trading Comps----|
             |-----Precedents------|
             |----LBO Floor----|
             low                high
```
**Components:** Each method produces a range; banker overlays on football field.
**Worked example (Consulting):** Valuing an AI copilot startup - DCF $180-220M, Comps $200-260M, Precedents $240-300M (strategic premium), LBO $150-180M; intrinsic range $200-260M.
**Trigger:** Pitch book page 1, MBA case wrap-up.

### 3.3 EV to Equity Bridge
**Purpose:** Don't mix up EV and Equity Value - the single most common basic mistake.
```
  Enterprise Value
  + Cash (excess)
  - Total Debt
  - Preferred Stock
  - Minority Interest
  - Pension Deficit / Lease Liabilities
  = Equity Value
  / Fully Diluted Shares (TSM)
  = Price per Share
```
**Components:** EV is core-operations value; Equity Value is what common holders receive after other claims.
**Worked example (Product):** Acquirer reads EV $1B in the comps sheet, forgets $200M net debt; wires $1B for equity instead of $800M - $200M overpayment.
**Trigger:** Any acquisition-math interview question.

### 3.4 Startup Stage to Method Matrix

| Stage | ARR/Metric | Primary Method | Secondary | Notes |
|-------|-----------|----------------|-----------|-------|
| Pre-seed | Idea only | Scorecard | Berkus | DCF useless |
| Seed | <$1M ARR | VC Method | Scorecard | Post-money $8-15M typical |
| Series A | $1-5M ARR | Revenue multiple | VC Method | 10-20x ARR for AI |
| Series B | $5-20M ARR | Revenue multiple | DCF sanity | Rule of 40 kicks in |
| Series C+ | $20M+ ARR | DCF + Comps | Precedents | Public-ready math |
| Growth/Pre-IPO | $100M+ ARR | DCF + Public Comps | Precedents | Full valuation toolkit |

**Worked example (AI Lead):** A $4M ARR vertical-AI startup with 150% NDR and 80% gross margin gets 15x ARR = $60M pre-money; VC-method cross-check from a $500M exit at 5x MoIC requires $100M post - triangulate to $70-80M post.
**Trigger:** Startup due diligence or fundraising advisory.

## 4. Formulas

### 4.1 Present / Future Value
**PV = FV / (1+r)^n ; FV = PV * (1+r)^n**
Threshold: r typically 8-15% for corporate projects; n in years.
Example (IT capex): A $500K automation project pays $800K in 5 years at r=10%; PV = 800/(1.10)^5 = $497K; NPV approx -$3K, marginal reject.

### 4.2 WACC and CAPM
**WACC = (E/V) * Re + (D/V) * Rd * (1 - t) ; Re = Rf + Beta * ERP**
Threshold: WACC typically 7-12% for large-cap, 12-20% for growth; tax shield ~21% (US).
Example (AI-SaaS): Rf=4%, Beta=1.4, ERP=5.5%; Re = 4% + 1.4*5.5% = 11.7%; mostly-equity firm WACC approx 11%.

### 4.3 Gordon Growth Terminal Value
**TV = FCFF_{n+1} / (WACC - g)**
Threshold: g must be < long-run GDP (~2-3%); WACC-g spread at least 4-5%.
Example (Consulting SaaS model): Year-10 FCFF $50M, g=2.5%, WACC=10%; TV = 50*1.025/(0.10-0.025) = $683M; PV of TV at year 10 = $683/(1.10)^10 = $263M.

### 4.4 EV to Equity Bridge
**EV = Equity Value + Net Debt + Minority Interest + Preferred Stock + Pension Deficit - Excess Cash**
Threshold: Flag if Net Debt > 3x EBITDA (leverage concern) or Cash > 30% of market cap (capital-return candidate).
Example (Product BizOps): Market cap $1.2B, Debt $300M, Cash $100M, no Pref/NCI; EV = 1200 + 300 - 100 = $1.4B; EV/EBITDA at $140M EBITDA = 10x.

### 4.5 IRR and MoIC
**IRR: 0 = Sum CF_t / (1+IRR)^t ; MoIC = Exit Equity / Entry Equity**
Threshold: PE target IRR ~20-25%, MoIC 2.5-3x over 5 years; VC target 3-5x MoIC, IRR 25-40%.
Example (LBO on IT services target): Entry equity $200M, exit equity $500M at year 5; MoIC = 2.5x; IRR approx 20.1%.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| Do | Don't |
|----|-------|
| Match numerator to denominator: EV with EBITDA/Sales, Equity with Net Income/EPS | Don't mix EV with equity multiples (EV/Net Income is nonsense) |
| Use market-value weights in WACC | Don't use book D/E ratios for WACC weights |
| Keep terminal g below long-run GDP (2-3%) | Don't set terminal g >= long-term GDP (implies firm eats economy) |
| Match FCFF with WACC (unlevered), FCFE with Re (levered) | Don't confuse FCFF vs FCFE or their discount rates |
| Use comps/VC method for pre-revenue startups | Don't value a pre-revenue startup with DCF (garbage-in-garbage-out) |
| Tax-effect cost of debt: Rd*(1-t) | Don't forget the tax shield on Rd in WACC |
| Use diluted shares via Treasury Stock Method when options are material | Don't use basic shares when options/RSUs are >2% dilutive |
| Subtract net debt (debt minus cash) | Don't forget cash in EV bridge |
| Cross-check DCF with comps and precedents | Don't rely on one method alone |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios

### 6.1 Consulting-Interview Prep: 10-Minute DCF on an AI-SaaS Target
A McKinsey BizOps candidate gets "value this vertical-AI SaaS company with $50M ARR, 40% growth, 25% FCF margin, WACC 11%." She forecasts 5 years (40%, 35%, 30%, 25%, 20% growth), FCF margin ramping 25% to 35%, terminal g=3%, TV via Gordon = FCF_6 * (1+g)/(WACC-g). Sums PV of CFs ($250M) plus PV of TV ($850M) = ~$1.1B EV. Subtracts $50M net debt, $1.05B equity. Tools: Google Sheets, Damodaran ERP tables, CapIQ for peer WACC.

### 6.2 Product-Ops Candidate for BizOps Role: Reading a Peer-Comp Sheet
Candidate is handed a CapIQ export of 8 AI-infra peers, EV/Revenue range 5-25x. She segments by growth (>50% vs <30%) and rule-of-40 score, applies the high-growth median (18x) to the target's $80M revenue = $1.44B EV. Cross-checks with EV/ARR (similar). Tools: Excel, CapIQ, Looker dashboards for internal metrics. She explains "I picked revenue multiple because EBITDA is negative on most peers."

### 6.3 ANTI-EXAMPLE: Confusing EV with Equity Value in Acquisition Math
An AI-product-manager candidate in a case interview sees "EV $2B, Net Debt $400M" and quotes "walk-away price $2B of equity." Interviewer flags the $400M overpayment - proper equity value is $1.6B. The candidate had confused EV (what the business is worth) with equity (what shareholders receive). Scoring impact: case downgraded from "hire" to "no hire" on business-acumen axis, a ~20% interview-score hit on a typical 5-point scale. Quantified damage: $400M overpayment on a $2B deal = 20% value destruction for acquirer. Tools: would have avoided with Excel walk-away template, any CapIQ export, or Carta for cap-table sanity.
