# Practice Q&A — Intermediate

Analysis, comparison, and applied reasoning covering Q71 to Q140.

## Q71. How does a DCF differ from a comparable company analysis?

**Level:** Intermediate

DCF values a business from its own projected cash flows discounted at its cost of capital. Comparable company analysis values it using multiples of similar listed peers. DCF is intrinsic and model-driven, while comps are market-driven and reflect current sentiment.

**Real-life applications:**
- Cross-checking an IPO price band
- Supporting fairness opinions
- Advising boards on M&A bids
- Preparing investment committee decks
- Valuing private firms for tax purposes

**Key concepts:** `dcf`, `comparables`, `intrinsic`, `market-based`, `cross-check`

---

## Q72. Why is EBITDA not a perfect measure of cash flow?

**Level:** Intermediate

EBITDA ignores capex, changes in working capital, and taxes — all real cash outflows. It can look strong even when the company is burning cash to fund growth or inventory. A capex-heavy firm can show high EBITDA but negative free cash flow.

**Real-life applications:**
- Stress-testing LBO models
- Spotting accounting red flags
- Comparing capital-light vs. heavy firms
- Building clean DCF bridges
- Challenging management guidance

**Key concepts:** `ebitda`, `capex`, `working-capital`, `cash-flow`, `red-flags`

---

## Q73. How does leverage affect the cost of equity?

**Level:** Intermediate

More debt increases the volatility of equity returns because fixed interest payments amplify swings in residual profit. Investors demand a higher cost of equity to compensate. This is reflected by relevering beta in CAPM.

**Real-life applications:**
- Adjusting betas in peer analysis
- Pricing equity for new issues
- Evaluating LBO target economics
- Recomputing WACC after capital structure changes
- Advising on dividend recaps

**Key concepts:** `leverage`, `cost-of-equity`, `beta`, `capm`, `risk`

---

## Q74. What is the Gordon Growth model?

**Level:** Intermediate

The Gordon Growth model computes terminal value as the next period's free cash flow divided by (discount rate minus growth rate). It assumes perpetual, steady growth. It is widely used because it is simple and tractable.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing DCF terminal value
- Valuing stable utilities and infrastructure
- Pricing dividend-based equity models
- Cross-checking exit-multiple TVs
- Running long-horizon project analysis

**Key concepts:** `terminal-value`, `perpetuity`, `growth`, `dcf`, `discount`

---

## Q75. How do you pick a peer group for comps?

**Level:** Intermediate

Choose listed companies with similar industry, business model, size, growth rate, and geography. Fewer, cleaner comps beat many loose ones. Always explain inclusions and exclusions in the footnotes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building IPO pricing decks
- Defending fairness opinions
- Supporting transaction pitches
- Justifying private company valuations
- Running sector research

**Key concepts:** `peers`, `industry`, `size`, `growth`, `selection`

---

## Q76. What is the difference between strategic and financial buyers?

**Level:** Intermediate

Strategic buyers are corporates acquiring for operational synergies and long-term fit. Financial buyers are PE funds seeking return through leverage and operational improvement. Strategic buyers typically pay more because they can monetize synergies.

**Real-life applications:**
- Designing sale processes
- Setting price expectations
- Benchmarking precedent deals
- Structuring confidentiality
- Advising on timing

**Key concepts:** `strategic`, `financial`, `synergies`, `leverage`, `buyer-types`

---

## Q77. How are synergies categorized?

**Level:** Intermediate

Synergies are typically grouped as revenue synergies (cross-sell, new markets), cost synergies (overhead rationalization, procurement), tax synergies (loss utilization, domicile), and financial synergies (lower cost of capital). Cost synergies are the most reliable and revenue synergies the least.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building post-deal synergy plans
- Justifying acquisition premiums
- Setting integration milestones
- Reporting to boards
- Designing earn-outs

**Key concepts:** `revenue`, `cost`, `tax`, `financial`, `synergies`

---

## Q78. What is a sensitivity analysis in valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Sensitivity analysis varies key assumptions — growth rate, WACC, margin — to see how the valuation changes. It shows which inputs drive the answer most. Presenting sensitivity tables builds credibility with boards and investors.

**Real-life applications:**
- Preparing investment committee papers
- Stress-testing DCFs
- Defending fairness opinions
- Pricing risk of long-dated contracts
- Explaining risk to boards

**Key concepts:** `sensitivity`, `assumptions`, `driver`, `stress-test`, `dcf`

## Q79. How do you handle stock-based compensation in valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Stock-based compensation is a real economic cost even though it is non-cash. Mature valuation practice treats it as an expense in the P&L and does not add it back to cash flow. Ignoring it overstates value, particularly for tech companies.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building tech-company DCFs
- Normalizing reported EBITDA
- Pricing IPOs accurately
- Benchmarking SaaS multiples
- Reviewing management guidance

**Key concepts:** `sbc`, `non-cash`, `dilution`, `tech`, `normalization`

## Q80. What is the difference between book value and market value?

**Level:** Intermediate

Book value reflects historical accounting — cost minus depreciation and adjustments. Market value reflects what buyers will pay today. Gaps can be large because accounting lags and intangibles are often underreported.

**Real-life applications:**
- Judging price-to-book ratios
- Valuing banks and insurers
- Assessing distressed asset floors
- Pricing private stakes
- Reconciling fair value adjustments

**Key concepts:** `book`, `market`, `accounting`, `intangibles`, `fair-value`

---

## Q81. Why are tech companies often valued on revenue multiples?

**Level:** Intermediate

Many tech firms sacrifice near-term profit to grow fast, so earnings are low or negative. Revenue multiples work when losses are by design and scale economics are proven. Analysts pair revenue multiples with unit economics to avoid overpaying.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing SaaS start-ups
- Pricing marketplaces
- Benchmarking rule-of-40 peers
- Setting IPO price ranges
- Calibrating growth-stage funds

**Key concepts:** `revenue-multiple`, `scale`, `saas`, `growth`, `unit-economics`

---

## Q82. What is the rule of 40?

**Level:** Intermediate

The rule of 40 says a healthy SaaS company's growth rate plus operating margin should be at least 40. It captures the trade-off between growth and profitability. Below 40, investors get wary; above, they reward with higher multiples.

**Real-life applications:**
- Screening SaaS investments
- Setting management KPIs
- Benchmarking IPO candidates
- Valuing growth-stage firms
- Tracking portfolio health

**Key concepts:** `saas`, `growth`, `margin`, `benchmark`, `health`

---

## Q83. How do you compute FCFF from net income?

**Level:** Intermediate

Start with net income, add back after-tax interest, add depreciation and amortization, subtract capex, and subtract the increase in working capital. This rebuilds cash available to all capital providers. It is the most common bridge used in valuation models.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building DCF cash-flow schedules
- Normalizing cross-period FCFF
- Cross-checking management FCF claims
- Adjusting for non-recurring items
- Preparing investor reports

**Key concepts:** `net-income`, `interest`, `d&a`, `capex`, `working-capital`

---

## Q84. What is a two-stage DCF?

**Level:** Intermediate

A two-stage DCF models a high-growth phase followed by a stable-growth perpetuity. Stage one uses explicit forecasts, often 5–10 years. Stage two computes terminal value with the Gordon Growth model.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing growth-stage companies
- Modelling cyclical recoveries
- Pricing pharma pipelines
- Valuing emerging-market franchises
- Cross-checking comps outputs

**Key concepts:** `two-stage`, `growth-phase`, `terminal-value`, `dcf`, `perpetuity`

---

## Q85. What drives the choice between P/E and EV/EBITDA?

**Level:** Intermediate

P/E works when comparing firms with similar capital structure and tax rates. EV/EBITDA is better when leverage or tax differs across peers, because it is capital-structure-neutral. For cross-border or varied-leverage groups, EV/EBITDA is usually cleaner.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing banks vs. industrials
- Running cross-border peer sets
- Valuing leveraged targets
- Benchmarking LBO deals
- Writing fairness opinions

**Key concepts:** `p/e`, `ev/ebitda`, `leverage`, `tax`, `comparability`

---

## Q86. How do you adjust for non-recurring items?

**Level:** Intermediate

Identify one-off gains or losses — asset sales, litigation, restructuring — and remove them from reported earnings. This gives a normalized, sustainable base for valuation. Applying the multiple to reported instead of normalized earnings is a common trap.

**Real-life applications:**
- Normalizing target EBITDA
- Preparing bank credit reviews
- Building DCFs on clean baselines
- Writing prospectuses
- Adjusting bonus calculations

**Key concepts:** `non-recurring`, `normalization`, `ebitda`, `sustainable`, `adjustments`

---

## Q87. What is the impact of a rising WACC on valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

A higher WACC discounts future cash flows more heavily, reducing valuation. The effect is larger for long-dated cash flows and terminal value. Rising interest rates are a classic driver of falling valuations.

**Real-life applications:**
- Explaining tech sell-offs during rate hikes
- Repricing long-duration projects
- Renegotiating infrastructure deals
- Adjusting M&A valuations
- Updating investment committee assumptions

**Key concepts:** `wacc`, `rates`, `long-duration`, `terminal-value`, `sensitivity`

---

## Q88. How does growth rate affect terminal value?

**Level:** Intermediate

A higher perpetual growth rate increases terminal value because cash flows compound for longer. The terminal value is highly sensitive — a 1% change in growth rate can shift value 20% or more. Growth rate should not exceed long-term GDP growth.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing DCF assumptions
- Running sensitivity tables
- Challenging aggressive models
- Setting board-level valuation ranges
- Negotiating M&A offers

**Key concepts:** `growth`, `terminal-value`, `gdp`, `sensitivity`, `gordon`

---

## Q89. What is the difference between FCFF and FCFE?

**Level:** Intermediate

FCFF is cash available to all capital providers before financing costs. FCFE is cash available to equity only, after interest and net debt repayments. FCFF is discounted at WACC; FCFE at cost of equity.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing banks with FCFE
- Using FCFF for industrials
- Matching cash flow to discount rate
- Cross-checking leveraged targets
- Pricing project finance

**Key concepts:** `fcff`, `fcfe`, `wacc`, `cost-of-equity`, `match`

---

## Q90. Why is matching cash flow to discount rate important?

**Level:** Intermediate

Mismatching causes double-counting or omission of financing effects. Using FCFF with cost of equity understates debt risk. Using FCFE with WACC double-counts interest. Always pair FCFF with WACC and FCFE with cost of equity.

**Real-life applications:**
- Auditing DCF models
- Correcting junior analyst errors
- Training new hires
- Preparing investment decks
- Reviewing third-party fairness reports

**Key concepts:** `matching`, `fcff`, `fcfe`, `wacc`, `cost-of-equity`

---

## Q91. What is the difference between accretion and dilution?

**Level:** Intermediate

Accretion is when a deal raises the buyer's earnings per share. Dilution is when it lowers them. EPS impact is often the first test boards apply to an M&A proposal, though it ignores longer-term value creation.

**Real-life applications:**
- Analyzing stock-funded deals
- Advising boards on EPS impact
- Choosing cash vs. stock mix
- Communicating with analysts
- Modelling year-one results

**Key concepts:** `eps`, `accretion`, `dilution`, `m&a`, `stock-deal`

---

## Q92. How does deal structure (cash vs. stock) affect valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Cash deals give sellers certainty but force buyers to use balance sheet or take on debt. Stock deals share risk with sellers and preserve buyer cash, but dilute existing shareholders. The mix signals how confident the buyer is in the deal's upside.

**Real-life applications:**
- Structuring merger agreements
- Negotiating collars and ratios
- Balancing leverage impact
- Aligning seller incentives
- Advising on taxation

**Key concepts:** `cash`, `stock`, `structure`, `risk-sharing`, `dilution`

---

## Q93. Why do private companies trade at a discount to listed peers?

**Level:** Intermediate

Private shares cannot be sold easily, making them less attractive to investors. They also usually have lower disclosure, governance, and liquidity. Typical private company discounts are 15–30% of public comparable values.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing for private funding rounds
- Pricing pre-IPO stakes
- Negotiating private equity buyouts
- Settling estate taxes
- Valuing employee options

**Key concepts:** `private`, `illiquidity`, `governance`, `disclosure`, `discount`

---

## Q94. How does beta change with leverage?

**Level:** Intermediate

A higher debt-to-equity ratio raises equity beta because financial risk amplifies operating risk. To compare peer betas, analysts unlever them to asset beta and relever at the target's capital structure. This is the Hamada adjustment.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building peer-based betas
- Relevering for target capital structure
- Pricing equity after recap
- Advising on optimal leverage
- Computing WACC consistently

**Key concepts:** `beta`, `leverage`, `hamada`, `unlever`, `relever`

---

## Q95. What does a negative enterprise value imply?

**Level:** Intermediate

Negative enterprise value occurs when net cash exceeds market cap — the company's cash alone is worth more than its equity. It signals distressed sentiment or hidden liabilities. It sometimes creates opportunities for activist investors.

**Real-life applications:**
- Identifying activist targets
- Spotting market mispricings
- Screening deep-value investments
- Evaluating distressed firms
- Checking for hidden liabilities

**Key concepts:** `negative-ev`, `net-cash`, `distress`, `activism`, `deep-value`

## Q96. How do you value a loss-making company?

**Level:** Intermediate

Use revenue multiples, unit economics, and forward-looking DCFs with path-to-profitability assumptions. Scenario-based models help when earnings are years away. Look for operating leverage — how fast margins grow as revenue scales.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing early-stage SaaS

- Advising growth-stage rounds

## Q97. What is the purpose of a fairness opinion?

**Level:** Intermediate

A fairness opinion is a banker's letter stating whether a deal price is fair from a financial point of view. Boards use it to satisfy their duty of care to shareholders. It does not guarantee the best price, only that it is within a defensible range.

**Real-life applications:**
- Supporting board approvals
- Protecting against shareholder suits
- Documenting deal process
- Disclosing in proxies
- Guarding directors' liability

**Key concepts:** `fairness-opinion`, `board`, `defense`, `duty-of-care`, `bankers`

---

## Q98. How do working capital changes affect cash flow?

**Level:** Intermediate

An increase in receivables or inventory ties up cash, reducing operating cash flow. A rise in payables frees up cash. Analysts watch working capital because it is often a silent drag on reported earnings converting to cash.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building cash flow forecasts
- Sizing working capital loans
- Negotiating supplier terms
- Monitoring receivables collection
- Stress-testing liquidity

**Key concepts:** `working-capital`, `receivables`, `inventory`, `payables`, `cash-conversion`

---

## Q99. How do capex assumptions affect DCF valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Higher capex reduces free cash flow and DCF value. But under-capitalizing hurts long-term growth and terminal value. Healthy DCFs use maintenance capex plus growth capex aligned with revenue projections.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building long-horizon DCFs
- Evaluating asset-heavy targets
- Pricing infrastructure deals
- Testing management projections
- Running scenario analyses

**Key concepts:** `capex`, `maintenance`, `growth`, `fcf`, `long-term`

---

## Q100. What is the difference between earnings and cash flow?

**Level:** Intermediate

Earnings follow accrual accounting — recognizing revenue and expenses when earned, not when cash moves. Cash flow tracks actual money in and out. Earnings can be managed through estimates; cash flow is harder to manipulate.

**Real-life applications:**
- Detecting earnings manipulation
- Cross-checking reported profit
- Valuing on cash-flow basis
- Auditing financial statements
- Training credit analysts

**Key concepts:** `earnings`, `cash`, `accrual`, `quality`, `manipulation`

---

## Q101. What is a 5-year forecast in DCF?

**Level:** Intermediate

A 5-year forecast is the explicit projection period in a DCF before terminal value. Five years balance detail with credibility; beyond that, assumptions get speculative. Fast-changing businesses may justify longer explicit forecasts.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building standard DCFs
- Aligning with management plans
- Framing investor discussions
- Testing long-term viability
- Setting terminal year conditions

**Key concepts:** `forecast`, `explicit-period`, `terminal-year`, `dcf`, `5-year`

## Q102. Why do analysts cross-check DCF with comps?

**Level:** Intermediate

DCF is sensitive to assumptions, while comps reflect live market pricing. Using both triangulates a defensible value range. Large gaps between DCF and comp values are a signal to question assumptions on one side.

**Real-life applications:**
- Triangulating IPO prices
- Stress-testing bid prices
- Preparing investment memos
- Defending fairness opinions
- Advising on offer strategy

**Key concepts:** `dcf`, `comps`, `triangulate`, `cross-check`, `range`

## Q103. What is sum-of-the-parts valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) values each business segment separately and adds them. It reveals hidden value inside conglomerates. SOTP is often higher than the listed market cap, giving rise to a conglomerate discount.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing diversified conglomerates
- Arguing for spin-offs
- Advising activist investors
- Reporting segment value
- Setting restructuring targets

**Key concepts:** `sotp`, `segments`, `conglomerate-discount`, `spin-off`, `activism`

---

## Q104. What is a conglomerate discount?

**Level:** Intermediate

A conglomerate discount is the gap between a diversified group's market cap and the sum of its parts' fair values. It reflects complexity, capital misallocation, and lack of focus. Spin-offs can close the gap.

**Real-life applications:**
- Justifying demergers
- Pricing holding companies
- Advising on restructuring
- Engaging with activists
- Reporting to investors

**Key concepts:** `discount`, `diversified`, `sotp`, `focus`, `restructuring`

---

## Q105. How is a holding company valued?

**Level:** Intermediate

A holding company is often valued as the sum of its stakes in underlying businesses, less a holding discount. The discount reflects lack of operational control by minority investors. Listed holdcos routinely trade 20–40% below NAV.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing listed investment vehicles
- Pricing family office stakes
- Advising on restructuring
- Tracking NAV discount trends
- Supporting estate planning

**Key concepts:** `holdco`, `nav`, `discount`, `stakes`, `structure`

## Q106. Why might two analysts get different DCF values?

**Level:** Intermediate

Different growth rates, WACC, margins, or terminal value assumptions produce different outputs. Small changes compound over a 10-year horizon. That is why sensitivity analysis and clear assumption documentation matter.

**Real-life applications:**
- Reconciling research reports
- Debating investment committee views
- Understanding price target gaps
- Auditing model assumptions
- Communicating with investors

**Key concepts:** `assumptions`, `growth`, `wacc`, `sensitivity`, `range`

## Q107. How do you value an asset-heavy company?

**Level:** Intermediate

Use a combination of DCF, EV/EBITDA, and asset-based approaches. Book values and replacement costs matter more than for asset-light firms. Watch for hidden value in real estate or equipment at historic cost.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing manufacturing companies
- Pricing utility assets
- Assessing shipping firms
- Benchmarking real estate
- Reporting replacement cost

**Key concepts:** `asset-heavy`, `replacement`, `book-value`, `ev/ebitda`, `dcf`

## Q108. How do you value an asset-light company?

**Level:** Intermediate

Asset-light firms — consulting, tech, brands — are valued mostly on earnings power and cash flow. Balance sheets carry little tangible value, so book value is irrelevant. Focus on recurring revenue, margin durability, and growth.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing SaaS firms
- Pricing consulting franchises
- Benchmarking agencies
- Valuing brand-led retailers
- Pricing licensing businesses

**Key concepts:** `asset-light`, `earnings-power`, `intangibles`, `recurring`, `margin`

## Q109. What is a control premium and who pays it?

**Level:** Intermediate

A control premium is the extra amount paid above minority market price to gain a majority stake. Acquirers pay it because they gain synergies, cash-flow control, and strategic upside. Without a control premium, few takeovers would close.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting takeover offers
- Justifying M&A prices
- Advising target boards
- Benchmarking premium ranges
- Pricing majority blocks

**Key concepts:** `control`, `premium`, `majority`, `synergies`, `takeover`

---

## Q110. What is a minority discount?

**Level:** Intermediate

A minority discount is a valuation haircut for stakes that do not give operational control. It accounts for the absence of voting and decision-making power. It is typically the inverse of the control premium, often 15–25%.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing minority stakes
- Pricing partial buyouts
- Settling shareholder disputes
- Estate tax valuations
- Joint venture accounting

**Key concepts:** `minority`, `discount`, `control`, `voting`, `stake`

**Real-life applications:**

## Q113. How does country risk affect valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Higher country risk raises the discount rate, typically through a country risk premium added to WACC. It reflects political, currency, and macroeconomic instability. Cross-border deals must quantify it carefully to avoid overpaying.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing emerging-market subs
- Pricing cross-border deals

## Q114. What is the CAPM formula?

**Level:** Intermediate

CAPM is cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × (equity risk premium). It translates market risk into a required return. It is the most widely used framework despite its simplifying assumptions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Computing cost of equity
- Pricing listed equities
- Setting corporate hurdle rates
- Valuing private firms
- Analyzing stock returns

**Key concepts:** `capm`, `risk-free`, `beta`, `premium`, `cost-of-equity`

---

## Q115. What are the limitations of CAPM?

**Level:** Intermediate

CAPM assumes markets are efficient, investors hold diversified portfolios, and beta captures all relevant risk. In practice, size, momentum, and quality factors also matter. Analysts often supplement CAPM with multifactor models.

**Real-life applications:**
- Choosing factor models
- Justifying premium adjustments
- Comparing academic and practical rates
- Running internal reviews
- Training juniors on limitations

**Key concepts:** `capm`, `limitations`, `assumptions`, `factors`, `practice`

---

## Q116. What is a leveraged recapitalization?

**Level:** Intermediate

A leveraged recap is when a company takes on new debt to return cash to shareholders via a special dividend or buyback. It changes the capital structure but not the operating business. It often raises the cost of equity but lowers WACC in moderate doses.

**Real-life applications:**
- Private equity dividend recaps
- Listed company buybacks

## Q117. How is debt capacity measured?

**Level:** Intermediate

Debt capacity is typically set by leverage ratios (debt/EBITDA) and interest coverage (EBITDA/interest). Lenders set covenants around these. Stable cash-flow businesses can safely carry higher leverage than cyclical ones.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sizing LBO debt
- Setting lender covenants

## Q118. What is a covenant in lending?

**Level:** Intermediate

A covenant is a condition lenders attach to a loan, such as maintaining a maximum leverage ratio. Breaching covenants can trigger defaults even if payments are on time. Covenant packages are central to LBO and corporate debt design.

**Real-life applications:**
- Structuring LBO debt
- Negotiating loan agreements
- Monitoring borrower compliance
- Managing waiver requests

**Key concepts:** `covenant`, `leverage`, `coverage`, `default`, `lender`

## Q119. What is IRR vs. NPV?

**Level:** Intermediate

IRR is the discount rate that makes a project's NPV zero — an annualized return. NPV is the absolute rupee value created at a given discount rate. NPV is better for ranking mutually exclusive projects; IRR is better for communicating returns.

**Real-life applications:**
- Ranking capex proposals
- Comparing investment returns
- Communicating to PE LPs
- Evaluating concessions
- Benchmarking project finance

**Key concepts:** `irr`, `npv`, `discount-rate`, `projects`, `comparison`

---

## Q120. What is payback period?

**Level:** Intermediate

Payback period is the time taken to recover the original investment from cash flows. It is simple but ignores the time value of money and post-payback returns. Use it as a liquidity check, not the main investment criterion.

**Real-life applications:**
- Quick liquidity screens
- Managing short-tenor projects
- Gauging capital recovery risk
- Comparing quick-payoff deals
- Supporting lending covenants

**Key concepts:** `payback`, `recovery`, `liquidity`, `simple`, `time-value`

---

## Q121. What is discounted payback?

**Level:** Intermediate

Discounted payback applies a discount rate to the cash flows before computing the payback time. It corrects the time-value flaw of the simple payback method. It still ignores cash flows after payback is reached.

**Real-life applications:**
- Evaluating long-horizon capex
- Stress-testing recovery risk
- Upgrading simple payback rules
- Supporting board capex reviews
- Communicating liquidity profiles

**Key concepts:** `discounted-payback`, `time-value`, `recovery`, `capex`, `risk`

---

## Q122. What is the difference between strategic value and standalone value?

**Level:** Intermediate

Standalone value is what the target is worth on its own. Strategic value includes synergies and strategic options unique to a specific buyer. Strategic value is always higher for the right buyer — the question is how much they will share with the seller.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting bid walk-away prices
- Running auction strategies
- Advising target boards
- Benchmarking acquirers
- Designing deal terms

## Q123. What is a DCF terminal multiple cross-check?

**Level:** Intermediate

Compute the implied EV/EBITDA or P/E multiple at the terminal year and compare with market multiples. If the implied multiple is far above peers, terminal assumptions are likely too aggressive. It is a quick reality test for DCF output.

**Real-life applications:**
- Auditing DCF models
- Training analysts
- Writing valuation memos
- Debating with committees
- Refining growth assumptions

**Key concepts:** `terminal`, `implied-multiple`, `cross-check`, `ebitda`, `reality-test`

---

## Q124. How do you value a cyclical company?

**Level:** Intermediate

Normalize earnings across the cycle — use a mid-cycle or average period. Avoid using peak or trough numbers. Apply multiples to normalized earnings and adjust for cycle position.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing steel, cement, autos
- Pricing commodity firms
- Advising on cycle timing
- Running scenario-based DCFs
- Benchmarking peer multiples

**Key concepts:** `cyclical`, `normalized`, `mid-cycle`, `peaks-troughs`, `scenario`

## Q125. What is a normalized EBITDA?

**Level:** Intermediate

Normalized EBITDA adjusts reported EBITDA for non-recurring items, owner-manager benefits, and unusual costs. The goal is a sustainable, repeatable number that buyers can underwrite. It is especially important for small-cap and private company sales.

**Real-life applications:**
- Preparing sell-side financials
- Writing quality of earnings reports
- Pricing M&A transactions
- Supporting bank financing
- Defending seller valuations

**Key concepts:** `normalization`, `adjustments`, `sustainable`, `qoe`, `sell-side`

## Q126. What is a quality of earnings (QoE) review?

**Level:** Intermediate

A QoE review, usually by an audit or advisory firm, analyzes the sustainability of reported earnings. It identifies one-offs, accounting choices, and working capital distortions. Buyers and lenders rely heavily on QoE reports in M&A.

**Real-life applications:**
- Supporting M&A diligence
- Confirming lender assumptions
- Normalizing EBITDA
- Challenging management numbers
- Pricing deferred consideration

**Key concepts:** `qoe`, `sustainability`, `diligence`, `adjustments`, `earnings`

## Q127. What is an enterprise DCF vs. an equity DCF?

**Level:** Intermediate

Enterprise DCF values the whole business using FCFF and WACC, yielding enterprise value. Equity DCF uses FCFE and cost of equity, yielding equity value directly. Each is correct when the matching cash flow and rate are used.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing industrials and SaaS
- Pricing banks and insurers
- Matching cash flow to rate
- Training valuation teams
- Building model standards

**Key concepts:** `enterprise-dcf`, `equity-dcf`, `fcff`, `fcfe`, `matching`

## Q128. Why are banks valued differently?

**Level:** Intermediate

Banks are regulated capital machines where debt is part of raw material, not just financing. Analysts use equity DCF with FCFE, dividend discount models, or price-to-book multiples. Standard EV/EBITDA does not apply cleanly.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing banks with P/B
- Applying dividend discount models
- Running regulatory capital stress
- Pricing insurance subs
- Building bank M&A models

**Key concepts:** `banks`, `p/b`, `fcfe`, `regulation`, `capital`

## Q129. What is embedded value in insurance?

**Level:** Intermediate

Embedded value is the sum of a life insurer's net asset value plus the present value of profits from the in-force book. It is the industry's preferred valuation metric. It strips out the distortion of long policy tails and accounting conservatism.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing life insurers
- Advising on insurance M&A
- Reporting to regulators
- Communicating to investors
- Setting management targets

**Key concepts:** `embedded-value`, `insurance`, `in-force`, `life`, `valuation`

## Q130. What is a dividend discount model (DDM)?

**Level:** Intermediate

A DDM values a stock as the present value of expected future dividends. It works best for mature, dividend-paying firms. It is a special case of equity DCF where all FCFE is assumed paid as dividends.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing utilities
- Valuing regulated banks
- Pricing dividend aristocrats
- Benchmarking yield-based products
- Teaching basic valuation

**Key concepts:** `ddm`, `dividends`, `present-value`, `mature`, `yield`

## Q131. How do you value a bond?

**Level:** Intermediate

Discount the bond's coupon payments and principal at the required yield. Higher yields imply lower bond prices. Duration measures price sensitivity to yield changes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pricing corporate bonds
- Valuing fixed income portfolios
- Setting bond issue prices
- Managing duration risk
- Trading fixed income

**Key concepts:** `bond`, `coupon`, `yield`, `duration`, `present-value`

## Q132. What is yield to maturity (YTM)?

**Level:** Intermediate

YTM is the total return an investor earns if the bond is held to maturity. It equates the bond's price with the present value of future cash flows. It is the bond-market equivalent of IRR.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing bond returns

- Computing spread to benchmarks
- Evaluating trade ideas

**Key concepts:** `ytm`, `bond`, `return`, `irr`, `maturity`

## Q133. How does inflation affect valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Inflation raises nominal cash flows but also nominal discount rates. In theory, the effects cancel, but in practice inflation also changes margins, capex, and working capital needs. Sensitivity analysis matters in high-inflation markets.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing emerging-market firms
- Pricing long-duration assets
- Running inflation scenarios
- Advising on pricing power
- Managing currency exposure

**Key concepts:** `inflation`, `nominal`, `real`, `margin`, `capex`

## Q134. What is the difference between nominal and real cash flows?

**Level:** Intermediate

Nominal cash flows include inflation; real cash flows strip it out. Match the discount rate consistently — nominal cash flow with nominal rate, real with real. Mixing them distorts valuation significantly.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing long-dated projects
- Comparing across currencies
- Adjusting for hyperinflation
- Teaching DCF discipline
- Running scenario models

**Key concepts:** `nominal`, `real`, `inflation`, `discount-rate`, `consistency`

## Q135. How does currency affect cross-border valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Forecast cash flows in the local currency, discount at a local-currency WACC, and translate the final value. Or forecast in the home currency using consistent FX forward rates. Mixing approaches causes distortions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing foreign subsidiaries
- Pricing cross-border M&A
- Managing FX risk
- Running consolidated reports
- Advising global corporates

**Key concepts:** `currency`, `fx`, `local`, `consolidated`, `consistency`

## Q136. What is a scenario analysis vs. sensitivity analysis?

**Level:** Intermediate

Sensitivity varies one input at a time to see marginal impact. Scenarios change a coherent bundle of inputs — like a recession or boom case. Scenarios tell a richer story about how the business could perform.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pitching investment committees

- Reporting to boards

## Q137. What is a Monte Carlo simulation in valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Monte Carlo runs thousands of scenarios using probability distributions on key inputs. It produces a distribution of outcomes rather than a single number. It is useful for complex projects or options.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q138. What is real options analysis?

**Level:** Intermediate

Real options analysis values the flexibility to expand, delay, or abandon a project. It extends DCF by treating managerial choices as options. It often raises the value of uncertain, high-optionality projects.

**Level:** Intermediate

A leverage ratio compares debt to EBITDA, equity, or assets. Debt/EBITDA is the most common operational measure. Higher leverage ratios mean higher financial risk and tighter covenants.

## 4. Formulas

1. **Hamada Beta Re-lever:** β_L = β_U · [1 + (1−t)·(D/E)]
   Threshold: re-lever to target, not book, capital structure; t = marginal cash tax rate.
   *Example:* Peer β_U = 1.1; target D/E = 0.4; t = 25% → β_L = 1.1 · (1 + 0.75·0.4) = 1.43 for an Indian AI-SaaS target.

2. **WACC:** WACC = (E/V)·Re + (D/V)·Rd·(1−t); Re = Rf + β·ERP + Size + CRP
   Threshold: WACC typically 9–14% for tech in India; <8% is a red flag.
   *Example:* Rf = 7.1% (GoI 10Y), ERP = 6%, β = 1.43, Size = 1%, CRP = 0 → Re = 16.7%; Rd·(1−t) = 8·0.75 = 6% → WACC at D/V=30% → 13.5%.

3. **FCFF to FCFE Bridge:** FCFE = FCFF − Interest·(1−t) + Net Borrowing
   Threshold: consistent denominator — FCFE pairs with Re; FCFF with WACC. Never cross them.
   *Example:* FCFF = ₹80cr, Interest = ₹20cr, t = 25%, Net Borrowing = +₹10cr → FCFE = 80 − 15 + 10 = ₹75cr.

4. **Gordon / Implied g:** TV = FCFF_{n+1} / (WACC − g); Implied g = WACC − FCFF_{n+1}/TV
   Threshold: g should be ≤ long-term nominal GDP (~5–6% India, ~4% US); sanity-check exit-multiple TV.
   *Example:* If exit multiple implies TV = ₹2,000cr and FCFF_{n+1}= ₹130cr at WACC=13%, implied g = 13% − 6.5% = 6.5% — at the upper edge, flag.

5. **LBO Returns:** MoIC = Exit Equity / Entry Equity; IRR solved from (Exit/Entry)^(1/n) − 1
   Threshold: PE target IRR 20–25%; MoIC ≥ 2.5x over 5 yrs.
   *Example:* Entry ₹100cr, Exit ₹300cr, 5 yrs → MoIC 3.0x, IRR ≈ 24.6%.

6. **EV Identity:** EV = Equity + Debt + Minority + Preferred + Leases + Pension − Excess Cash
   Threshold: never miss operating leases (IFRS 16) post-2019.
   *Example:* AI-SaaS firm: Equity ₹800cr + Debt ₹200cr + Leases ₹50cr − Cash ₹150cr = EV ₹900cr.

7. **Accretion/Dilution:** ΔEPS = [Target NI + Syn·(1−t) − After-tax Financing Cost] / PF Shares − Current EPS
   Threshold: >+2% accretive Y1 is a selling point; dilution >3% usually kills pitches.
   *Example:* Acquiring consulting firm adds NI ₹60cr + ₹20cr synergy − ₹25cr interest; new shares 10cr; PF shares 110cr → PF EPS ₹(Existing NI + 55)/110 vs ₹Existing/100.

8. **Burn Multiple (SaaS):** Burn Multiple = Net Burn / Net New ARR
   Threshold: <1 excellent; 1–2 healthy; >3 investigate.
   *Example:* Gen-AI startup burns ₹30cr/quarter, adds ₹15cr net new ARR → Burn Multiple 2.0 = OK but monitor.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Unlever peer β, re-lever at target D/E | Use peer β_L directly across different capital structures |
| Use market-value D/E weights in WACC | Use book D/E — distorts tech firms with low book equity |
| Cross-check DCF TV by implied growth AND implied exit multiple | Accept one TV method without sanity check |
| Haircut synergies (50–70% realisation) | Credit 100% of announced synergies in accretion math |
| Apply TSM for options/RSUs in diluted share count | Use basic shares for equity value per share |
| Cap terminal g at long-term nominal GDP | Set g > GDP — implies firm eventually dwarfs economy |
| Include operating leases (IFRS 16) in Net Debt | Ignore leases — understates EV for retail/airlines |
| Use forward multiples for growth firms | Mix forward multiples with trailing EBITDA |
| Segment WACC for diversified firms | Apply one firm-wide WACC to bank + industrial segments |
| Treat SBC as a real cost in FCF | Add SBC back and claim it's "non-cash" for SaaS FCF |
| Use harmonic/median for multiples | Use arithmetic mean — high outliers dominate |
| Decompose control premium into synergy + hype | Attribute entire premium to synergies |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios

### Scenario 1 — BizOps Analyst Builds 3-Statement DCF (AI-SaaS target)
BizOps analyst in a consulting firm is asked to model a ₹500cr ARR AI-SaaS target. Over 3 days they build: revenue by ARR × NRR (115%), COGS with GPU infra, S&M spend tied to Magic Number = 0.9, full debt schedule with a ₹100cr revolver, and cash sweep at 75%. DCF run at WACC 13.5%, TV via exit multiple 10x EBITDA; cross-checked against implied g = 5.8% (acceptable). Output: EV ₹4,200cr; triangulated against comps (median EV/ARR = 8x) = ₹4,000cr. Tools used: Excel + Anaplan for scenario toggles, CapIQ for peer multiples, ChartMogul for NRR/churn input.

### Scenario 2 — PE Associate Sizes LBO (IT-services carve-out)
Early-associate at a growth-PE fund sizes a ₹1,500cr LBO of an IT-services carve-out. Capital stack: 60% debt (senior 4.0x + mezz 1.5x EBITDA, mezz has 50% PIK), 40% equity. Builds 5-yr model with EBITDA growing 12%→16% margin; exit at 9x (vs entry 8x); debt paydown ₹400cr via 100% sweep. Value-creation bridge: EBITDA growth ₹350cr + multiple expansion ₹120cr + debt paydown ₹400cr − fees ₹50cr = exit equity ₹1,420cr on ₹600cr entry → MoIC 2.4x, IRR 19%. Sensitivity grid WACC × exit multiple shows IRR 14–24%. Tools: Excel, PitchBook for precedents, Damodaran for β-unlever table.

### Scenario 3 — ANTI-EXAMPLE (Product Manager on Strategy Rotation)
PM rotating into corporate-strategy builds a DCF for a ₹1,200cr consumer-tech target. Discounts FCFF using **cost of equity (Re 15%)** instead of WACC (10.5%) — mismatch in denominator. Result: EV undervalued by ~₹400cr. Pitched to CFO, who spots the error in 90 seconds. Cost: (a) 3 weeks of rework, (b) lost pole position in the M&A pursuit — competing bidder tabled at ₹1,100cr while team was re-running model, (c) PM removed from this deal team; next rotation delayed 6 months. Lesson: FCFF ⇔ WACC; FCFE ⇔ Re. Never cross.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. **Scope** the valuation: pull source docs (CIM, filings, management model) and confirm EV- or equity-value objective.
2. **Build** the 3-statement model with fully linked IS→CF→BS and a debt schedule with revolver + cash sweep.

8. **Draft** IC memo: thesis, value-creation bridge (if LBO), sensitivities, red flags, and role-lens question for the decision-maker.

- SAFE mechanics — cap vs discount interaction and MFN clauses need a worked example.
- Modern burn multiple — source still emphasises CAC payback only.
- Cycle-adjusted precedent comps — no mention of normalising 2021 peak tech multiples.
- Segment WACC for diversified firms (e.g., IT-services + BPO + products under one HoldCo).

## 9. Quick-Recall Card
- DCF: FCFF⇔WACC, FCFE⇔Re. Never cross.
- TV: always cross-check implied g and implied exit multiple; cap g ≤ nominal GDP.
- Comps: harmonic/median, forward + trailing, cycle-adjust precedents.
- LBO returns: debt paydown + EBITDA growth + multiple expansion − fees.
- EV↔Equity: subtract debt/leases/minority/preferred, add excess cash, use TSM shares.
