# Business Planning

## Overview

Business planning is the process of defining what a venture will do, how it will make money, and what resources it needs to get started. A business plan documents the value proposition, target market, revenue model, cost structure, and key milestones. It serves as both a roadmap for the founder and a communication tool for investors and partners.

---

## Why It Matters

Starting a venture without a plan is like driving cross-country without a map. You might get somewhere, but you will waste fuel and miss turns. A clear business plan forces founders to test assumptions on paper before spending real money, and it gives investors the confidence that the team has thought through the hard questions.

## Key Principles

- Start with the customer problem, not the product features
- Keep financial projections honest and grounded in verifiable assumptions
- A lean business plan that evolves beats a 50-page document that gathers dust
- Identify key risks upfront and explain how you will mitigate them
- Update the plan regularly as you learn from the market

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Value Proposition** | A clear statement of the benefit a product delivers and why customers should choose it over alternatives |
| **Revenue Model** | The mechanism through which a business earns money, such as subscriptions, per-unit sales, or advertising |
| **Business Model Canvas** | A one-page visual framework that maps nine building blocks of a business, from customer segments to cost structure |
| **Break-Even Point** | The sales volume at which total revenue equals total costs and the business starts generating profit |

## Use Case

Two co-founders want to launch a pet-sitting marketplace. Before writing any code, they use the Business Model Canvas to map customer segments, channels, and revenue streams. They discover that their biggest risk is not building the app but convincing pet owners to trust strangers, so they design a vetting and insurance system as part of the core offering.

## Scenario

> A university student pitched a campus delivery app to an angel investor. The investor asked three questions: unit economics, customer acquisition cost, and monthly burn rate. Because the student had built a simple financial model, she answered all three on the spot. The investor wrote a check the same week, citing the founder's clarity as the deciding factor.

## Examples

- Dropbox validated its business plan by releasing a simple explainer video before building the product; 70,000 people signed up for the waitlist overnight, proving demand
- A social enterprise in India used a one-page lean plan to secure a government grant for solar lantern distribution in rural villages, launching within 60 days of approval

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Business planning turns an idea into an executable operating and funding logic. For an IT, AI, product, or consulting leader, the plan is the artifact that links customer problem, value proposition, revenue model, and resource needs to a decision on whether to build, fund, or kill the initiative.

It matters because it forces assumptions onto paper before capital, engineering time, and market credibility are spent. The practical question it answers is: "Is this venture worth backing, and what evidence would change that answer?"

## 2. Jargon & Terminology

| Term | Full Form | Plain-English Meaning | Why It Exists | How It's Measured | Where You'll Hear It |
|------|-----------|----------------------|---------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Value Proposition | - | The customer benefit promise that explains why someone should choose this offer | To connect a real problem to a clear solution | Customer interviews, conversion, retention, willingness to pay | Startup pitches, product strategy, consulting decks |
| Revenue Model | - | The way the business makes money | To convert value delivered into cash collected | ARPU, average order value, subscription revenue, take rate | Business plans, finance reviews, board decks |
| Business Model Canvas | BMC | A one-page map of the business across nine building blocks | To make the model visible and comparable | Completeness of the nine blocks and consistency between them | Founder workshops, strategy sessions, incubators |
| Break-Even Point | - | The sales level where revenue equals total cost | To show when the venture stops losing money | Units sold or revenue at break-even | FP&A, fundraising, pricing discussions |
| Customer Acquisition Cost | CAC | The cost to win one customer | To test whether growth is economical | Marketing and sales spend divided by new customers | Growth meetings, SaaS unit economics, investor Q&A |
| Monthly Burn Rate | - | Net cash lost each month | To know how fast cash is being consumed | Cash outflow minus inflow per month | Startup finance, runway planning |

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Business Model Canvas
**Purpose:** To check whether the venture logic is complete and internally consistent.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Key Partners | Key Activities | Key Resources
Customer Relationships | Value Proposition | Channels
Customer Segments | Revenue Streams | Cost Structure
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Customer Segments: who pays and who uses the offer.
Value Proposition: the problem solved and the promised outcome.
Channels: how the offer reaches the customer.
Customer Relationships: how trust, support, and retention are managed.
Revenue Streams: how money comes in.
Key Activities: what must be done well every week.
Key Resources: people, data, IP, tools, and capital required.
Key Partners: outside dependencies that reduce cost or risk.
Cost Structure: fixed and variable costs that determine viability.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A B2B AI workflow tool maps users as operations managers, buyers as CIOs, channels as partner-led selling, and revenue as annual subscriptions. The canvas shows the real bottleneck is integration and security review, not model quality, so the launch plan prioritizes SOC 2 readiness and ERP connectors.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When stakeholders are arguing about features but not agreeing on the business logic.

### Break-Even Analysis
**Purpose:** To determine the minimum sales needed before the venture becomes profitable.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Revenue
  ^
  |        /
  |       /
  |      /   profit
  |-----x----------------> Units sold
  |    /|
  |   / | costs
  |  /  |
  | /   |
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Fixed costs: expenses that do not change with volume in the short run.
Variable cost per unit: incremental cost for each sale.
Price per unit: what the customer pays.
Break-even quantity: the point where total revenue equals total cost.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting-led AI implementation offer charges $40,000 per project, has $12,000 variable delivery cost, and $180,000 fixed monthly overhead. Break-even units are 180,000 / (40,000 - 12,000) = 6.43, so the team needs 7 projects per month to avoid losses.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When pricing or headcount decisions need a hard viability threshold.

### Lean Planning Loop
**Purpose:** To keep the plan alive by tying assumptions to experiments.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Assumption -> Test -> Evidence -> Update -> Next decision
```
Components explained:
Assumption: a belief about demand, pricing, retention, or channel.
Test: the smallest credible experiment.
Evidence: the observed result.
Update: what changes in the plan after learning.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team assumes a weekly AI summary feature will raise retention. It tests with 50 users, measures usage and renewal intent, then either scales the feature or drops it before building expensive automation.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team needs a fast, disciplined way to reduce uncertainty.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: `Break-even units = Fixed costs / (Price per unit - Variable cost per unit)`
Variables:
Fixed costs = costs that stay constant over the decision horizon
Price per unit = revenue earned from one sale or one customer period
Variable cost per unit = direct cost of serving one sale
Why this formula exists: It answers how much volume the venture needs before it stops losing money.
How to interpret the output:
Value < target volume -> the plan is underpowered -> reduce costs or raise price
Value around target volume -> the plan is fragile -> improve conversion or margin
Value > target volume -> the plan is viable only with scale -> validate demand before expanding
Worked example with numbers: Fixed costs = 300,000, price = 50, variable cost = 20. Break-even units = 300,000 / 30 = 10,000 units.

Formula: `Monthly burn rate = Cash outflows - Cash inflows`
Variables:
Cash outflows = salaries, cloud spend, marketing, rent, and other spend
Cash inflows = revenue and funding receipts in the same month
Why this formula exists: It shows how quickly the venture is consuming cash.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> cash is being generated -> growth can be funded internally
Value 0 to moderate positive -> manageable burn -> track runway closely
Value high positive -> urgent funding risk -> cut spend or raise capital
Worked example with numbers: Outflows = 120,000 and inflows = 45,000, so burn rate = 75,000 per month.

Formula: `Runway (months) = Cash balance / Monthly burn rate`
Variables:
Cash balance = available cash in bank
Monthly burn rate = net monthly cash consumption
Why this formula exists: It tells founders when they will run out of money.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 3 months -> emergency mode -> reduce spend and prioritize fundraising
Value 3-6 months -> warning zone -> tighten plan and pipeline
Value > 6 months -> healthier buffer -> execute with discipline
Worked example with numbers: Cash balance = 600,000 and burn rate = 75,000, so runway = 8 months.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Write the plan around features first | Start with a customer problem and a measurable outcome |
| Assume demand without evidence | Tie each demand claim to interviews, pilots, or conversion data |
| Treat the revenue model as a footnote | Make revenue timing and collection explicit |
| Ignore fixed and variable costs | Build break-even math into the first draft |
| Lock the plan for a year | Update the plan after each learning loop or milestone review |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI workflow startup deciding whether to raise seed capital
Situation: The team has a prototype, 12 design partners, and a subscription model. The question is whether the plan is investable or still too speculative.
Applicable framework/metric: Business Model Canvas plus runway.
Analysis: If fixed costs are $220,000 per month and expected contribution margin is $18,000 per customer per year, the team cannot justify a burn of $90,000 per month unless customer proof is already strong. With $540,000 in cash, runway is 6 months, which is enough for one focused validation cycle.
Decision rule: If runway is below 6 months and paid pilots are below 5, slow hiring. If runway is 6-9 months and pilots are 5-15, continue with strict milestones. If paid pilots exceed 15, prepare the fundraise.
Action: Build a one-page model, set weekly pilot conversion targets, and make funding contingent on signed annual contracts.

Scenario 2: Consulting firm launching a new digital offer
Situation: A consulting team wants to sell a diagnostics-plus-dashboard package to mid-market clients. The issue is whether the pricing covers delivery and sales effort.
Applicable framework/metric: Break-even analysis and CAC.
Analysis: If one project brings $35,000 revenue, costs $10,000 to deliver, and $5,000 of sales effort is needed, contribution is $20,000. At $200,000 fixed monthly overhead, break-even is 10 projects per month. If CAC rises above $6,000, payback starts to stretch beyond acceptable sales cycles.
Decision rule: If break-even volume is above current pipeline capacity, reprice or narrow the offer. If CAC is falling and pipeline supports 10+ projects, scale. If the model depends on one large client, de-risk it before expansion.
Action: Separate delivery cost from selling cost, test a pilot package, and track CAC payback monthly.

Scenario 3: Product team deciding whether to build a premium tier
Situation: A product manager wants to add an enterprise tier with SSO, audit logs, and admin controls. The team needs to know whether this is a revenue model upgrade or just feature creep.
Applicable framework/metric: Revenue model and lean planning loop.
Analysis: If the new tier increases ARPU by $800 per customer per year but adds $300 in support and infrastructure cost, the incremental margin is $500. A pilot with 20 customers should generate at least $10,000 incremental annual margin to justify the roadmap slot.
Decision rule: If pilot willingness to pay is below the cost uplift, do not build. If willingness to pay is marginal, test with manual service first. If customers commit in writing, automate the capability.
Action: Run a concierge pilot, price the tier before building it, and review retention after 60 days.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Draft a one-page business model canvas that names the buyer, user, pain point, and revenue stream.
2. Convert the canvas into a simple operating model with assumptions for volume, pricing, margin, and fixed costs.
3. Calculate break-even units, monthly burn rate, and runway using conservative inputs.
4. Define 3 to 5 milestones that would materially reduce uncertainty, such as paid pilots, LOIs, or retention thresholds.
5. Assign each assumption to a validation test with an owner, deadline, and pass/fail criterion.
6. Review the plan weekly and update any assumption that lacks evidence.
7. Use the plan as an investor and management communication artifact, not as a static document.
