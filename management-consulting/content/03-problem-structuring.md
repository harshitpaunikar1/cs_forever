# Problem Structuring

## Overview

Problem structuring is the practice of breaking a complex, messy business question into smaller, manageable pieces that can be analyzed individually. Consultants use frameworks like issue trees, hypothesis trees, and MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive) breakdowns to ensure nothing important is missed and no effort is wasted on overlapping areas.

---

## Why It Matters

Most business problems look overwhelming at first glance — declining profits, unhappy customers, failed product launches. Without structure, teams chase symptoms instead of root causes, duplicate effort, and miss critical factors. A well-structured problem cuts analysis time in half and ensures the final recommendation addresses the real issue, not just the loudest complaint.

## Key Principles

- Start with a single, clearly worded problem statement before breaking it down
- Use MECE logic so that sub-problems do not overlap and together cover the full issue
- Hypothesis-driven structuring lets you test assumptions early instead of boiling the ocean
- Keep the structure simple — three to five branches at each level is ideal
- Revisit and adjust the structure as new data comes in during the engagement

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **MECE** | Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive — a way of dividing a problem so categories do not overlap and nothing is left out |
| **Issue Tree** | A visual diagram that breaks a broad question into progressively narrower sub-questions |
| **Hypothesis Tree** | Similar to an issue tree but starts with a proposed answer and maps the evidence needed to prove or disprove it |
| **Root Cause** | The fundamental reason a problem exists, as opposed to its visible symptoms |

## Use Case

A consumer electronics company sees falling revenue. The consulting team structures the problem into three branches: volume decline, price erosion, and product mix shift. Each branch is then analyzed with its own data set, allowing the team to pinpoint that volume is stable but average selling price has dropped 15% due to aggressive competitor discounting.

## Scenario

> A national pizza chain asked consultants why profitability fell despite rising sales. The team built an issue tree splitting profit into revenue and cost branches. Revenue was up 8%, but cost analysis revealed that ingredient costs rose 20% after a supplier contract expired. The structured approach led directly to renegotiating supplier terms and introducing a smaller menu to reduce waste, restoring margins within two quarters.

## Examples

- A consultant uses a MECE breakdown to split customer churn into voluntary (price, service, competition) and involuntary (payment failure, relocation) categories, ensuring every lost customer is accounted for
- An issue tree for "Why is our new product underperforming?" branches into awareness, trial, repeat purchase, and distribution — each investigated by a separate analyst

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Problem structuring is the discipline of turning a vague business headache into a clear, testable question. It is the first real consulting move after the client says, “something is wrong.”

For an IT, AI, Product, or Consulting leader, this matters because the wrong structure wastes time even when the analysis is technically strong. A good structure tells the team what to look at, what to ignore, and which hypotheses can be disproven quickly.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### MECE Breakdown Tree
**Purpose:** Split a problem into branches that do not overlap and together cover the whole issue.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Problem
├─ Revenue
├─ Cost
└─ Mix / productivity / utilization
```

Axes / Components explained:
Component 1: completeness, meaning all major causes are included.
Component 2: exclusivity, meaning branches do not double count.
Component 3: testability, meaning each branch can be checked with data.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company’s declining profit can be split into acquisition, retention, pricing, and support-cost branches. The consultant then checks each branch separately rather than mixing all four together.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when the team has many ideas but no clean analytic structure.

### Hypothesis-First Tree
**Purpose:** Start with the most likely answers and test them in order of value.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Problem -> likely cause 1 -> evidence
       -> likely cause 2 -> evidence
       -> likely cause 3 -> evidence
```

Axes / Components explained:
Component 1: hypothesis, meaning a proposed explanation.
Component 2: evidence, meaning the data needed to confirm or reject it.
Component 3: decision path, meaning what action follows if the hypothesis is true.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: If a new AI feature is underperforming, the first hypothesis might be poor adoption, the second weak model quality, and the third bad onboarding. The team checks usage, error rates, and funnel drop-off instead of analyzing everything at once.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when speed matters and the likely causes are already visible.

### Root-Cause to Action Matrix
**Purpose:** Convert the answer into a fix, not just a diagnosis.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Root cause -> lever -> owner -> metric -> action
```

Axes / Components explained:
Component 1: root cause, meaning the real driver of the issue.
Component 2: lever, meaning the controllable factor that can change the result.
Component 3: owner, meaning the person or team accountable.
Component 4: metric, meaning the signal that shows whether the fix worked.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: If churn is caused by weak onboarding, the lever is activation flow, the owner is product, and the metric is 30-day retention. The structure prevents the team from stopping at “customers are unhappy.”
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when the discussion is getting stuck at analysis without action.

## 4. Formulas

Formula 1: `Branch coverage = covered branches / total relevant branches`
Variables:
covered branches = branches explicitly included in the structure
total relevant branches = branches that matter to the problem
Why this formula exists: It checks whether the problem tree is complete enough to trust.
How to interpret the output:
Low -> missing major causes -> restructure
Medium -> usable but incomplete -> review gaps
High -> strong coverage -> move to evidence
Worked example with numbers: If a profit problem has 5 relevant branches and the team covers 4, coverage is 80%.

Formula 2: `Hypothesis hit rate = confirmed hypotheses / tested hypotheses`
Variables:
confirmed hypotheses = tested ideas supported by evidence
tested hypotheses = all ideas actually checked
Why this formula exists: It shows whether the first guesses were useful.
How to interpret the output:
Low -> the team guessed poorly or structured badly
Medium -> some good calls, some dead ends
High -> strong prioritization and good problem framing
Worked example with numbers: If 3 of 5 hypotheses are confirmed, hit rate is 60%.

Formula 3: `Impact concentration = impact from top branch / total impact`
Variables:
impact from top branch = contribution from the largest cause
total impact = total problem size
Why this formula exists: It shows whether one branch explains most of the issue.
How to interpret the output:
Low -> the problem is spread across many causes
Medium -> a few causes matter
High -> one lever likely dominates
Worked example with numbers: If one branch explains 70% of the decline, the team should fix that branch first.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Start collecting data before defining the problem. | Write a one-sentence problem statement first. |
| Mix symptoms and root causes in the same branch. | Separate visible effects from underlying drivers. |
| Create a huge tree with no decision value. | Keep the structure simple and testable. |
| Put overlapping causes in different branches. | Use MECE logic to avoid double counting. |
| Stop at diagnosis and never translate to action. | Link every branch to a lever, owner, and metric. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Declining SaaS margin
Situation: A software company says profits are down, but leadership is unsure whether the issue is pricing, churn, or support cost.
Applicable framework/metric: MECE Breakdown Tree; branch coverage.
Analysis: A clean split shows revenue is flat, support cost is rising, and one enterprise segment is discounting too aggressively. The structure makes it obvious that the pricing branch is not the only one worth fixing.
Decision rule: If one branch explains most of the issue, solve it first. If several branches are similar in size, run parallel workstreams.
Action: Assign one owner per branch and review each branch with data, not opinions.

### Scenario 2: New product underperformance
Situation: A product team launched a new AI workflow tool, but adoption is below target.
Applicable framework/metric: Hypothesis-First Tree; hypothesis hit rate.
Analysis: The team tests three likely causes: weak awareness, poor onboarding, and feature gaps. The data shows awareness is fine, onboarding is the real bottleneck, and the model accuracy is acceptable.
Decision rule: If one hypothesis explains the majority of the drop, prioritize that fix before redesigning the whole product.
Action: Shorten setup steps, add guided tours, and track activation conversion.

### Scenario 3: Consulting turnaround for a regional chain
Situation: A regional retail chain wants to understand why same-store sales are falling even though traffic is stable.
Applicable framework/metric: Root-Cause to Action Matrix; impact concentration.
Analysis: The root cause is not traffic but lower average basket size in two high-traffic locations. The fix is localized, not enterprise-wide.
Decision rule: If the top branch contributes most of the decline, target that branch with a focused intervention.
Action: Pilot pricing and merchandising changes in the two weak stores before rolling out system-wide.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Write a single, clear problem statement before any analysis starts.
2. Split the problem into MECE branches.
3. Decide whether a branch tree or a hypothesis tree is faster for the case.
4. Attach metrics to each branch so the structure can be tested.
5. Assign owners to each branch or workstream.
6. Convert the final answer into a lever, a decision, and a next action.
7. Review the structure again after the first evidence pass and prune dead branches.
