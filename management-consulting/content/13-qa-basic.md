# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and concepts covering management consulting fundamentals, analytical frameworks, and consulting skills (Q1 – Q70).

## Q1. What is management consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Management consulting is a professional service where external advisors help organizations solve business problems, improve performance, and make better strategic decisions. Consultants bring specialized expertise, structured analytical methods, and an outside perspective that internal teams often lack.

**Real-life applications:**
- A struggling retailer hires consultants to identify why sales are falling
- A government agency uses consultants to redesign citizen service processes
- A startup engages consultants to build a go-to-market strategy
- A hospital brings in consultants to reduce patient wait times

**Key concepts:** `consulting`, `advisory services`, `external expertise`, `business problem-solving`, `strategic decisions`

---

## Q2. What is the primary role of a management consultant?

**Level:** Basic

The primary role is to diagnose business problems, analyze data and processes, and recommend actionable solutions to client organizations. Consultants also help implement changes, transfer knowledge to client teams, and sometimes serve as interim leaders during transitions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Diagnosing why a product launch failed and recommending course corrections
- Analyzing a company's cost structure to find savings opportunities
- Helping a firm enter a new market by building a market entry strategy
- Training an internal team to use new analytical tools after the engagement ends

**Key concepts:** `diagnosis`, `recommendation`, `implementation`, `knowledge transfer`, `business advisor`

---

## Q3. What is a consulting engagement?

**Level:** Basic

A consulting engagement is a defined project between a consulting firm and a client, with agreed-upon scope, timeline, deliverables, and fees. It typically begins with a proposal or statement of work and ends with the delivery of findings and recommendations.

**Real-life applications:**
- A six-month engagement to redesign a company's supply chain
- A four-week diagnostic to assess merger feasibility
- A 12-month transformation program with multiple workstreams
- A two-week rapid assessment of cybersecurity vulnerabilities

**Key concepts:** `engagement`, `scope`, `deliverables`, `statement of work`, `project timeline`

---

## Q4. What is a deliverable in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

A deliverable is any tangible output produced by the consulting team as part of the engagement. Common deliverables include strategy reports, financial models, implementation roadmaps, presentation decks, and process redesign documents.

**Real-life applications:**
- A 50-page market analysis report delivered to the CEO
- A financial model projecting five-year revenue under different scenarios
- A process map showing the redesigned customer onboarding workflow
- A slide deck summarizing recommendations for the board of directors

**Key concepts:** `deliverable`, `output`, `report`, `financial model`, `implementation roadmap`

---

## Q5. What is a subject matter expert (SME) in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

A subject matter expert is a consultant with deep knowledge in a specific field such as healthcare, digital technology, finance, or supply chain. SMEs are brought onto engagements when the project requires specialized expertise that generalist consultants do not have.

**Real-life applications:**
- A healthcare SME advising on hospital workflow optimization
- A cybersecurity SME assessing a bank's data protection systems
- A tax SME guiding a multinational through cross-border tax compliance
- A logistics SME redesigning a company's warehouse operations

**Key concepts:** `subject matter expert`, `specialization`, `domain knowledge`, `expertise`, `consulting team`

---

## Q6. What are the main phases of a consulting project lifecycle?

**Level:** Basic

The main phases are: proposal and scoping, discovery and data gathering, analysis, recommendation development, presentation, and implementation support. Each phase builds on the previous one, and skipping phases typically leads to weaker outcomes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Scoping meetings to define what the project will and will not cover
- Interviewing 30 employees during the discovery phase
- Analyzing sales data to find patterns during the analysis phase
- Presenting a prioritized action plan to the executive team

**Key concepts:** `project lifecycle`, `scoping`, `discovery`, `analysis`, `implementation`

---

## Q7. What is the scoping phase of a consulting project?

**Level:** Basic

Scoping is the initial phase where the consulting team and the client agree on the problem to be solved, the boundaries of the engagement, the timeline, the budget, and what success looks like. A clear scope prevents misunderstandings and cost overruns later.

**Real-life applications:**
- Defining that the project covers North American operations only, not global
- Setting a 10-week timeline with three milestone check-ins
- Agreeing that the deliverable is a strategy report, not full implementation
- Clarifying which departments will be included in the review

**Key concepts:** `scoping`, `project boundaries`, `timeline`, `budget`, `success criteria`

---

## Q8. What is the discovery phase in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

The discovery phase is when consultants gather information about the client's current situation through interviews, surveys, data requests, site visits, and document reviews. The goal is to understand the problem deeply before jumping to solutions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Conducting 25 stakeholder interviews across different departments
- Reviewing three years of financial statements and operational reports
- Visiting factory floors to observe production processes firsthand
- Distributing an employee survey to measure engagement and pain points

**Key concepts:** `discovery`, `data gathering`, `interviews`, `document review`, `current state analysis`

---

## Q9. What is a steering committee in a consulting engagement?

**Level:** Basic

A steering committee is a group of senior client executives who oversee the consulting project. They review progress at regular intervals, approve major decisions, resolve escalated issues, and ensure the project stays aligned with the organization's strategic priorities.

**Real-life applications:**
- Monthly steering committee meetings to review project milestones
- The committee deciding to expand the project scope after initial findings
- A senior VP using the committee to resolve a resource conflict between departments
- The committee approving the final recommendations before implementation begins

**Key concepts:** `steering committee`, `governance`, `oversight`, `decision-making`, `escalation`

---

## Q10. What is problem structuring in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Problem structuring is the process of breaking a large, complex business question into smaller, more manageable sub-questions that can be individually analyzed. It uses logical frameworks to ensure all aspects of the problem are covered without overlap.

**Real-life applications:**
- Splitting "why are profits declining?" into revenue issues and cost issues
- Breaking a customer churn problem into voluntary and involuntary categories
- Structuring a market entry question by geography, product, and channel
- Decomposing an efficiency problem by process step to find bottlenecks

**Key concepts:** `problem structuring`, `decomposition`, `issue tree`, `logical framework`, `sub-questions`

---

## Q11. What does MECE mean?

**Level:** Basic

MECE stands for Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive. It is a principle used to divide a problem into categories that do not overlap (mutually exclusive) and that together cover every possibility (collectively exhaustive). It ensures clean, gap-free analysis.

**Real-life applications:**
- Dividing customers into new, returning, and lapsed categories for analysis
- Splitting costs into fixed and variable to ensure nothing is double-counted
- Categorizing revenue streams by product line with no overlap
- Breaking a market into geographic regions that together cover the entire territory

**Key concepts:** `MECE`, `mutually exclusive`, `collectively exhaustive`, `logical structure`, `clean analysis`

---

## Q12. What is an issue tree?

**Level:** Basic

An issue tree is a visual diagram that breaks a broad business question into progressively narrower sub-questions. Each level branches into MECE categories, helping the consulting team systematically work through every dimension of the problem.

**Real-life applications:**
- An issue tree for "How can we grow revenue?" branching into pricing, volume, and new markets
- Breaking "Why is employee turnover high?" into compensation, culture, management, and career growth branches
- Using an issue tree to organize the analysis plan for a due diligence engagement
- Presenting the issue tree to the client to align on the scope of analysis

**Key concepts:** `issue tree`, `breakdown`, `sub-questions`, `structured analysis`, `MECE`

---

## Q13. What is a hypothesis tree?

**Level:** Basic

A hypothesis tree starts with a proposed answer to the business question and maps out the evidence needed to prove or disprove it. Unlike an issue tree which explores broadly, a hypothesis tree focuses the team's effort on testing a specific belief, making analysis faster and more targeted.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hypothesizing that revenue decline is driven by price, then listing data needed to confirm or reject
- Testing the hypothesis that customer churn is caused by poor onboarding
- Starting with "We should enter the Indian market" and listing conditions that must be true
- Using a hypothesis tree to prioritize which analyses to run first in a tight timeline

**Key concepts:** `hypothesis tree`, `hypothesis-driven`, `evidence`, `testing assumptions`, `focused analysis`

---

## Q14. What is root cause analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Root cause analysis is a method of identifying the fundamental reason a problem exists, rather than just treating its symptoms. Techniques like the "5 Whys" and fishbone diagrams help consultants dig beneath the surface to find what is actually driving the issue.

**Real-life applications:**
- Asking "why?" five times to discover that late deliveries are caused by a single bottleneck in the packing department
- Using a fishbone diagram to map all possible causes of product defects
- Discovering that high customer complaints stem from unclear billing, not product quality
- Tracing a recurring IT outage back to an undersized server that was supposed to be temporary

**Key concepts:** `root cause`, `5 Whys`, `fishbone diagram`, `symptom vs cause`, `diagnostic analysis`

---

## Q15. What is client relationship management in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Client relationship management is the practice of building trust, maintaining open communication, and managing expectations between the consulting team and the client organization. Good relationships lead to smoother projects, better cooperation, and repeat business.

**Real-life applications:**
- Weekly status calls to keep the client informed of progress and risks
- Sharing draft findings before the formal presentation to avoid surprises
- Scheduling a private check-in with a disengaged stakeholder
- Sending a post-engagement follow-up to ensure recommendations are on track

**Key concepts:** `client relationship`, `trust`, `communication`, `expectations`, `stakeholder engagement`

---

## Q16. What is a project sponsor in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

A project sponsor is the senior client executive who champions the consulting engagement within the organization. They provide strategic direction, secure funding and resources, remove organizational roadblocks, and serve as the ultimate decision-maker for the project.

**Real-life applications:**
- The CFO sponsoring a cost reduction engagement and allocating budget for it
- The sponsor intervening when a department head refuses to share data with the consultants
- The sponsor presenting the consulting team's recommendations to the board
- A sponsor checking in monthly to ensure the project stays aligned with corporate strategy

**Key concepts:** `sponsor`, `executive champion`, `authority`, `resource allocation`, `decision-maker`

---

## Q17. What is a quick win in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

A quick win is a small, visible improvement that the consulting team delivers early in the engagement to build credibility and momentum. Quick wins demonstrate value before the larger, more complex recommendations are ready, helping to maintain client confidence and stakeholder support.

**Real-life applications:**
- Identifying an immediate $100K saving from renegotiating a single vendor contract in week two
- Fixing a broken reporting dashboard that frustrated managers for months
- Streamlining one approval step that saves employees 30 minutes per day
- Resolving a customer complaint bottleneck that had been ignored for a year

**Key concepts:** `quick win`, `credibility`, `momentum`, `early value`, `stakeholder confidence`

---

## Q18. What is change resistance?

**Level:** Basic

Change resistance is the pushback that employees and stakeholders exhibit when facing organizational changes recommended by consultants. It can be active (open objections, complaints) or passive (ignoring new processes, slow adoption), and it is a natural human response to uncertainty and perceived loss of control.

**Real-life applications:**
- Employees refusing to use a new CRM system because they prefer the old one
- A department head quietly undermining a restructuring plan behind closed doors
- Staff expressing anxiety about job losses during a merger integration
- Teams reverting to old processes within weeks of a new system going live

**Key concepts:** `change resistance`, `pushback`, `adoption`, `organizational change`, `uncertainty`

---

## Q19. What is SWOT analysis?

**Level:** Basic

SWOT analysis is a framework that evaluates an organization by mapping its internal Strengths and Weaknesses and its external Opportunities and Threats. It provides a structured snapshot of where the company stands and what it should focus on.

**Real-life applications:**
- A restaurant chain identifying its strong brand (strength) and limited digital ordering (weakness)
- Spotting a growing demand for organic food (opportunity) that aligns with the company's sourcing capabilities
- Recognizing a new competitor entering the market (threat)
- Using SWOT results to prioritize which strategic initiatives to fund next year

**Key concepts:** `SWOT`, `strengths`, `weaknesses`, `opportunities`, `threats`

---

## Q20. What is PEST analysis?

**Level:** Basic

PEST analysis scans the external macro-environment by examining Political, Economic, Social, and Technological factors that could affect an organization. It helps consultants and managers anticipate changes outside the company's control that may create risks or opportunities.

**Real-life applications:**
- Assessing how upcoming data privacy regulations (political) will affect a tech company
- Evaluating the impact of rising interest rates (economic) on a real estate developer
- Tracking shifting consumer preferences toward sustainability (social) for a food brand
- Monitoring AI advancements (technological) that could disrupt a customer service operation

**Key concepts:** `PEST`, `political`, `economic`, `social`, `technological`

---

## Q21. What are Porter's Five Forces?

**Level:** Basic

Porter's Five Forces is a framework for analyzing the competitive intensity and attractiveness of an industry. The five forces are: threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitute products, and competitive rivalry among existing firms.

**Real-life applications:**
- Analyzing whether a new coffee shop can survive in a market dominated by large chains (competitive rivalry)
- Assessing how much power a single raw material supplier has over a manufacturer
- Evaluating whether ride-sharing apps are a substitute threat to taxi companies
- Determining if high capital requirements prevent new competitors from entering the airline industry

**Key concepts:** `Porter's Five Forces`, `competitive rivalry`, `bargaining power`, `threat of entry`, `substitutes`

---

## Q22. What is the bargaining power of buyers?

**Level:** Basic

The bargaining power of buyers describes how much influence customers have over pricing and terms. When buyers are few, large, or have many alternatives, they can demand lower prices, better quality, or more favorable terms — squeezing the seller's margins.

**Real-life applications:**
- A supermarket chain negotiating steep discounts from food suppliers because of its purchasing volume
- Individual consumers having low bargaining power when buying from a monopoly utility provider
- A corporate client demanding customized service terms from a consulting firm because it represents 30% of the firm's revenue
- Online comparison tools increasing buyer power by making price differences transparent

**Key concepts:** `buyer power`, `negotiation`, `pricing pressure`, `alternatives`, `customer concentration`

---

## Q23. What is the threat of new entrants?

**Level:** Basic

The threat of new entrants measures how easy it is for new competitors to enter an industry. When barriers to entry are low (minimal capital, no regulations, easy technology access), new competitors can appear quickly and erode existing firms' profits.

**Real-life applications:**
- Low barriers in food delivery apps leading to dozens of new entrants each year
- High barriers in pharmaceutical manufacturing due to regulatory approval requirements
- A software startup entering the accounting market with a free cloud-based tool
- Brand loyalty acting as a barrier, making it hard for new cola brands to compete with Coca-Cola and Pepsi

**Key concepts:** `barriers to entry`, `new entrants`, `competition`, `capital requirements`, `regulatory barriers`

---

## Q24. What is the McKinsey 7S Framework?

**Level:** Basic

The McKinsey 7S Framework analyzes organizational effectiveness by examining seven interconnected elements: Strategy, Structure, Systems, Shared Values, Style, Staff, and Skills. It shows that changing one element without adjusting the others leads to misalignment and poor performance.

**Real-life applications:**
- Checking whether a new digital strategy aligns with current IT systems and staff skills
- Using 7S during a merger to identify cultural clashes between two organizations
- Diagnosing why a reorganization failed — the structure changed but management style did not
- Assessing a startup's readiness to scale by reviewing all seven elements

**Key concepts:** `McKinsey 7S`, `organizational alignment`, `strategy`, `structure`, `shared values`

---

## Q25. What are the hard elements in the 7S Framework?

**Level:** Basic

The hard elements are Strategy, Structure, and Systems. They are called "hard" because they are tangible, easier to define, and can be directly influenced by management decisions such as org chart changes, strategic plans, and IT system implementations.

**Real-life applications:**
- Redrawing the organizational structure to support a new business strategy
- Implementing a new ERP system to replace outdated manual processes
- Creating a formal strategic plan with measurable objectives and timelines
- Changing reporting lines to reduce decision-making bottlenecks

**Key concepts:** `hard elements`, `strategy`, `structure`, `systems`, `tangible changes`

---

## Q26. What are the soft elements in the 7S Framework?

**Level:** Basic

The soft elements are Shared Values, Style, Staff, and Skills. They are "soft" because they involve culture, behavior, and human capabilities — things that are harder to measure and slower to change, but often more critical to long-term success.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company's shared value of "customer first" guiding how employees handle complaints
- A CEO's leadership style shifting from command-and-control to collaborative after coaching
- Investing in staff development programs to close a skills gap in data analytics
- Recognizing that the wrong leadership style is undermining a new strategy

**Key concepts:** `soft elements`, `shared values`, `leadership style`, `staff`, `skills`

---

## Q27. What is benchmarking?

**Level:** Basic

Benchmarking is the practice of comparing your organization's performance metrics, processes, or practices against those of leading competitors or best-in-class companies. The goal is to identify performance gaps and set evidence-based improvement targets.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing your call center's average handle time against the industry median
- Benchmarking manufacturing defect rates against the top 10% of plants in the sector
- A hospital comparing readmission rates with peer institutions to identify improvement areas
- A software company benchmarking developer productivity against industry surveys

**Key concepts:** `benchmarking`, `performance gap`, `comparison`, `best-in-class`, `improvement targets`

---

## Q28. What is a performance gap?

**Level:** Basic

A performance gap is the measurable difference between your current level of performance and a benchmark target — whether that target comes from competitors, industry averages, or internal top performers. Identifying gaps is the first step toward focused improvement.

**Real-life applications:**
- Discovering your order fulfillment takes 5 days versus the competitor benchmark of 2 days
- Finding that your employee engagement score is 15 points below the industry average
- Noticing that one branch processes loans in half the time of another, creating an internal gap
- Quantifying a $2M annual cost difference between your procurement costs and the benchmark

**Key concepts:** `performance gap`, `benchmark target`, `measurement`, `improvement`, `gap analysis`

---

## Q29. What is internal benchmarking?

**Level:** Basic

Internal benchmarking compares performance across different units, branches, or departments within the same organization. It is the easiest form of benchmarking because the data is accessible and the operating context is similar, making comparisons more fair and actionable.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing sales per employee across 50 retail stores to identify top and bottom performers
- Benchmarking patient satisfaction scores across hospital departments
- Identifying that one factory has 30% fewer safety incidents and studying what it does differently
- Comparing customer complaint resolution times between regional offices

**Key concepts:** `internal benchmarking`, `cross-unit comparison`, `best practices`, `performance variation`, `internal data`

---

## Q30. What is competitive benchmarking?

**Level:** Basic

Competitive benchmarking measures your organization's performance against direct competitors in the same industry. It uses publicly available data, industry reports, and sometimes third-party research to see how you stack up on key metrics like cost, quality, speed, and customer satisfaction.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing your market share trend against the top three competitors over five years
- Using industry salary surveys to benchmark your compensation against competitors
- Analyzing competitors' product launch frequency to assess your innovation speed
- Reviewing customer reviews of competitors to identify their strengths and your relative weaknesses

**Key concepts:** `competitive benchmarking`, `competitors`, `industry comparison`, `market position`, `performance metrics`

---

## Q31. What is a best practice?

**Level:** Basic

A best practice is a method, technique, or process that has been proven through evidence and experience to produce superior results compared to alternatives. In consulting, identifying and transferring best practices is one of the most direct ways to improve client performance.

**Real-life applications:**
- A best practice for onboarding new employees that reduces time-to-productivity by 40%
- Adopting a proven inventory management technique from the automotive industry for retail
- Standardizing a successful sales script used by top-performing reps across all teams
- Implementing daily stand-up meetings as a best practice borrowed from agile software development

**Key concepts:** `best practice`, `proven method`, `superior results`, `standardization`, `knowledge transfer`

---

## Q32. What is knowledge transfer in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Knowledge transfer is the process of passing expertise, methods, tools, and insights from the consulting team to the client's internal team. The goal is to ensure the client can sustain and build on the improvements after the consultants leave.

**Real-life applications:**
- Training client analysts to maintain the financial model the consultants built
- Documenting the new process so that future employees can follow it without consultant help
- Running workshops to teach managers the analytical frameworks used during the engagement
- Creating a playbook that captures the methodology behind the consulting team's recommendations

**Key concepts:** `knowledge transfer`, `sustainability`, `training`, `documentation`, `capability building`

---

## Q33. What is a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP)?

**Level:** Basic

A Standard Operating Procedure is a documented, step-by-step set of instructions for performing a routine task consistently. SOPs reduce variability, ensure quality, and make it easy to train new staff. Consultants often create or improve SOPs as part of operational improvement engagements.

**Real-life applications:**
- An SOP for processing customer returns that ensures every step is followed identically across stores
- A documented procedure for monthly financial close that reduces errors and speeds up reporting
- An SOP for onboarding new hires that covers paperwork, IT setup, training schedule, and first-week check-ins
- A warehouse picking SOP that specifies the exact sequence of steps to minimize errors

**Key concepts:** `SOP`, `standard procedure`, `consistency`, `documentation`, `quality control`

---

## Q34. What is stakeholder management?

**Level:** Basic

Stakeholder management is the process of identifying everyone with an interest in or influence over a project, understanding their needs and concerns, and engaging them strategically throughout the engagement. It ensures that the people who matter most are informed, supportive, and aligned.

**Real-life applications:**
- Creating a stakeholder map at the start of every consulting engagement
- Adjusting communication frequency and detail level for different stakeholder groups
- Meeting privately with a skeptical department head to address concerns before they escalate
- Keeping the project sponsor updated so they can advocate for the project internally

**Key concepts:** `stakeholder management`, `influence`, `engagement`, `communication`, `alignment`

---

## Q35. What is a stakeholder map?

**Level:** Basic

A stakeholder map is a visual tool that identifies all relevant stakeholders and plots them based on their level of influence and interest in the project. It helps the consulting team decide who needs the most attention, who should be kept informed, and who might become a blocker if not managed.

**Real-life applications:**
- Plotting the CEO (high influence, high interest) as a key stakeholder requiring regular updates
- Identifying a mid-level IT manager (high influence, low interest) who controls system access needed for the project
- Discovering that frontline supervisors (low influence, high interest) are anxious and need reassurance
- Updating the stakeholder map monthly as new players emerge or attitudes shift

**Key concepts:** `stakeholder map`, `influence`, `interest`, `power-interest grid`, `engagement strategy`

---

## Q36. What is a power-interest grid?

**Level:** Basic

A power-interest grid is a 2x2 matrix that classifies stakeholders by their level of power (ability to influence outcomes) and their level of interest (how much they care about the project). It guides how much effort to invest in engaging each stakeholder group.

**Real-life applications:**
- High power, high interest stakeholders are managed closely with frequent, detailed updates
- High power, low interest stakeholders are kept satisfied with periodic briefings
- Low power, high interest stakeholders are kept informed through newsletters or town halls
- Low power, low interest stakeholders are monitored but not actively engaged

**Key concepts:** `power-interest grid`, `stakeholder classification`, `engagement priority`, `influence`, `interest`

---

## Q37. What is coalition building in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Coalition building is the practice of uniting supportive stakeholders into an aligned group that collectively advances the consulting project's goals. A coalition provides broader organizational backing, making it harder for individual resistors to block progress.

**Real-life applications:**
- Aligning the heads of sales, marketing, and operations behind a customer experience initiative
- Getting three board members to co-sponsor a transformation program
- Building a coalition of branch managers who pilot new processes and share success stories
- Uniting mid-level managers across departments who share frustrations with the current system

**Key concepts:** `coalition building`, `alignment`, `collective support`, `organizational backing`, `influence`

---

## Q38. What is the pyramid principle in consulting presentations?

**Level:** Basic

The pyramid principle is a communication structure where you start with the main conclusion or recommendation, then support it with grouped arguments, each backed by data. It ensures that even if the audience only hears the first 30 seconds, they get the key message.

**Real-life applications:**
- Opening a board presentation with "We recommend entering the Brazilian market" before explaining the supporting analysis
- Structuring a strategy memo with the recommendation first, followed by three reasons, each supported by evidence
- Training junior consultants to answer questions with the conclusion first, not a chronological narrative
- Using the pyramid structure in emails to busy executives who read only the first paragraph

**Key concepts:** `pyramid principle`, `top-down communication`, `conclusion first`, `structured argument`, `executive communication`

---

## Q39. What is an executive summary?

**Level:** Basic

An executive summary is a concise overview — typically one to two pages — placed at the beginning of a report or presentation. It captures the key findings, recommendations, and expected impact so that senior leaders can understand the essentials without reading the entire document.

**Real-life applications:**
- A one-page executive summary for a 60-page operational review
- The CEO reading only the executive summary before the board meeting and being fully prepared
- A summary slide at the start of a presentation deck that covers the three main recommendations
- An executive summary used by the sponsor to brief other senior leaders who were not in the presentation

**Key concepts:** `executive summary`, `concise overview`, `key findings`, `recommendations`, `senior leadership`

---

## Q40. What is storyboarding in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Storyboarding is the process of planning the flow and structure of a presentation by sketching out slide titles, key messages, and the logical sequence before creating the actual slides. It ensures the presentation tells a coherent story rather than being a random collection of data.

**Real-life applications:**
- A team sketching 15 slide titles on sticky notes and rearranging them until the flow makes sense
- The engagement manager reviewing the storyboard before the team spends time building slides
- Using a storyboard to identify missing analysis — if a slide title says "Market size" but no data exists yet, it becomes a task
- Sharing the storyboard with the client for early feedback on the presentation direction

**Key concepts:** `storyboarding`, `presentation planning`, `slide structure`, `logical flow`, `narrative`

---

## Q41. What is a ghost deck?

**Level:** Basic

A ghost deck is a skeleton presentation with placeholder slide titles, rough layouts, and structural notes but no final content. It is used early in the engagement to align the team and the client on what the final presentation will cover, before any detailed analysis is done.

**Real-life applications:**
- Creating a ghost deck in week one and sharing it with the steering committee for feedback
- Using placeholder text like "Chart: Market share trend 2020-2025" to show what data will go where
- The engagement manager reviewing the ghost deck to ensure the story logic is sound
- Revising the ghost deck after the client says they want more emphasis on competitive analysis

**Key concepts:** `ghost deck`, `skeleton presentation`, `placeholder`, `early alignment`, `presentation structure`

---

## Q42. What is report writing in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Report writing is the practice of documenting the consulting team's analysis, findings, and recommendations in a clear, structured, professional written format. The report becomes the lasting record of the engagement that clients reference during implementation.

**Real-life applications:**
- A 30-page strategy report with executive summary, findings, recommendations, and appendix
- A one-page implementation brief for the project manager who will execute the plan
- A technical appendix with detailed financial models and data tables for analysts to verify
- A quarterly progress report tracking implementation of previous consulting recommendations

**Key concepts:** `report writing`, `documentation`, `findings`, `recommendations`, `professional communication`

---

## Q43. What is an appendix in a consulting report?

**Level:** Basic

An appendix is the section at the end of a consulting report that contains detailed supporting material — data tables, methodology explanations, interview summaries, financial models — that is too granular for the main body but essential for readers who want to verify or dig deeper.

**Real-life applications:**
- Placing detailed survey results in the appendix while summarizing key stats in the main report
- Including the full financial model in the appendix so the CFO's team can audit the assumptions
- Adding interview transcripts to the appendix for reference without cluttering the narrative
- Storing detailed competitive profiles in the appendix while the main report shows only the comparison summary

**Key concepts:** `appendix`, `supporting material`, `detail`, `verification`, `supplementary data`

---

## Q44. What is change management?

**Level:** Basic

Change management is the structured approach to transitioning individuals, teams, and organizations from a current state to a desired future state. It focuses on the people side of change — communication, training, support, and reinforcement — to ensure new processes, systems, or structures are actually adopted.

**Real-life applications:**
- Preparing employees for a new software system with training sessions and help desks
- Communicating the reasons for a restructuring to reduce anxiety and rumors
- Identifying change champions in each department to model new behaviors
- Tracking adoption rates weekly after a process change goes live

**Key concepts:** `change management`, `transition`, `adoption`, `people side`, `organizational change`

---

## Q45. What is the ADKAR model?

**Level:** Basic

ADKAR is a change management model that outlines five stages individuals go through during change: Awareness (knowing the change is happening and why), Desire (wanting to participate), Knowledge (understanding how to change), Ability (demonstrating new skills), and Reinforcement (sustaining the change over time).

**Real-life applications:**
- Running awareness campaigns before a system migration so employees know what is coming
- Addressing desire by explaining how the change benefits employees personally, not just the company
- Providing hands-on training workshops to build knowledge and ability
- Recognizing and rewarding employees who adopt new behaviors to reinforce the change

**Key concepts:** `ADKAR`, `awareness`, `desire`, `knowledge`, `reinforcement`

---

## Q46. What is a change champion?

**Level:** Basic

A change champion is an employee — at any level of the organization — who actively supports, promotes, and models the new way of working. Change champions bridge the gap between leadership's vision and frontline reality, helping peers adopt changes and providing feedback to the project team.

**Real-life applications:**
- A branch manager volunteering to pilot a new customer service process and sharing results with other branches
- A senior engineer advocating for a new project management tool among skeptical colleagues
- A nurse demonstrating the new electronic health records system to peers during shifts
- Change champions reporting common concerns from frontline staff back to the consulting team

**Key concepts:** `change champion`, `advocacy`, `peer influence`, `adoption`, `frontline support`

## Q47. What is adoption rate in change management?

**Level:** Basic

Adoption rate is the percentage of affected employees who are actually using the new process, system, or behavior as intended. It is the key metric that distinguishes a successfully implemented change from one that was merely announced.

**Real-life applications:**
- Measuring that 85% of sales reps are logging activities in the new CRM two months after launch
- Tracking adoption by department to identify teams that need additional support
- Comparing adoption rates across regions to find best practices from high-adoption areas
- Setting a target of 90% adoption within 90 days as the definition of success for a system rollout

**Key concepts:** `adoption rate`, `usage measurement`, `implementation success`, `behavior change`, `tracking`

---

## Q48. What is resistance management?

**Level:** Basic

Resistance management is the systematic process of identifying, understanding, and addressing opposition to organizational changes. It treats resistance as a normal, expected part of change and uses targeted interventions — communication, involvement, training — to reduce it.

**Real-life applications:**
- Holding one-on-one meetings with vocal resistors to understand their specific concerns
- Involving skeptical managers in the design of the new process so they have ownership
- Addressing fears about job loss transparently rather than avoiding the topic
- Creating a FAQ document that responds directly to the most common objections

**Key concepts:** `resistance management`, `opposition`, `intervention`, `communication`, `involvement`

---

## Q49. What is a consulting proposal?

**Level:** Basic

A consulting proposal is the formal document a consulting firm submits to a potential client outlining the problem to be addressed, the proposed approach, the team, the timeline, the deliverables, and the fees. It is both a sales document and a project plan.

**Real-life applications:**
- A consulting firm submitting a 15-page proposal to win a government contract
- The proposal specifying that the team will include one partner, two managers, and four analysts
- Clearly defining three deliverables: a diagnostic report, a strategy recommendation, and an implementation roadmap
- The client comparing three proposals from different firms before selecting one

**Key concepts:** `proposal`, `approach`, `team composition`, `timeline`, `fees`

---

## Q50. What is a workstream in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

A workstream is a distinct track of work within a larger consulting engagement, typically run by a sub-team focused on a specific area. Large engagements often have multiple workstreams running in parallel, each with its own scope, timeline, and deliverables.

**Real-life applications:**
- A transformation program with separate workstreams for finance, operations, and IT
- Each workstream leader reporting progress at the weekly steering committee meeting
- The finance workstream analyzing cost reduction while the operations workstream redesigns processes
- Workstreams converging at the end to produce an integrated set of recommendations

**Key concepts:** `workstream`, `parallel work`, `sub-team`, `program structure`, `integrated delivery`

---

## Q51. What is due diligence in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Due diligence is a thorough investigation of a target company conducted before a major transaction such as a merger, acquisition, or investment. Consultants examine financials, operations, legal risks, market position, and management quality to help the buyer understand what they are getting.

**Real-life applications:**
- Reviewing three years of audited financials to verify revenue claims during an acquisition
- Interviewing key employees of the target company to assess management capability
- Identifying hidden liabilities such as pending lawsuits or environmental cleanup obligations
- Evaluating whether the target's customer contracts will survive the ownership change

**Key concepts:** `due diligence`, `investigation`, `acquisition`, `risk assessment`, `transaction support`

---

## Q52. What is a retainer in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

A retainer is an ongoing fee arrangement where a client pays a consulting firm a regular amount for continued access to advice and support, rather than paying per project. It provides the client with ready access to expertise and gives the consulting firm predictable revenue.

**Real-life applications:**
- A small business paying a strategy consultant $5,000 per month for ongoing advisory support
- A CEO having a standing monthly call with a retained advisor for board preparation
- A retainer arrangement allowing a company to call on consultants for ad-hoc questions without a new proposal each time
- Using a retainer to ensure the consulting firm is available for urgent requests during a critical period

**Key concepts:** `retainer`, `ongoing advisory`, `recurring fee`, `access`, `consulting arrangement`

---

## Q53. What is scope creep?

**Level:** Basic

Scope creep is the gradual expansion of a consulting project's scope beyond what was originally agreed, usually without corresponding adjustments to timeline, budget, or resources. It happens when new requests are added incrementally, and it is one of the most common causes of project overruns.

**Real-life applications:**
- The client asking the consulting team to "also look at" three additional departments not in the original scope
- A four-week engagement stretching to eight weeks because of unapproved additions
- The consulting team working weekends to meet the original deadline despite expanded requirements
- Preventing scope creep by logging every new request and formally approving changes through the steering committee

**Key concepts:** `scope creep`, `project boundaries`, `change control`, `budget overrun`, `timeline extension`

---

## Q54. What is a case interview?

**Level:** Basic

A case interview is a type of job interview used by consulting firms where the candidate is presented with a business problem and must work through it in real time. It tests structured thinking, analytical ability, business judgment, and communication skills.

**Real-life applications:**
- A candidate being asked to estimate how many gas stations there are in the United States
- Solving a profitability problem for a hypothetical airline during a 30-minute interview
- Demonstrating MECE structuring and hypothesis-driven thinking in real time
- The interviewer evaluating how the candidate handles ambiguity and communicates reasoning

**Key concepts:** `case interview`, `structured thinking`, `business judgment`, `analytical ability`, `consulting recruitment`

---

## Q55. What is the difference between strategy consulting and operations consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Strategy consulting focuses on high-level questions like which markets to enter, how to compete, and where to invest. Operations consulting focuses on improving how things are done — making processes faster, cheaper, and more reliable. Strategy answers "what to do" while operations answers "how to do it better."

**Real-life applications:**
- Strategy consultants advising a tech company on whether to acquire a competitor
- Operations consultants redesigning a warehouse layout to reduce picking time by 30%
- A strategy engagement determining that a bank should focus on small business lending
- An operations engagement streamlining the loan approval process to support that strategic focus

**Key concepts:** `strategy consulting`, `operations consulting`, `high-level decisions`, `process improvement`, `specialization`

---

## Q56. What is a consulting framework?

**Level:** Basic

A consulting framework is a structured model or template that consultants use to organize their thinking about a business problem. Frameworks like SWOT, Porter's Five Forces, and the 7S model provide a repeatable approach to analysis that ensures consistency and completeness.

**Real-life applications:**
- Using Porter's Five Forces to evaluate whether to enter a new industry
- Applying the 4P framework (Product, Price, Place, Promotion) to a marketing strategy question
- Using a profitability framework to split revenue and cost issues for analysis
- Teaching clients to use frameworks so they can perform similar analyses independently

**Key concepts:** `framework`, `structured thinking`, `analytical model`, `consistency`, `repeatability`

---

## Q57. What is data-driven consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Data-driven consulting relies on quantitative evidence — financial data, operational metrics, survey results, market research — to support findings and recommendations. It replaces opinion-based advice with fact-based analysis, making recommendations more credible and defensible.

**Real-life applications:**
- Analyzing two years of transaction data to identify which customer segments are most profitable
- Using employee survey data to pinpoint the departments with the lowest engagement scores
- Building a financial model that projects the ROI of a proposed investment
- Presenting benchmark data to show the client exactly where they lag behind competitors

**Key concepts:** `data-driven`, `quantitative analysis`, `evidence-based`, `credibility`, `financial modeling`

---

## Q58. What is a gap analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Gap analysis compares the current state of an organization, process, or capability against a desired future state to identify what needs to change. The "gap" becomes the basis for the consulting team's recommendations and the client's improvement plan.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing current IT capabilities against the requirements of a planned digital transformation
- Identifying that a company needs 50 more trained engineers to meet its growth target
- Finding a gap between current customer satisfaction scores and the target set by the board
- Mapping the skills gap in a marketing team that needs to shift from traditional to digital channels

**Key concepts:** `gap analysis`, `current state`, `desired state`, `improvement plan`, `capability assessment`

---

## Q59. What is a value proposition for a consulting firm?

**Level:** Basic

A consulting firm's value proposition is the clear statement of what it offers clients, how it solves their problems, and why it is better than competitors or internal alternatives. It answers the question every client asks: "Why should I hire you instead of doing this ourselves or hiring someone else?"

**Real-life applications:**
- A firm positioning itself as the leader in healthcare consulting with 200+ hospital engagements
- Highlighting a proprietary benchmarking database that no competitor can match
- Emphasizing speed to insight: "We deliver actionable findings in four weeks, not four months"
- A boutique firm differentiating on partner-level attention versus large firms where junior staff do most of the work

**Key concepts:** `value proposition`, `differentiation`, `client value`, `competitive advantage`, `positioning`

---

## Q60. What is ethical consulting practice?

**Level:** Basic

Ethical consulting practice means maintaining honesty, objectivity, and integrity throughout the engagement. It includes avoiding conflicts of interest, presenting findings truthfully even when they are uncomfortable, protecting confidential client information, and recommending only what genuinely benefits the client.

**Real-life applications:**
- Declining an engagement where the consulting firm has a financial interest in a recommended vendor
- Presenting data that contradicts the CEO's preferred strategy because the evidence is clear
- Protecting proprietary information shared by one client from being used in a competitor's engagement
- Refusing to extend an engagement unnecessarily just to increase billing

**Key concepts:** `ethics`, `objectivity`, `confidentiality`, `conflict of interest`, `integrity`

---

## Q61. What is a consulting fee structure?

**Level:** Basic

A consulting fee structure defines how the client pays for consulting services. Common models include hourly rates, daily rates, fixed project fees, retainers, and performance-based fees. The right structure depends on the engagement type, scope clarity, and client preferences.

**Real-life applications:**
- Charging a fixed fee of $150,000 for a defined strategy project with clear deliverables
- Billing $3,000 per day per consultant for an advisory engagement with uncertain scope
- A performance-based fee where the consultant earns a percentage of documented cost savings
- A monthly retainer of $10,000 for ongoing strategic advisory to a mid-size company

**Key concepts:** `fee structure`, `pricing`, `fixed fee`, `hourly rate`, `performance-based`

## Q62. What is the role of a consulting partner?

**Level:** Basic

A consulting partner is the most senior member of a consulting firm. Partners are responsible for winning new business, maintaining key client relationships, overseeing engagement quality, and guiding the strategic direction of the firm. They typically spend more time on business development and client management than on day-to-day analysis.

**Real-life applications:**
- A partner pitching a $2 million transformation engagement to a Fortune 500 company
- The partner joining the steering committee meeting to address senior-level client concerns
- A partner mentoring junior consultants and reviewing the quality of deliverables
- The partner stepping in to resolve a major disagreement between the consulting team and the client

**Key concepts:** `partner`, `business development`, `client relationship`, `engagement oversight`, `seniority`

## Q63. What is the role of a consulting analyst?

**Level:** Basic

A consulting analyst is typically an entry-level team member who performs the detailed data gathering, number crunching, research, and slide building that supports the engagement. Analysts are the backbone of the delivery team, turning raw data into the insights that managers and partners present to clients.

**Real-life applications:**
- An analyst building a financial model to project revenue under three different scenarios
- Researching competitor pricing and packaging it into a comparison slide
- Conducting 15 interviews and summarizing key themes for the engagement manager
- Cleaning and analyzing a dataset of 50,000 customer transactions to find patterns

**Key concepts:** `analyst`, `data analysis`, `research`, `modeling`, `entry-level consulting`

## Q64. What is a consulting engagement letter?

**Level:** Basic

An engagement letter is the formal agreement between a consulting firm and a client that specifies the scope of work, responsibilities, deliverables, timeline, fees, confidentiality terms, and termination conditions. It serves as the legal and operational foundation of the engagement.

**Real-life applications:**
- The engagement letter specifying that the consulting firm will deliver a final report within 12 weeks
- Including a confidentiality clause that prevents the firm from sharing client data with third parties
- Defining a termination clause allowing either party to end the engagement with 30 days' notice
- The client's legal team reviewing the engagement letter before signing

**Key concepts:** `engagement letter`, `scope of work`, `agreement`, `confidentiality`, `legal terms`

## Q65. What is a lessons learned review?

**Level:** Basic

A lessons learned review is a structured session held after a project or engagement concludes to identify what worked well, what did not, and what should be done differently next time. It captures institutional knowledge and prevents the same mistakes from being repeated.

**Real-life applications:**
- A consulting team reviewing why a deliverable was two weeks late and how to prevent it next time
- Documenting that early stakeholder engagement was the key success factor in a smooth implementation
- Identifying that a data request took too long because it was not specific enough
- Sharing lessons learned across the firm so other teams benefit from the experience

**Key concepts:** `lessons learned`, `post-project review`, `continuous improvement`, `knowledge capture`, `reflection`

## Q66. What is the difference between a consultant and a contractor?

**Level:** Basic

A consultant provides expert advice, analysis, and recommendations to help clients solve problems and make better decisions. A contractor performs specific, pre-defined tasks — often technical — on behalf of the client. Consultants advise on what to do and why; contractors execute specific assignments.

**Real-life applications:**
- A consultant recommending a new IT architecture; a contractor building it
- A consultant designing a marketing strategy; a contractor running the social media accounts
- A consultant diagnosing why a project is failing; a contractor filling a vacant project manager role
- Companies sometimes hiring consultants first to define what contractors should build

**Key concepts:** `consultant`, `contractor`, `advisory`, `execution`, `expertise`

## Q67. What is thought leadership in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Thought leadership is the practice of a consulting firm publishing original ideas, research, frameworks, or insights that establish it as an authority in a specific domain. It builds the firm's reputation, attracts clients, and differentiates it from competitors.

**Real-life applications:**
- McKinsey publishing quarterly reports on global economic trends
- A boutique consulting firm writing white papers on healthcare innovation
- A partner speaking at an industry conference about supply chain resilience
- Publishing a proprietary benchmark study that clients cite in their own board presentations

**Key concepts:** `thought leadership`, `published research`, `authority`, `reputation`, `differentiation`

## Q68. What is a request for proposal (RFP)?

**Level:** Basic

A request for proposal is a formal document issued by an organization inviting consulting firms to submit proposals for a defined project. The RFP specifies the problem, requirements, evaluation criteria, and timeline, allowing the organization to compare multiple firms before selecting one.

**Real-life applications:**
- A city government issuing an RFP for consulting services to redesign its public transit system
- A corporation sending an RFP to five consulting firms for a digital transformation engagement
- The RFP specifying that proposals must include team qualifications, approach, timeline, and pricing
- A selection committee scoring proposals against weighted criteria to choose the best firm

**Key concepts:** `RFP`, `proposal process`, `evaluation criteria`, `competitive bidding`, `selection`

## Q69. What is confidentiality in consulting?

**Level:** Basic

Confidentiality in consulting means protecting all sensitive information a client shares during the engagement — financial data, strategic plans, employee records, proprietary processes — from unauthorized disclosure. It is a fundamental ethical and legal obligation that underpins the trust between consultant and client.

**Real-life applications:**
- Using anonymized data when presenting case studies based on past client work
- Securing client documents on encrypted servers and limiting access to team members
- Refusing to share one client's competitive intelligence with another client in the same industry
- Including non-disclosure agreements in every engagement letter

**Key concepts:** `confidentiality`, `non-disclosure`, `data protection`, `trust`, `ethical obligation`

## Q70. What is a consulting firm's utilization rate?

**Level:** Basic

Utilization rate is the percentage of a consultant's available working hours that are spent on billable client work. It is one of the most important financial metrics for a consulting firm because it directly determines revenue generation and profitability.

**Real-life applications:**
- A firm targeting 75% utilization, meaning consultants spend three-quarters of their time on billable projects
- A consultant's utilization dropping to 50% between engagements, signaling a need for more business development
- Tracking utilization by team to balance workloads and prevent burnout in over-utilized groups
- A partner monitoring firm-wide utilization as an early warning sign of a revenue shortfall

**Key concepts:** `utilization rate`, `billable hours`, `profitability`, `capacity`, `revenue metric`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Consulting Delivery Lifecycle
**Purpose:** Show how a project moves from question to impact.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
scope -> discovery -> analysis -> recommendation -> presentation -> implementation
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: scoping, which defines the problem and boundaries.
Component 2: discovery, which collects evidence.
Component 3: analysis, which turns evidence into insight.
Component 4: implementation, which converts insight into change.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team engaging consultants to reduce churn must first scope the churn question, then gather customer and usage data, then analyze the drivers, then present a recommendation, and finally monitor adoption after the change.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is trying to skip straight from symptoms to solutions.

### Problem Structuring Stack
**Purpose:** Break messy questions into manageable analysis lanes.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
big question -> issue tree -> MECE buckets -> hypotheses -> evidence
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: issue tree, which creates the question map.
Component 2: MECE, which prevents overlap and gaps.
Component 3: hypotheses, which focus the work.
Component 4: evidence, which confirms the answer.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: "Should we launch an AI support tool?" becomes demand, cost, risk, and capability branches. Each branch gets a hypothesis and a data request.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the client says the problem is "everything."

### Adoption and Alignment Loop
**Purpose:** Connect stakeholder support, change leadership, and adoption.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
stakeholder map -> champions -> quick wins -> communication -> adoption -> reinforcement
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: stakeholder map, which identifies influence and resistance.
Component 2: champions, which spread credibility.
Component 3: quick wins, which create momentum.
Component 4: reinforcement, which keeps the change from fading.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A CRM rollout succeeds when the consulting team finds champions, lands a quick win by fixing the reporting dashboard, and then reinforces the new process with training and manager check-ins.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When implementation is stuck even though the analysis is done.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Scope Creep Index
Formula: `Scope Creep Index = actual effort / planned effort`
Variables:
actual effort = hours or cost spent
planned effort = original estimate
Why this formula exists: It shows whether the engagement is staying inside the contract.
How to interpret the output:
Value = 1.0 -> on plan
Value 1.1-1.2 -> tolerable drift
Value > 1.2 -> project control problem
Worked example with numbers: A 500-hour estimate that becomes 650 hours yields 1.3. The team needs a scope reset.

### Formula 2: Adoption Rate
Formula: `Adoption Rate = active users / target users`
Variables:
active users = people actually using the new method
target users = people expected to use it
Why this formula exists: It measures whether the recommendation changed behavior.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> communication or training gap
Value medium -> partial implementation
Value high -> the change is sticking
Worked example with numbers: 180 of 225 managers using the new performance process gives 80%. That is workable, but not complete.

### Formula 3: Performance Gap
Formula: `Performance Gap = benchmark target - current performance`
Variables:
benchmark target = external or internal target
current performance = actual measured result
Why this formula exists: It converts benchmarking into a size-of-opportunity number.
How to interpret the output:
Value small -> incremental improvement
Value medium -> meaningful process redesign
Value large -> major transformation needed
Worked example with numbers: If the target is 10 days and current performance is 18 days, the gap is 8 days. That justifies process redesign.

### Formula 4: Utilization Rate
Formula: `Utilization Rate = billable hours / total available hours`
Variables:
billable hours = time charged to client work
total available hours = all working hours
Why this formula exists: It measures consulting firm productivity.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> capacity is underused
Value medium -> healthy mix
Value high -> revenue strong but burnout risk
Worked example with numbers: 120 billable hours out of 160 available gives 75% utilization.

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Jump straight to the answer. | Structure the problem first. |
| Treat a framework as the end product. | Use the framework to reach a decision. |
| Ignore adoption after the deck is delivered. | Track implementation and reinforcement. |
| Let scope keep expanding. | Use scope and change control to keep work bounded. |
| Write for yourself instead of the executive reader. | Lead with the conclusion and make the story MECE. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Retail chain turnaround
Situation: A retailer hires consultants because sales are down and the board wants a quick diagnosis.
Applicable framework/metric: Problem Structuring Stack and Scope Creep Index.
Analysis: The team breaks the problem into traffic, conversion, basket size, and margin. That prevents the project from turning into a vague "fix everything" exercise.
Decision rule: If the issue tree is incomplete, do not move to recommendation. If scope creeps beyond 1.2x, reset the project charter.
Action: Create the issue tree, assign owners to each branch, and freeze the analysis scope.

### Scenario 2: CRM rollout
Situation: A consulting team helps a sales organization adopt a new CRM.
Applicable framework/metric: Adoption and Alignment Loop, Adoption Rate.
Analysis: The system is live, but only 60% of reps use it consistently. Champions in each region and a quick dashboard fix lift trust and usage.
Decision rule: If adoption is below 70%, prioritize enablement and quick wins. If above 85%, shift to reinforcement and analytics.
Action: Train managers, simplify reporting, and track weekly adoption by team.

### Scenario 3: Market-entry proposal
Situation: A client asks whether to enter a new geography.
Applicable framework/metric: Consulting Delivery Lifecycle, Performance Gap.
Analysis: The proposal must define scope, data needs, and a benchmark for success. The team compares the target market's margin potential against current capability gaps.
Decision rule: If capability gaps are large and the market is only slightly better than current, do not enter yet. If the gap is manageable and the market is attractive, proceed with a pilot.
Action: Build a pilot plan, set a go/no-go gate, and prepare the executive summary.
