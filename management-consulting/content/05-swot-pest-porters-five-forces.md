# SWOT, PEST, Porter's 5 Forces

## Overview

SWOT, PEST, and Porter's Five Forces are three of the most widely used analytical frameworks in consulting. SWOT maps a company's Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats. PEST scans the external environment across Political, Economic, Social, and Technological dimensions. Porter's Five Forces evaluates the competitive intensity of an industry by examining suppliers, buyers, substitutes, new entrants, and rivalry.

---

## Why It Matters

These frameworks give consultants a common language and structured lens for analyzing any business situation. Without them, strategy discussions drift into opinion-based arguments. With them, teams can systematically identify where the real risks and opportunities lie, prioritize actions, and communicate findings clearly to executives who are already familiar with the models.

## Key Principles

- SWOT is most useful when strengths and weaknesses are based on data, not wishful thinking
- PEST forces you to look beyond the company and industry to macro-level trends that shape the playing field
- Porter's Five Forces explains why some industries are highly profitable and others are not
- These frameworks are starting points for analysis, not final answers — always go deeper
- Combining frameworks (e.g., PEST to identify external factors, then SWOT to assess the company's position) gives richer insights

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **SWOT** | A framework dividing analysis into Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats |
| **PEST** | A macro-environment scan covering Political, Economic, Social, and Technological factors |
| **Bargaining Power** | The ability of buyers or suppliers to influence prices, quality, or terms in their favor |
| **Threat of Substitutes** | The risk that customers will switch to a different product or service that meets the same need |
| **Competitive Rivalry** | The intensity of competition among existing firms in an industry |

## Use Case

A consulting team advising a European airline uses PEST to assess post-pandemic travel regulations, fuel price trends, shifting consumer preferences toward sustainability, and new booking technology. They then apply Porter's Five Forces to evaluate how low-cost carriers, aircraft lessors, and alternative transport (trains) affect profitability.

## Scenario

> A mid-size beverage company wanted to enter the plant-based milk market. Consultants ran a SWOT analysis showing strong brand recognition (strength) but no plant-based manufacturing capability (weakness). PEST analysis revealed favorable social trends toward health-conscious eating and upcoming government subsidies for plant-based food production. Porter's model showed low supplier power but high rivalry from established brands. The recommendation: enter via acquisition of a small oat-milk producer rather than building from scratch, saving two years of setup time.

## Examples

- A SWOT analysis for a regional bank reveals that its biggest threat is not competing banks but fintech startups offering faster loan approvals
- A PEST scan for a construction firm highlights an upcoming change in building codes (political) that will require expensive retrofits, prompting the firm to specialize in compliance consulting

## 1. Topic Snapshot
SWOT, PEST, and Porter's Five Forces are the standard consulting lenses for understanding a company's position, the external environment, and industry economics.
For an IT, AI, Product, or Consulting leader, they are a decision filter: what can the company control, what must it respond to, and where is profit structurally easier or harder to win.
The real value is not filling a 2x2 box; it is using the frameworks to decide where to invest, enter, defend, or exit.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### SWOT Matrix
**Purpose:** Separate what the company controls from what it must react to.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
            Internal
        +---------------+
        | Strengths     | Weaknesses
External+---------------+----------------
        | Opportunities | Threats
        +---------------+
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Strengths, which are assets or capabilities the firm can lean on.
Component 2: Weaknesses, which are internal gaps that limit execution.
Component 3: Opportunities, which are external openings the firm can pursue.
Component 4: Threats, which are external risks that can hurt performance.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud software vendor may have a strong engineering team and weak enterprise sales coverage. That creates an opportunity to partner for distribution and a threat from incumbents with larger go-to-market reach.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team needs a fast, shared picture of internal capability versus external risk.

### PEST Scan
**Purpose:** Surface external forces before the strategy gets too narrow.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Political | Economic | Social | Technological
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Political, meaning regulation, public policy, and government action.
Component 2: Economic, meaning growth, inflation, rates, and purchasing power.
Component 3: Social, meaning demographics, values, and behavior shifts.
Component 4: Technological, meaning tools, platforms, and innovation shifts.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A digital health product may face privacy regulation, changing consumer trust, new reimbursement rules, and faster AI-enabled diagnostics. PEST shows why a good product can still fail if the environment turns against it.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When leadership is over-focusing on product features and under-weighting the outside world.

### Five Forces Industry Pressure Map
**Purpose:** Diagnose where industry profits leak away.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
          Threat of New Entrants
Supplier Power  Rivalry  Buyer Power
          Threat of Substitutes
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: supplier power, which compresses margins through input costs.
Component 2: buyer power, which compresses margins through discount pressure.
Component 3: rivalry, which drives price, marketing, and capacity wars.
Component 4: substitutes and entrants, which cap long-term profit.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: In AI tools for customer support, open-source models, hyperscalers, and platform bundling can all raise substitution pressure, while large enterprise buyers demand lower prices and custom integration.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team asks why an apparently growing market still produces weak margins.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: SWOT Opportunity Score
Formula: `Opportunity Score = impact x fit x urgency`
Variables:
impact = size of potential gain
fit = how well the idea matches current capabilities
urgency = how time-sensitive the window is
Why this formula exists: It helps rank ideas instead of treating every opportunity equally.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> park it
Value medium -> test cheaply
Value high -> prioritize now
Worked example with numbers: A market with impact 8, fit 6, and urgency 7 gives a score of 336. The company should explore it, but only if execution capacity exists.

### Formula 2: External Pressure Index
Formula: `External Pressure Index = average(PEST severity scores)`
Variables:
PEST severity scores = rating for political, economic, social, and technological pressure
Why this formula exists: It turns a narrative scan into a comparable risk readout.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> stable environment
Value medium -> watch and prepare
Value high -> strategy must change
Worked example with numbers: Scores of 3, 4, 2, and 5 yield 3.5. That means the team should actively monitor the environment and create response plans.

### Formula 3: Industry Attractiveness Score
Formula: `Industry Attractiveness Score = 5 - average(Five Forces intensity)`
Variables:
Five Forces intensity = rating for each force from low to high pressure
Why this formula exists: It converts pressure into a quick attractiveness check.
How to interpret the output:
Value near 5 -> attractive industry
Value near 3 -> mixed industry
Value near 1 -> structurally tough industry
Worked example with numbers: If force intensities average 4, attractiveness is 1. That signals a hard market where only top operators are likely to win.

### Formula 4: Strategic Fit Gap
Formula: `Strategic Fit Gap = external requirement score - internal capability score`
Variables:
external requirement score = what the market demands
internal capability score = what the company can currently deliver
Why this formula exists: It identifies where the strategy is too ambitious or too defensive.
How to interpret the output:
Value near 0 -> fit is good
Positive value -> capability investment needed
Negative value -> current strengths may exceed the need
Worked example with numbers: If the market requires 9 on enterprise sales sophistication and the company scores 6, the gap is 3. The firm needs commercial capability before scaling.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Use SWOT as a brainstorming dump. | Turn each quadrant into a ranked action list. |
| Confuse external trends with internal weaknesses. | Separate what the firm controls from what it only observes. |
| Stop at the framework instead of acting on it. | Use the scan to choose enter, invest, defend, or exit. |
| Treat Five Forces as static. | Reassess forces when technology or regulation changes. |
| Ignore whether the company has the capability to respond. | Check strategic fit before recommending expansion. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Plant-based dairy entry
Situation: A consumer brand wants to enter plant-based milk. The team likes the growth story but has limited manufacturing capability.
Applicable framework/metric: SWOT and Five Forces.
Analysis: PEST shows social demand is favorable, but Five Forces shows high rivalry and moderate buyer power. SWOT reveals the brand is strong but the operations base is weak.
Decision rule: If capability gaps are large and rivalry is high, acquire or partner instead of building from scratch. If capability is strong and the force score is moderate, build internally.
Action: Compare acquisition targets, estimate launch speed, and choose the lowest-risk route to market.

### Scenario 2: SaaS market expansion
Situation: A software company wants to expand into a regulated industry.
Applicable framework/metric: PEST and Strategic Fit Gap.
Analysis: Political and technological factors may be supportive, but compliance and sales cycle complexity create a capability gap.
Decision rule: If fit gap is positive and high, add compliance, sales, or implementation capability before launch. If fit gap is near zero, move ahead with a pilot.
Action: Run a market-entry checklist and test with one pilot customer segment.

### Scenario 3: Retail bank margin defense
Situation: A retail bank sees margins compress despite steady account growth.
Applicable framework/metric: Five Forces and External Pressure Index.
Analysis: Buyer power is rising, substitutes from fintech are increasing, and rivalry is intense. Even a good product may struggle if external pressure is high.
Decision rule: If external pressure is high, defend by improving differentiation and lowering friction. If pressure is moderate, invest selectively.
Action: Redesign pricing, strengthen digital onboarding, and track churn by segment.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Define the decision first: enter, invest, defend, or exit.
2. Run PEST to understand what the firm cannot control.
3. Use Five Forces to test whether the industry can support profit.
4. Apply SWOT to connect the environment to internal strengths and gaps.
5. Rank the findings by impact, not by how easy they are to talk about.
6. Convert the analysis into one recommended strategic move.
7. Revisit the framework when market or regulation changes.
