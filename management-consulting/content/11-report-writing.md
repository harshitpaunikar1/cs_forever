# Report Writing

## Overview

Report writing in consulting is the craft of documenting analysis, findings, and recommendations in a clear, structured, and professional written format. Consulting reports serve as the permanent record of the engagement — they outlive presentations and become the reference documents that clients use to guide implementation long after the consultants leave.

---

## Why It Matters

A well-written report turns weeks of analysis into a lasting asset for the client. It provides the evidence base for decisions, the implementation roadmap for project managers, and the justification for budget approvals. A poorly written report — vague, disorganized, or full of jargon — gets filed away and ignored, wasting the entire engagement.

## Key Principles

- Structure the report around decisions the reader needs to make, not around the analysis you performed
- Use plain language; if a term needs a definition, define it the first time it appears
- Every recommendation should be specific, actionable, and tied to a measurable outcome
- Use visuals (charts, tables, diagrams) to replace dense paragraphs wherever possible
- Include an executive summary at the front — many senior leaders will read only that section

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Executive Summary** | A concise section at the beginning of a report that captures the key findings and recommendations in one to two pages |
| **Findings** | The factual results of the analysis, presented without opinion or recommendation |
| **Recommendation** | A specific proposed action backed by the findings, including expected impact and implementation steps |
| **Appendix** | A section at the end of a report containing detailed data, methodology notes, or supporting analysis too granular for the main body |

## Use Case

A consulting firm delivers a 40-page report on digital transformation for a manufacturing company. The executive summary covers the top three recommendations in one page. The main body has four chapters — one per workstream — each with findings, recommendations, and a timeline. The appendix includes interview transcripts, survey data, and detailed financial models for readers who want to verify the numbers.

## Scenario

> A consulting team submitted a 90-page operational review to a utility company. Two months later, the client had not acted on any recommendation. The engagement partner discovered the report was too dense and lacked clear prioritization. The team rewrote the executive summary into a two-page action plan with three priorities ranked by impact and effort, and added a one-page implementation timeline. Within a week, the CEO approved funding for the top two initiatives.

## Examples

- A consultant replaces a two-page written description of market share trends with a single bar chart comparing the client to four competitors over five years, making the decline immediately visible
- The report includes a "next steps" table at the end of each chapter listing the specific action, the responsible person, the deadline, and the expected outcome

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Report writing in consulting is the discipline of turning analysis into a decision-ready document that a client can use after the team has left. For IT, AI, product, and consulting leaders, the quality test is simple: does the report change a decision, speed implementation, or reduce uncertainty?

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### 3.1 Decision-First Report Structure
**Purpose:** Organize the report around the choices the client must make, not around the chronology of the analysis.

```
Decision -> Evidence -> Options -> Recommendation -> Next Steps
```

**Components explained:**
Decision: the question the report must help answer.
Evidence: the facts that matter.
Options: the plausible paths forward.
Recommendation: the best path based on evidence.
Next Steps: who does what by when.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** a product team needs a report on whether to launch a new AI feature. The report should open with the launch decision, summarize evidence from user tests and support logs, compare rollout options, recommend a phased release, and end with an owner-specific action plan.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** when the report draft reads like an analysis log instead of a decision document.

### 3.2 Executive Summary Pyramid
**Purpose:** Put the conclusion first, then the supporting logic underneath.

```
Top: answer
Middle: reasons
Bottom: evidence
```

**Components explained:**
Answer: the headline recommendation.
Reasons: the 2-3 main arguments.
Evidence: the data and examples supporting those reasons.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** a consulting report on cloud cost reduction opens with "move 30% of workloads to reserved capacity," then explains why, then shows the workload mix, utilization patterns, and cost curve.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** when senior stakeholders need the answer before they need the appendix.

### 3.3 Recommendation Prioritization Matrix
**Purpose:** Rank recommendations by impact and effort so the client knows where to start.

```
High impact / Low effort -> Do first
High impact / High effort -> Plan carefully
Low impact / Low effort -> Quick wins
Low impact / High effort -> Avoid
```

**Components explained:**
Impact: the size of the business benefit.
Effort: the time, cost, and complexity required.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** a report for a SaaS company lists workflow automation as high impact/low effort, a full platform redesign as high impact/high effort, and cosmetic dashboard changes as low impact/low effort. The matrix turns a long list into a sequence.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** when every recommendation sounds important and nothing is prioritized.

### 3.4 Evidence Traceability Chain
**Purpose:** Show how raw data turns into a claim the client can trust.

```
Source data -> Analysis -> Finding -> Recommendation
```

**Components explained:**
Source data: interviews, metrics, documents, or benchmarks.
Analysis: the method used to interpret them.
Finding: the conclusion about what is happening.
Recommendation: the action implied by the finding.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** a report on AI adoption links survey data, usage telemetry, and team interviews to a finding that adoption is weak because workflows are fragmented, then recommends a product-owner model and a single intake flow.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** when the client asks, "Where did this conclusion come from?"

## 4. Formulas

1. **Executive Summary Density**
   Formula: `Density = Core decisions covered / Summary pages`
   Variables:
   `Core decisions covered` = the number of important decisions the summary addresses
   `Summary pages` = pages in the executive summary
   Why this formula exists: it checks whether the summary is concise and decision-rich.
   How to interpret the output:
   Low density → too thin to be useful
   High density → concise but decision-heavy
   Worked example with numbers: 3 decisions in 2 pages = 1.5 decisions per page.

2. **Recommendation Activation Rate**
   Formula: `Activation = Recommendations acted on / Recommendations delivered`
   Variables:
   `Recommendations acted on` = items the client begins to implement
   `Recommendations delivered` = total recommendations in the report
   Why this formula exists: it measures whether the report changes behavior.
   How to interpret the output:
   Low activation → report was read but not used
   High activation → report drove action
   Worked example with numbers: 4 acted on from 5 delivered = 80% activation.

3. **Evidence-to-Claim Ratio**
   Formula: `Ratio = Evidence items / Major claims`
   Variables:
   `Evidence items` = charts, quotes, metrics, or documents supporting the report
   `Major claims` = the core assertions in the report
   Why this formula exists: it checks whether claims are properly backed.
   How to interpret the output:
   Low ratio → under-supported conclusions
   Balanced ratio → credible and readable
   Worked example with numbers: 12 evidence items for 6 claims = 2:1 support.

4. **Readability Burden**
   Formula: `Burden = Average sentence length + jargon count`
   Variables:
   `Average sentence length` = average words per sentence
   `Jargon count` = unexplained technical or consulting terms
   Why this formula exists: it estimates how hard the report is to read.
   How to interpret the output:
   Higher burden → harder to use, especially for executives
   Lower burden → easier to skim and act on
   Worked example with numbers: 24-word average sentences plus 8 jargon terms = high burden.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Don't write the report in the same order you did the analysis. | Do structure it around the decision the client must make. |
| Don't bury the answer on page 37. | Do lead with the conclusion and supporting logic. |
| Don't overload the body with raw detail. | Do move detail to the appendix and keep the main story clean. |
| Don't leave recommendations vague. | Do specify owner, timing, expected impact, and dependencies. |
| Don't use charts as decoration. | Do use visuals to replace text where they make the logic clearer. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Digital Transformation Report
Situation: A consulting team delivers a 60-page digital transformation report to an industrial company. The client says the document is thorough but unclear about what to do first.
Applicable framework/metric: Recommendation Prioritization Matrix and Activation Rate.
Analysis: The team had evidence, but not a decision hierarchy. After rewriting the report into a 3-page executive summary, a priority table, and a 90-day roadmap, the client approves two quick wins and one major platform initiative.
Decision rule: If the report is comprehensive but inactive, rework the structure before adding more analysis.
Action: Recast the report around three decision points and attach an owner to each next step.

### Scenario 2: AI Product Board Memo
Situation: A product team needs board approval for an AI feature. The first draft is heavy on technical detail and light on business impact.
Applicable framework/metric: Executive Summary Density and Evidence-to-Claim Ratio.
Analysis: The revised memo opens with the launch recommendation, quantifies revenue and support impact, and moves model-level details to the appendix. Board approval comes in the same meeting because the action is now obvious.
Decision rule: If executives need to hunt for the answer, the report is not ready.
Action: Rewrite for decision velocity, not technical completeness.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Identify the one decision the report must support.
2. Draft the executive summary before writing the body.
3. Build a claim map that links each recommendation to evidence.
4. Prioritize recommendations by impact and effort.
5. Push supporting detail, methodology, and data tables into the appendix.
6. Run a readability check with someone outside the project team.
7. Add a one-page action plan with owners, timing, and expected outcome.
