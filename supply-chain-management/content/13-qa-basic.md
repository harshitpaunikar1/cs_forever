# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational questions covering core supply chain concepts.

---

## Q1. What is Supply Chain Management (SCM)?

**Level:** Basic

Supply Chain Management is the planning and control of how goods and services move from raw materials to the final customer. It coordinates activities like buying, producing, storing, transporting, and delivering so that the right product reaches the right place at the right time.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies use SCM to keep products available in stores and online. Hospitals rely on SCM to ensure medicines are in stock. Governments use SCM for disaster relief distribution. Even a small bakery manages supply chains when ordering flour and delivering bread.

**Key concepts:** `Supply chain` `SCM` `flow management` `coordination` `customer demand`

---

## Q2. What is a supply chain?

**Level:** Basic

A supply chain is the complete network of people, organizations, resources, and activities involved in creating and delivering a product or service. It starts with suppliers of raw materials and ends with customers who buy and use the product.

**Real-life applications:**

- A tea supply chain includes tea farmers, processors, packaging units, transporters, wholesalers, retailers, and consumers. Each link affects availability and price. Delays at any point can cause shortages. Strong supply chains keep everyday items affordable and accessible.

**Key concepts:** `Network` `suppliers` `manufacturers` `distributors` `retailers` `customers`

---

## Q3. Why is SCM important for businesses?

**Level:** Basic

SCM is important because it helps businesses reduce costs, improve delivery speed, and keep customers satisfied. When a supply chain runs smoothly, companies avoid running out of stock or storing too much inventory, which saves money and builds trust.

**Real-life applications:**

- Retailers avoid empty shelves by managing replenishment well. E-commerce firms use SCM to deliver fast and accurately. Manufacturers prevent production stoppages by ensuring materials arrive on time. Good SCM also reduces waste and improves profitability.

**Key concepts:** `Cost control` `service level` `availability` `efficiency` `customer satisfaction`

---

## Q4. What are the main stages of a supply chain?

**Level:** Basic

The main stages are sourcing raw materials, manufacturing or processing, warehousing, transportation, distribution, and retailing or delivery to customers. These stages work together to transform inputs into finished products and move them to where they are needed.

**Real-life applications:**

- For packaged snacks, ingredients are sourced, cooked in factories, stored in warehouses, transported to distributors, delivered to stores, and purchased by consumers. Schools use similar stages for textbooks. Public health programs use these stages for vaccines.

**Key concepts:** `Sourcing` `production` `warehousing` `logistics` `distribution` `retail`

---

## Q5. What is the difference between logistics and SCM?

**Level:** Basic

Logistics focuses mainly on transportation, warehousing, and delivery of goods, while SCM is broader and includes logistics plus sourcing, production planning, supplier coordination, and customer demand management. Logistics is a part of supply chain management.

**Real-life applications:**

- A courier company mainly handles logistics by moving parcels. A smartphone brand manages SCM by sourcing parts, planning production, and then using logistics partners for delivery. Governments also plan SCM for public supplies and use logistics to move them.

**Key concepts:** `Logistics` `SCM scope` `transportation` `warehousing` `coordination`

---

## Q6. Who are the key participants in a supply chain?

**Level:** Basic

Key participants typically include suppliers, manufacturers, warehouses, distributors, retailers, logistics providers, and customers. Each participant has a role in moving materials, information, and products through the chain.

**Real-life applications:**

- In pharmaceuticals, suppliers provide chemicals, manufacturers make medicines, distributors supply pharmacies, and patients are end customers. NGOs also act as supply chain participants when distributing aid. Coordination between participants prevents delays and shortages.

**Key concepts:** `Supplier` `manufacturer` `distributor` `retailer` `customer` `logistics provider`

## Q7. What is demand in SCM?

**Level:** Basic

Demand is the quantity of a product or service that customers want to buy within a specific time period. Understanding demand helps companies plan production and inventory so they can meet customer needs without excessive waste.

**Real-life applications:**

- During festivals, demand for sweets increases, so shops order more ingredients. Schools forecast demand for uniforms at the start of the year. Public transport services also adjust capacity based on demand patterns like rush hours.

**Key concepts:** `Demand` `customer needs` `planning` `variability` `market requirements`

---

## Q8. What is supply in SCM?

**Level:** Basic

Supply is the ability to provide products or materials to meet demand. It includes how much suppliers can deliver, how much factories can produce, and how quickly goods can be replenished when needed.

**Real-life applications:**

- If a dairy can supply only limited milk each day, stores must plan accordingly. In emergencies, governments must ensure supply of food and medicine. When supply is disrupted, prices can rise and availability can drop.

**Key concepts:** `Supply` `capacity` `replenishment` `availability` `constraints`

---

## Q9. What is a supplier in a supply chain?

**Level:** Basic

A supplier is a person or company that provides raw materials, components, or services needed to make a product. Suppliers can be local or global, and their reliability strongly affects production and delivery.

**Real-life applications:**

- A car maker depends on suppliers for tires, glass, and electronics. A restaurant relies on suppliers for vegetables and spices. If suppliers deliver late or poor-quality items, businesses lose sales and customers face shortages.

**Key concepts:** `Supplier` `inputs` `components` `reliability` `procurement`

---

## Q10. What is procurement?

**Level:** Basic

Procurement is the process of selecting suppliers and purchasing goods and services needed by an organization. It includes activities like negotiating prices, signing contracts, and ensuring timely delivery of the right quality items.

**Real-life applications:**

- A university procures computers for labs through vendor evaluation and purchase orders. Hospitals procure surgical supplies to ensure continuous patient care. Public procurement ensures fairness and proper use of taxpayer funds.

**Key concepts:** `Procurement` `sourcing` `supplier selection` `contracts` `purchasing`

---

## Q11. What is inventory?

**Level:** Basic

Inventory is the stock of goods and materials that a business keeps to support sales and production. It can include raw materials, work-in-process items, and finished products waiting to be sold or delivered.

**Real-life applications:**

- Grocery stores keep inventory of food items to serve daily customers. Factories keep raw material inventory to avoid production stoppages. Too much inventory increases storage costs, while too little causes lost sales.

**Key concepts:** `Inventory` `stock` `raw materials` `WIP` `finished goods`

---

## Q12. What is lead time?

**Level:** Basic

Lead time is the total time taken from placing an order to receiving the product. It includes processing time, production time, and transportation time. Shorter lead times generally improve customer satisfaction.

**Real-life applications:**

- If a store’s lead time from its supplier is 7 days, it must reorder early to avoid stockouts. Online shoppers also notice lead time as delivery time. In healthcare, long lead times can risk patient treatment delays.

**Key concepts:** `Lead time` `order cycle` `delivery time` `responsiveness` `replenishment`

---

## Q13. What is a stockout?

**Level:** Basic

A stockout happens when a product is unavailable because inventory has run out. Stockouts often lead to lost sales, customer dissatisfaction, and damage to a company’s reputation.

**Real-life applications:**

- A pharmacy facing a stockout of essential medicine can harm patient care. Retail stockouts make customers shift to competitors. In public services, stockouts of critical supplies can reduce trust in institutions.

**Key concepts:** `Stockout` `shortage` `lost sales` `service failure` `availability`

---

## Q14. What is a warehouse?

**Level:** Basic

A warehouse is a facility used to store goods before they are distributed to stores or customers. Warehouses help balance supply and demand by holding inventory closer to where it will be needed.

**Real-life applications:**

- E-commerce companies use warehouses to store products for fast delivery. Food warehouses support supermarkets with steady replenishment. Disaster relief agencies use warehouses to keep emergency supplies ready.

**Key concepts:** `Warehouse` `storage` `distribution center` `inventory holding` `fulfillment`

---

## Q15. What is transportation in SCM?

**Level:** Basic

Transportation refers to moving goods between supply chain locations, such as from suppliers to factories or warehouses to customers. It affects delivery speed, cost, and product condition.

**Real-life applications:**

- Fresh fruits need fast and careful transportation to avoid spoilage. Manufacturing plants depend on transportation for timely parts delivery. Cities rely on transportation networks to keep essential goods flowing daily.

**Key concepts:** `Transportation` `shipping` `delivery` `freight` `logistics`

---

## Q16. What is distribution in SCM?

**Level:** Basic

Distribution is the process of delivering finished products from warehouses or factories to retailers or customers. It focuses on ensuring products reach the right markets efficiently and reliably.

**Real-life applications:**

- A beverage company distributes bottles to thousands of retail shops using planned routes. Textbook distribution ensures schools receive books before the academic year. Poor distribution can create local shortages even when overall stock exists.

**Key concepts:** `Distribution` `last-mile` `delivery network` `channel` `replenishment`

## Q17. What is a customer service level?

**Level:** Basic

Customer service level is a measure of how well a supply chain meets customer expectations, such as having products available, delivering on time, and maintaining quality. Higher service levels usually require better planning and sometimes higher cost.

**Real-life applications:**

- Supermarkets aim for high service levels to keep shelves filled. Online stores compete using fast delivery promises. Hospitals must maintain high service levels for critical supplies, where failure can have serious consequences.

**Key concepts:** `Service level` `availability` `on-time delivery` `reliability` `performance`

## Q18. What is the role of information in SCM?

**Level:** Basic

Information helps supply chain partners make better decisions about ordering, production, and delivery. When information is accurate and timely, companies can reduce uncertainty and avoid overstocking or stockouts.

**Real-life applications:**

- Retail sales data helps manufacturers plan production. Tracking information helps customers see delivery status. Governments use information systems to monitor food grain stocks and plan distribution to different regions.

**Key concepts:** `Information flow` `visibility` `data accuracy` `coordination` `decision-making`

## Q19. What is supply chain visibility?

**Level:** Basic

Supply chain visibility means being able to see what is happening across the supply chain, such as inventory levels, order status, and shipment locations. Better visibility helps reduce delays and improve planning.

**Real-life applications:**

- A company with shipment tracking can inform customers of delays early. Retailers with visibility can move stock from low-demand stores to high-demand stores. Public agencies use visibility to monitor vaccine stocks across regions.

**Key concepts:** `Visibility` `tracking` `transparency` `monitoring` `real-time status`

## Q20. What is a bottleneck in a supply chain?

**Level:** Basic

A bottleneck is a point in the supply chain where capacity is limited, causing delays and slowing the overall flow. Bottlenecks reduce efficiency because the entire chain can only move as fast as the slowest step.

**Real-life applications:**

- If a factory has only one packaging machine, it may become a bottleneck even if production is fast. Port congestion can bottleneck international shipping. Identifying bottlenecks helps firms invest in improvements where they matter most.

**Key concepts:** `Bottleneck` `capacity constraint` `delay` `throughput` `flow`

---

## Q21. What is an order in SCM?

**Level:** Basic

An order is a request made to purchase or replenish a product, usually placed by a retailer, distributor, or customer. Orders trigger supply chain activities like picking, packing, shipping, and replenishment.

**Real-life applications:**

- A retailer orders shampoo from a distributor based on shelf levels. A hospital orders gloves when stocks fall. Online customers place orders that trigger warehouse picking and last-mile delivery.

**Key concepts:** `Order` `replenishment` `purchase request` `fulfillment` `demand signal`

---

## Q22. What is order fulfillment?

**Level:** Basic

Order fulfillment is the process of completing a customer order, including receiving the order, picking the item, packing it, shipping it, and delivering it. Good fulfillment ensures accuracy and timely delivery.

**Real-life applications:**

- E-commerce fulfillment centers pick and pack thousands of orders daily. Restaurants fulfill orders by preparing and delivering meals. Public services fulfill requests by distributing documents or supplies efficiently.

**Key concepts:** `Fulfillment` `picking` `packing` `shipping` `delivery accuracy`

---

## Q23. What is a distribution center (DC)?

**Level:** Basic

A distribution center is a type of warehouse designed mainly for receiving, sorting, and shipping goods quickly to stores or customers. Unlike storage-focused warehouses, DCs emphasize fast movement of products.

**Real-life applications:**

- Retail chains use DCs to supply multiple stores daily. E-commerce firms place DCs near cities to speed delivery. Emergency response systems use DCs to quickly send supplies during floods or earthquakes.

**Key concepts:** `Distribution center` `cross-docking` `fast replenishment` `hub` `outbound shipping`

## Q24. What is the difference between raw materials and finished goods?

**Level:** Basic

Raw materials are basic inputs used to make products, such as cotton, steel, or flour. Finished goods are completed products ready to be sold, such as clothing, cars, or packaged bread.

**Real-life applications:**

- A garment factory stores raw fabric before stitching and finished shirts after production. Food companies store raw ingredients and finished packaged snacks. Tracking both types helps avoid production delays and delivery problems.

## Q25. What is a supply chain disruption?

**Level:** Basic

A supply chain disruption is an unexpected event that interrupts the normal flow of goods, such as strikes, natural disasters, supplier failure, or transport delays. Disruptions often cause shortages and increased costs.

**Real-life applications:**

- Heavy rains can delay truck deliveries to stores. A factory shutdown can stop supplies to many retailers. Governments plan for disruptions in essential goods like fuel, medicines, and food to protect citizens.

**Key concepts:** `Disruption` `interruption` `risk` `delay` `contingency`

## Q26. What is reverse logistics?

**Level:** Basic

Reverse logistics is the process of moving goods back from customers to sellers, such as returns, repairs, recycling, or disposal. It helps recover value and handle products responsibly.

**Real-life applications:**

- E-commerce companies manage returns and refunds through reverse logistics. Electronics companies collect old devices for recycling. Bottle return systems also use reverse logistics to reduce waste.

**Key concepts:** `Reverse logistics` `returns` `recycling` `refurbishment` `waste management`

## Q27. What does “flow” mean in SCM?

**Level:** Basic

Flow in SCM refers to movement across the supply chain. The main flows are product flow (goods moving), information flow (data and signals), and money flow (payments). Good flow keeps the system stable and efficient.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Product flow brings groceries to stores. Information flow tells suppliers what sold yesterday. Money flow ensures suppliers get paid on time. Problems in any flow can disrupt the entire chain.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### End-to-End Flow Map
**Purpose:** Show the whole chain from need to delivery without losing the handoffs.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Need -> demand -> plan -> source -> make -> move -> sell -> feedback
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Need: customer requirement that starts the chain.
Plan: forecast, capacity, and replenishment choices.
Source: procurement and supplier selection.
Make: production or processing.
Move: logistics, warehousing, and transportation.
Sell/feedback: service level and sales data that close the loop.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS hardware team maps laptop spares from demand signal to warehouse receipt to support tickets so it can see where service breaks and where to add buffers. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When teams are debating symptoms but not the full chain.

### Service-Cost Trade-off Curve
**Purpose:** Decide how much inventory, capacity, and transport cost are worth paying for service.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Low cost -> low inventory / higher shortage risk
Balanced -> acceptable service / acceptable cost
High service -> more stock / more cost
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Cost: procurement, transport, storage, and holding cost.
Service: availability, delivery performance, and customer trust.
Trade-off point: the level where additional cost no longer creates enough service gain.
Buffer: safety stock or spare capacity that protects service.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI team holding spare GPUs or routers may accept higher holding cost because downtime would be more expensive than the buffer. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When finance pushes for lower cost but operations needs more availability.

### Bottleneck and Visibility Matrix
**Purpose:** Locate where the chain slows down and whether the team can see it early enough.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
High visibility + low bottleneck = smooth flow
High visibility + high bottleneck = known problem
Low visibility + high bottleneck = hidden crisis
Low visibility + low bottleneck = blind spot
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Visibility: real-time status, tracking, and transparency.
Bottleneck: capacity constraint that limits throughput.
Monitoring: the ability to detect delay risk before it becomes a stockout.
Correction: corrective action, routing changes, or capacity additions.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A retailer with live inventory visibility can move stock from one store to another before a bottleneck at a regional DC turns into lost sales. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team suspects a problem but cannot tell where it sits.

### Coordination Loop
**Purpose:** Keep suppliers, internal teams, and distribution partners aligned.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Sales data -> plan -> PO / order -> supplier -> transport -> receipt -> update
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Sales data: the demand signal that starts the loop.
Plan: production, replenishment, and delivery scheduling.
Order/PO: the formal request that triggers action.
Receipt/update: confirmation that closes the loop and refreshes the next decision.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting-led procurement team shares weekly sales data with vendors so order timing, shipment planning, and service levels stay aligned. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When partners are working in silos or using different numbers.

## 4. Formulas

The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are the standard control metrics that make the chapter operational. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: Inventory Turnover
Formula: `inventory_turnover = COGS / average_inventory`
Variables:
`COGS` = cost of goods sold
`average_inventory` = average inventory held over the period
Why this formula exists: It answers how quickly the chain turns stock into sales.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> inventory is sitting too long -> reduce stock or improve forecast
Value moderate -> inventory is moving at a healthy pace -> maintain
Value high -> inventory is moving quickly -> watch service level and replenishment
Worked example with numbers: If COGS is $12M and average inventory is $2M, turnover is 6x. Decision: the chain is reasonably efficient, but the team should still compare the number to category norms.

### Formula 2: Fill Rate
Formula: `fill rate = fulfilled demand / total demand`
Variables:
`fulfilled demand` = demand satisfied from available stock
`total demand` = all demand requests in the period
Why this formula exists: It answers how often the chain actually served the customer.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.95 -> service risk is visible -> fix availability and replenishment
Value 0.95-0.98 -> acceptable for many categories -> monitor key SKUs
Value > 0.98 -> strong service -> protect the policy that enabled it
Worked example with numbers: If 9,600 of 10,000 units of demand are fulfilled, fill rate is 96%. Decision: keep the policy, but review the SKUs causing the remaining shortage.

### Formula 3: Stockout Rate
Formula: `stockout_rate = stockout_events / demand_events`
Variables:
`stockout_events` = occasions when a customer wanted the item and it was unavailable
`demand_events` = total demand occasions
Why this formula exists: It answers how often the system fails to have the item when needed.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.02 -> low shortage pressure -> maintain current policy
Value 0.02-0.05 -> watch list -> improve visibility and replenishment timing
Value > 0.05 -> service failure -> raise buffer, speed transport, or fix bottlenecks
Worked example with numbers: If 30 of 800 demand events are stockouts, stockout rate is 3.75%. Decision: the item needs a service review, especially if it is critical or customer-facing.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat SCM as just transportation. | Manage the full chain from sourcing to customer service. |
| Ignore the difference between stock and inventory position. | Track on-hand, on-order, and backlog together. |
| Buy cheap items without checking total cost. | Compare procurement, transport, storage, and stockout cost. |
| React only after a stockout. | Use forecasting, safety stock, and visibility to act earlier. |
| Let each function optimize its own numbers in isolation. | Coordinate plans across suppliers, operations, logistics, and customers. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Retail Replenishment for Fast-Moving Staples
Situation: A grocery chain sells rice and flour steadily across hundreds of stores. The stores have decent sales data, but replenishment is late in a few regions and shelves empty before the next truck arrives. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: End-to-End Flow Map + Inventory Turnover + Fill Rate.
Analysis: Average inventory is $2M and COGS is $12M, so turnover is 6x. The fill rate is 96%, which is good but not perfect for high-visibility staples.
Decision rule: If fill rate falls below 95%, increase safety stock or improve delivery performance; if turnover is too low, reduce excess inventory; if both are strong, keep the current policy.
Action: Tighten replenishment timing in the weak regions, review the suppliers with the longest lead time, and move one more SKU into daily review.

### Scenario 2: AI Hardware and Remote Support Spares
Situation: A product team supporting a fleet of remote employees keeps chargers, routers, and headset spares in one regional warehouse. A small shortage causes outsized downtime because users cannot work without the equipment. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Service-Cost Trade-off Curve + Stockout Rate.
Analysis: The item has a 3.75% stockout rate, which is acceptable for a low-priority item but not for a critical work-enablement item. The holding cost is low relative to the downtime cost, so more buffer is justified.
Decision rule: If stockout cost is materially higher than holding cost, raise the buffer; if stockouts are under 2%, keep the policy; if the shortage is concentrated in one hub, fix the bottleneck instead of increasing inventory everywhere.
Action: Increase safety stock for critical spares, keep a leaner buffer for low-impact accessories, and monitor hub-level visibility daily.

### Scenario 3: Logistics Bottleneck in Fulfillment
Situation: An e-commerce team sees faster demand growth than its packaging line can handle. The warehouse looks full enough, but orders still ship late because one machine slows the entire flow. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Bottleneck and Visibility Matrix + Throughput.
Analysis: The packaging line processes 50 orders per hour while demand is 70 orders per hour, so the bottleneck is obvious. Even with good visibility, throughput cannot exceed the constrained step.
Decision rule: If throughput is below demand, add capacity or reroute work; if the bottleneck is hidden, improve visibility first; if the same bottleneck repeats, invest in permanent capacity.
Action: Add a second packing station, shift simple orders to another lane, and track queue build-up every hour until throughput catches up.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Map the full supply chain and identify who supplies, who transforms, who moves, and who serves the customer.
2. Pull the key operating data: demand, lead time, inventory, fill rate, stockouts, and delivery performance.
3. Classify each item or flow by importance so critical items get different treatment from routine ones.
4. Build a single dashboard that combines visibility, service, and cost rather than splitting them across teams.
5. Review bottlenecks, supplier reliability, and forecast error on a fixed cadence.
6. Align procurement, logistics, and customer teams around the same demand signal and the same KPIs.
7. Revisit the policy when the network, seasonality, or customer promise changes.
