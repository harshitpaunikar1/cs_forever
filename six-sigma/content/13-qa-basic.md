# Six Sigma — Basic Q&A (Q1 to Q70)

## Q1. What is Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

Six Sigma is a data-driven methodology for reducing defects and improving process quality. It originated at Motorola in the 1980s and was widely adopted by companies like General Electric. The goal is to reduce process variation so that defects occur at a rate of no more than 3.4 per million opportunities.

**Real-life applications:**
- Manufacturing companies reducing product defects on assembly lines
- Hospitals improving patient safety by standardizing clinical procedures
- Banks streamlining loan processing to reduce errors
- Airlines minimizing baggage handling mistakes

**Key concepts:** `Six Sigma`, `defects`, `variation`, `quality`, `process improvement`

---

## Q2. What does the term "sigma" represent in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

Sigma is the Greek letter used in statistics to represent standard deviation, which measures how spread out data points are from the mean. In Six Sigma, a higher sigma level means fewer defects. A process operating at six sigma has extremely low variation and produces only 3.4 defects per million opportunities.

**Real-life applications:**
- Measuring the consistency of product dimensions in manufacturing
- Evaluating the reliability of service delivery times
- Comparing quality levels across different production lines
- Setting performance benchmarks for supplier evaluation

**Key concepts:** `sigma`, `standard deviation`, `variation`, `defects per million opportunities`, `sigma level`

---

## Q3. What is a defect in Six Sigma terminology?

**Level:** Basic

A defect is any instance where a product or service fails to meet a customer requirement or specification. Defects are the primary target of Six Sigma projects because they represent failures in the process. The goal is to identify what causes defects and eliminate those causes systematically.

**Real-life applications:**
- A scratched screen on a new phone identified during quality inspection
- An incorrect charge on a customer's bank statement
- A restaurant order delivered with the wrong items
- A software application that crashes during a specific user action

**Key concepts:** `defect`, `customer requirement`, `specification`, `quality`, `defect identification`

---

## Q4. What does DPMO stand for and why is it important?

**Level:** Basic

DPMO stands for Defects Per Million Opportunities. It is the standard metric in Six Sigma for measuring process performance. DPMO normalizes defect rates so that processes with different numbers of opportunities can be compared on a common scale. A Six Sigma process has a DPMO of 3.4.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing defect rates between factories with different production volumes
- Benchmarking a company's quality against industry standards
- Tracking improvement progress over the life of a Six Sigma project
- Reporting quality metrics to executive leadership

**Key concepts:** `DPMO`, `defects per million opportunities`, `process performance`, `benchmarking`, `sigma level`

---

## Q5. What are the five phases of DMAIC?

**Level:** Basic

DMAIC stands for Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, and Control. These five phases form the core problem-solving framework in Six Sigma. Each phase has specific objectives and deliverables, and the phases are completed sequentially, with tollgate reviews between them.

**Real-life applications:**
- Structuring a project to reduce customer complaint resolution time
- Guiding a team through a systematic investigation of production waste
- Organizing a quality improvement initiative in a hospital
- Managing a process optimization effort in software delivery

**Key concepts:** `DMAIC`, `Define`, `Measure`, `Analyze`, `Improve`

---

## Q6. What is the purpose of the Define phase?

**Level:** Basic

The Define phase establishes the problem statement, project scope, goals, and team roles. It ensures that everyone involved understands what the project aims to achieve and what is included or excluded from the effort. Key outputs include a project charter and a SIPOC diagram.

**Real-life applications:**
- Writing a project charter for a defect reduction initiative
- Identifying customers and their requirements for a new service
- Setting measurable goals for reducing order processing time
- Creating a SIPOC map to visualize the high-level process

**Key concepts:** `Define phase`, `project charter`, `problem statement`, `scope`, `SIPOC`

---

## Q7. What is a project charter in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

A project charter is a formal document that defines the purpose, scope, objectives, timeline, and team members of a Six Sigma project. It serves as the contract between the project team and leadership, ensuring alignment on expectations. The charter is created during the Define phase and referenced throughout the project.

**Real-life applications:**
- Documenting the business case for reducing shipping errors
- Gaining executive approval for a quality improvement project
- Aligning cross-functional team members on project boundaries
- Providing a reference point during tollgate reviews

**Key concepts:** `project charter`, `scope`, `objectives`, `Define phase`, `stakeholder alignment`

---

## Q8. What is Voice of the Customer (VOC)?

**Level:** Basic

Voice of the Customer is the process of capturing what customers need, want, and expect from a product or service. VOC data is gathered through surveys, interviews, complaints, and other feedback channels. It is used in the Define phase to translate customer needs into measurable Critical to Quality characteristics.

**Real-life applications:**
- Surveying airline passengers about their top priorities for flight experience
- Analyzing customer support tickets to identify recurring pain points
- Conducting focus groups before launching a new product
- Reviewing online reviews to understand customer expectations

**Key concepts:** `Voice of the Customer`, `VOC`, `customer requirements`, `CTQ`, `feedback`

---

## Q9. What does CTQ stand for and what does it mean?

**Level:** Basic

CTQ stands for Critical to Quality. CTQs are the specific, measurable characteristics of a product or process that must meet customer expectations. They translate broad customer needs captured through VOC into concrete targets that the project team can measure and improve.

**Real-life applications:**
- Defining maximum acceptable wait time for a customer service call
- Setting dimensional tolerances for a manufactured component
- Establishing a target delivery window for an e-commerce order
- Specifying the maximum acceptable error rate for data entry

**Key concepts:** `CTQ`, `Critical to Quality`, `customer requirements`, `measurable characteristics`, `specifications`

---

## Q10. What is a SIPOC diagram?

**Level:** Basic

A SIPOC diagram is a high-level process map that identifies Suppliers, Inputs, Process steps, Outputs, and Customers. It provides a bird's-eye view of the process being studied and is typically created during the Define phase. SIPOC helps the team agree on the process boundaries and understand who is involved at each stage.

**Real-life applications:**
- Mapping the order-to-delivery process for a retail company
- Identifying all inputs and suppliers for a food manufacturing line
- Clarifying the scope of a billing process improvement project
- Communicating the high-level process to stakeholders who are not process experts

**Key concepts:** `SIPOC`, `process map`, `suppliers`, `inputs`, `outputs`

---

## Q11. What happens during the Measure phase?

**Level:** Basic

The Measure phase focuses on collecting data to establish a baseline of current process performance. The team identifies relevant metrics, validates the measurement system, creates a data collection plan, and gathers enough data to understand the current level of variation and defects. This baseline is essential for later comparison.

**Real-life applications:**
- Timing each step of a manufacturing process to find bottlenecks
- Recording error rates in an invoicing system over 30 days
- Measuring call handle times across all customer service agents
- Collecting patient throughput data in a hospital emergency department

**Key concepts:** `Measure phase`, `baseline`, `data collection`, `metrics`, `measurement system`

---

## Q12. What is a baseline in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

A baseline is the measured level of process performance before any improvements are made. It serves as the reference point against which all improvements are compared. Without a reliable baseline, it is impossible to prove that a project actually improved the process.

**Real-life applications:**
- Recording the current average cycle time before process redesign
- Documenting the current defect rate before implementing a new quality check
- Measuring customer satisfaction scores before a service improvement initiative
- Capturing the current DPMO to compare against the post-improvement target

**Key concepts:** `baseline`, `current performance`, `benchmark`, `before measurement`, `comparison`

---

## Q13. What is Measurement System Analysis (MSA)?

**Level:** Basic

Measurement System Analysis is a set of studies that evaluate whether a measurement system is accurate, precise, and consistent. Before collecting data, teams must ensure that the tools and methods they use to measure actually produce reliable results. A flawed measurement system can lead to wrong conclusions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing whether two inspectors grade the same product consistently
- Verifying that a scale measures weight accurately before a food processing study
- Checking whether a software timer records transaction times with sufficient precision
- Evaluating whether survey questions are interpreted the same way by different respondents

**Key concepts:** `MSA`, `Measurement System Analysis`, `accuracy`, `precision`, `reliability`

---

## Q14. What is a Gage R&R study?

**Level:** Basic

A Gage R&R study is a specific type of Measurement System Analysis that evaluates the repeatability and reproducibility of a measurement tool. Repeatability checks whether the same person gets the same result when measuring the same item multiple times. Reproducibility checks whether different people get the same result when measuring the same item.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing whether multiple quality inspectors measure part thickness consistently
- Evaluating whether a blood pressure monitor gives the same reading when used repeatedly
- Verifying that different lab technicians obtain the same chemical test results
- Checking whether automated sensors on different machines report the same values

**Key concepts:** `Gage R&R`, `repeatability`, `reproducibility`, `measurement variation`, `MSA`

---

## Q15. What is the Analyze phase used for?

**Level:** Basic

The Analyze phase is where the team examines the data collected during the Measure phase to identify the root causes of defects or process variation. The goal is to move beyond symptoms and prove, using data and statistical tools, which specific factors are driving the problem.

**Real-life applications:**
- Using a Pareto chart to find that 80% of shipping delays come from one warehouse
- Running a hypothesis test to determine if a machine setting affects product quality
- Analyzing call center data to find the most common reasons for customer escalations
- Identifying the time of day when manufacturing defects are most frequent

**Key concepts:** `Analyze phase`, `root cause`, `data analysis`, `Pareto chart`, `hypothesis testing`

---

## Q16. What is a Pareto chart?

**Level:** Basic

A Pareto chart is a bar chart that ranks categories of problems or causes in descending order of frequency. It is based on the Pareto principle, which states that roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes. This tool helps teams focus their efforts on the few factors that have the greatest impact.

**Real-life applications:**
- Ranking the top reasons for customer returns in an online store
- Identifying the most frequent types of defects on a production line
- Prioritizing the leading causes of employee injuries in a factory
- Determining which software bugs account for the most user complaints

**Key concepts:** `Pareto chart`, `80/20 rule`, `Pareto principle`, `prioritization`, `frequency analysis`

---

## Q17. What is the purpose of the Improve phase?

**Level:** Basic

The Improve phase is where the team develops, tests, and implements solutions that address the verified root causes from the Analyze phase. The team generates multiple solution ideas, evaluates them, runs pilot tests, and rolls out the best solution with a detailed implementation plan.

**Real-life applications:**
- Piloting a new assembly process at one factory before expanding to all sites
- Testing a revised call script with a small group of agents before full deployment
- Running a Design of Experiments to find optimal machine settings
- Implementing a new software workflow to reduce manual data entry errors

**Key concepts:** `Improve phase`, `pilot test`, `solution implementation`, `DOE`, `solution selection`

---

## Q18. What is a pilot test?

**Level:** Basic

A pilot test is a small-scale trial of a proposed solution conducted before full implementation. It allows the team to verify that the solution works as expected, identify any unintended consequences, and make adjustments before committing resources to a full rollout.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing a new checkout process at three stores before rolling it out chain-wide
- Running a revised manufacturing recipe on one production line for a week
- Trialing a new patient intake form at one hospital department
- Deploying a software update to a small group of beta users first

**Key concepts:** `pilot test`, `trial`, `validation`, `risk reduction`, `small-scale implementation`

---

## Q19. What is the Control phase and why is it important?

**Level:** Basic

The Control phase is the final step of DMAIC where the team ensures that improvements are maintained over time. It involves setting up monitoring systems, updating standard operating procedures, training employees, and creating response plans for when the process starts to drift. Without Control, improvements often fade away.

**Real-life applications:**
- Installing a real-time dashboard that tracks defect rates on a production line
- Updating the employee handbook with new process steps after an improvement
- Creating an escalation protocol for when customer wait times exceed the target
- Scheduling monthly audits to verify that the improved process stays on track

**Key concepts:** `Control phase`, `monitoring`, `sustainability`, `SOP`, `control chart`

---

## Q20. What is a control chart?

**Level:** Basic

A control chart is a graph that plots process data over time along with a center line and upper and lower control limits. It is used to monitor process stability and distinguish between normal variation and unusual signals. Control charts are essential tools in both the Measure and Control phases.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking daily production output to detect shifts in process performance
- Monitoring patient wait times in a hospital to spot unusual spikes
- Watching error rates in a data processing center for signs of system problems
- Checking monthly sales figures for patterns that indicate process changes

**Key concepts:** `control chart`, `control limits`, `center line`, `process monitoring`, `stability`

---

## Q21. What is a standard operating procedure (SOP)?

**Level:** Basic

A standard operating procedure is a written document that describes the step-by-step instructions for performing a task or process consistently. SOPs ensure that everyone follows the same method, reducing variation caused by individual differences. They are updated during the Control phase to reflect the improved process.

**Real-life applications:**
- Writing a step-by-step guide for assembling a product on a factory line
- Documenting the process for handling customer complaints in a call center
- Creating a checklist for medical device sterilization in a hospital
- Establishing a procedure for software deployment to production servers

**Key concepts:** `SOP`, `standard operating procedure`, `documentation`, `consistency`, `process standardization`

---

## Q22. What is variation in the context of Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

Variation is the natural or unnatural differences in process outputs. Every process has some level of variation, and the goal of Six Sigma is to reduce it to the point where outputs consistently meet customer specifications. Understanding and controlling variation is the central theme of Six Sigma.

**Real-life applications:**
- Differences in fill volume between bottles on a production line
- Variation in response times among customer service agents
- Fluctuations in the dimensions of machined parts
- Differences in patient recovery times across hospital departments

**Key concepts:** `variation`, `process output`, `consistency`, `standard deviation`, `Six Sigma goal`

---

## Q23. What is common cause variation?

**Level:** Basic

Common cause variation is the natural, inherent randomness present in every process. It is predictable and stable, arising from the combined effect of many small factors. Common cause variation cannot be eliminated entirely but can be reduced through fundamental process changes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Slight temperature fluctuations in a climate-controlled warehouse
- Minor differences in typing speed among trained data entry clerks
- Small variations in the weight of cookies produced by the same recipe
- Natural fluctuations in daily website traffic

**Key concepts:** `common cause variation`, `inherent variation`, `natural variation`, `predictable`, `stable process`

---

## Q24. What is special cause variation?

**Level:** Basic

Special cause variation is unusual, unexpected variation caused by specific, identifiable factors. It makes a process unstable and unpredictable. When special cause variation is detected, typically through a control chart, the team investigates and removes the cause to restore stability.

**Real-life applications:**
- A sudden spike in defects caused by a new batch of raw material
- An equipment malfunction that shifts output dimensions
- A software bug introduced by a recent code update
- A power outage that disrupts production for several hours

**Key concepts:** `special cause variation`, `assignable cause`, `unstable process`, `investigation`, `control chart signal`

---

## Q25. What is a process map?

**Level:** Basic

A process map is a visual diagram that shows the sequence of steps, decisions, and flows in a process from start to finish. It helps teams understand how work actually gets done, identify redundancies, and spot areas where problems may arise. Process maps range from high-level overviews to detailed step-by-step diagrams.

**Real-life applications:**
- Diagramming the steps from customer order to product delivery
- Mapping the patient journey from admission to discharge in a hospital
- Visualizing the software development lifecycle from requirements to deployment
- Charting the flow of a financial transaction from initiation to settlement

**Key concepts:** `process map`, `flowchart`, `workflow`, `process visualization`, `process steps`

---

## Q26. What is Lean Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

Lean Six Sigma combines the waste-elimination principles of Lean manufacturing with the defect-reduction methods of Six Sigma. Lean focuses on speed and efficiency by removing non-value-adding activities, while Six Sigma focuses on quality by reducing variation. Together they provide a comprehensive approach to process improvement.

**Real-life applications:**
- A hospital reducing wait times while also reducing medication errors
- A factory eliminating unnecessary material handling steps and tightening quality tolerances simultaneously
- A bank speeding up loan approvals while reducing documentation mistakes
- A warehouse improving picking speed and picking accuracy at the same time

**Key concepts:** `Lean Six Sigma`, `waste elimination`, `defect reduction`, `efficiency`, `quality`

---

## Q27. What are the eight types of waste in Lean?

**Level:** Basic

The eight types of waste in Lean are often remembered by the acronym DOWNTIME: Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, Non-utilized talent, Transportation, Inventory, Motion, and Extra processing. Each type represents an activity that consumes resources without adding value from the customer's perspective.

**Real-life applications:**
- Overproduction of parts that sit in inventory and become obsolete
- Unnecessary transportation of materials between buildings
- Employees waiting for approvals that could be automated
- Extra processing steps like redundant inspections that do not catch additional defects

**Key concepts:** `eight wastes`, `DOWNTIME`, `Lean`, `non-value-adding`, `waste elimination`

---

## Q28. What is value stream mapping?

**Level:** Basic

Value stream mapping is a Lean tool that creates a visual representation of every step in a process, distinguishing between value-adding and non-value-adding activities. It shows the flow of materials and information from supplier to customer, making waste visible so the team can target it for elimination.

**Real-life applications:**
- Mapping the end-to-end order fulfillment process to find delays
- Visualizing the flow of insurance claims from submission to payout
- Charting the production process to identify where work-in-progress inventory builds up
- Documenting the software release process to find unnecessary handoffs

**Key concepts:** `value stream mapping`, `value-adding`, `non-value-adding`, `process flow`, `waste identification`

---

## Q29. What is Kaizen?

**Level:** Basic

Kaizen is a Japanese philosophy meaning continuous improvement. In practice, it involves making small, incremental changes to processes on an ongoing basis, often through short, focused events called Kaizen events or blitzes. Everyone in the organization, from frontline workers to executives, is expected to participate in Kaizen.

**Real-life applications:**
- A factory team holding a week-long Kaizen event to reorganize a workstation
- Office employees suggesting daily improvements to their filing system
- A restaurant staff meeting weekly to discuss small efficiency gains
- A software team running regular retrospectives to improve development practices

**Key concepts:** `Kaizen`, `continuous improvement`, `incremental change`, `Kaizen event`, `employee involvement`

---

## Q30. What is the 5S methodology?

**Level:** Basic

5S is a workplace organization method from Lean consisting of five steps: Sort, Set in Order, Shine, Standardize, and Sustain. The goal is to create a clean, organized, and efficient workspace where tools and materials are easy to find and problems are immediately visible.

**Real-life applications:**
- Organizing a manufacturing workstation so tools are within arm's reach
- Clearing unnecessary files and supplies from office desks
- Labeling storage areas in a warehouse for quick material retrieval
- Establishing daily cleaning routines in a commercial kitchen

**Key concepts:** `5S`, `Sort`, `Set in Order`, `Shine`, `Standardize`

---

## Q31. What is a fishbone diagram?

**Level:** Basic

A fishbone diagram, also called an Ishikawa or cause-and-effect diagram, is a visual tool used to brainstorm and organize potential causes of a problem. The problem is placed at the head of the fish, and major categories of causes form the bones. Common categories include people, process, materials, equipment, environment, and methods.

**Real-life applications:**
- Investigating the causes of high employee turnover in a department
- Exploring why a product fails quality tests intermittently
- Identifying reasons for delays in a construction project
- Brainstorming causes of data entry errors in an accounting system

**Key concepts:** `fishbone diagram`, `Ishikawa diagram`, `cause and effect`, `brainstorming`, `root cause categories`

---

## Q32. What is the 5 Whys technique?

**Level:** Basic

The 5 Whys is a root cause analysis technique where the team repeatedly asks "why" a problem occurs until they reach the fundamental cause. While five is the typical number of iterations, the actual count may vary. The technique is simple yet powerful for drilling past symptoms to find actionable root causes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Asking why a machine broke down and tracing it back to a missed maintenance schedule
- Investigating why a customer complaint was not resolved and finding a training gap
- Determining why a shipment was late and uncovering a supplier communication issue
- Exploring why a website went down and discovering an unmonitored server capacity limit

**Key concepts:** `5 Whys`, `root cause analysis`, `iterative questioning`, `problem investigation`, `fundamental cause`

---

## Q33. What is FMEA?

**Level:** Basic

FMEA stands for Failure Mode and Effects Analysis. It is a systematic method for identifying potential failure modes in a product or process, assessing their severity, likelihood of occurrence, and detectability, and then prioritizing corrective actions. Each failure mode receives a Risk Priority Number to guide action.

**Real-life applications:**
- Evaluating potential failure modes in a new automotive braking system
- Assessing risks in a pharmaceutical packaging process before production starts
- Analyzing potential points of failure in a hospital medication administration process
- Reviewing a software deployment pipeline for possible failure scenarios

**Key concepts:** `FMEA`, `failure mode`, `Risk Priority Number`, `severity`, `occurrence`

---

## Q34. What is a Risk Priority Number (RPN)?

**Level:** Basic

A Risk Priority Number is a numerical score calculated by multiplying severity, occurrence, and detection ratings for each potential failure mode in an FMEA. Higher RPNs indicate higher risk and are prioritized for corrective action. RPN helps teams focus limited resources on the most critical failure modes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Ranking manufacturing defects to decide which to address first
- Prioritizing software bugs based on their impact, frequency, and ease of detection
- Deciding which hospital safety risks need immediate intervention
- Allocating engineering resources to the highest-risk design features

**Key concepts:** `RPN`, `Risk Priority Number`, `severity`, `occurrence`, `detection`

---

## Q35. What is the difference between a defect and a defective?

**Level:** Basic

A defect is a single instance of non-conformance within a unit, while a defective is an entire unit that contains one or more defects and is therefore unacceptable. A single product can have multiple defects but is counted as one defective. This distinction matters when calculating metrics like DPU versus fraction defective.

**Real-life applications:**
- A shirt with both a missing button and a loose thread has two defects but is one defective
- A printed circuit board with three solder defects is one defective unit
- A loan application with two errors in different fields is one defective application
- A restaurant meal with incorrect seasoning and wrong side dish has two defects but is one defective order

**Key concepts:** `defect`, `defective`, `unit`, `DPU`, `non-conformance`

---

## Q36. What is the sigma level of a process?

**Level:** Basic

The sigma level is a statistical measure that indicates how many standard deviations fit between the process mean and the nearest specification limit. A higher sigma level means fewer defects. A 1-sigma process produces about 690,000 DPMO, while a 6-sigma process produces only 3.4 DPMO.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company calculating that its current billing process operates at 3 sigma and targeting 5 sigma
- Benchmarking a factory's quality against the Six Sigma standard
- Reporting sigma levels to leadership as a summary of process performance
- Comparing the quality of different product lines within the same company

**Key concepts:** `sigma level`, `standard deviation`, `DPMO`, `process performance`, `specification limit`

---

## Q37. Who are the key roles in a Six Sigma project?

**Level:** Basic

The key roles include the Executive Sponsor who provides resources and removes barriers, the Champion who oversees the project and connects it to business strategy, the Black Belt who leads the project full-time, the Green Belt who supports the project part-time, and team members who contribute process knowledge and execute tasks.

**Real-life applications:**
- A vice president serving as Executive Sponsor for a company-wide quality initiative
- A department manager acting as Champion for a defect reduction project
- A trained Black Belt leading a cross-functional team through DMAIC
- Frontline workers participating as team members and providing process insights

**Key concepts:** `Executive Sponsor`, `Champion`, `Black Belt`, `Green Belt`, `team roles`

---

## Q38. What is a Green Belt in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

A Green Belt is a Six Sigma practitioner who has completed foundational training in DMAIC methodology and statistical tools. Green Belts typically lead smaller projects or serve as team members on Black Belt projects while maintaining their regular job responsibilities. They are the backbone of most Six Sigma programs.

**Real-life applications:**
- A production supervisor leading a project to reduce scrap rates in their area
- A nurse managing a project to improve patient discharge times on their floor
- An IT analyst supporting a Black Belt project to reduce system downtime
- A finance team member leading an effort to reduce invoice processing errors

**Key concepts:** `Green Belt`, `certification`, `DMAIC`, `part-time practitioner`, `project leadership`

---

## Q39. What is a Black Belt in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

A Black Belt is a Six Sigma expert who works full-time on improvement projects. They receive advanced training in statistical analysis, project management, and change management. Black Belts lead complex, high-impact projects and mentor Green Belts. Certification typically requires completing a defined number of projects with demonstrated financial impact.

**Real-life applications:**
- Leading a cross-departmental project to redesign a supply chain process
- Mentoring three Green Belts on their first Six Sigma projects
- Conducting advanced statistical analysis for a manufacturing quality project
- Presenting project results and financial savings to executive leadership

**Key concepts:** `Black Belt`, `full-time practitioner`, `advanced statistics`, `mentoring`, `certification`

---

## Q40. What is a tollgate review?

**Level:** Basic

A tollgate review is a formal checkpoint at the end of each DMAIC phase where the project team presents its work to stakeholders for evaluation. Reviewers assess whether the deliverables for that phase are complete and satisfactory before approving the team to proceed to the next phase. Tollgates maintain project rigor and prevent shortcuts.

**Real-life applications:**
- Presenting the project charter and SIPOC at the end of the Define phase
- Reviewing the data collection plan and baseline metrics after the Measure phase
- Demonstrating root cause verification to stakeholders before proceeding to Improve
- Confirming that control plans are in place before closing a project

**Key concepts:** `tollgate review`, `phase gate`, `stakeholder review`, `DMAIC checkpoint`, `project governance`

---

## Q41. What is the difference between Six Sigma and total quality management (TQM)?

**Level:** Basic

Six Sigma is a structured, project-based methodology that uses statistical tools to reduce defects, while Total Quality Management is a broader management philosophy focused on continuous improvement across the entire organization. Six Sigma is more data-driven and project-focused, while TQM emphasizes company-wide cultural commitment to quality.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company using TQM as its overarching quality philosophy and Six Sigma for specific improvement projects
- Choosing Six Sigma when a quantifiable reduction in defects is needed within a defined timeline
- Applying TQM principles to build a quality-first culture across all departments
- Using Six Sigma's statistical rigor to complement a TQM initiative

**Key concepts:** `Six Sigma`, `TQM`, `Total Quality Management`, `data-driven`, `continuous improvement`

---

## Q42. What is a process in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

A process is any series of steps or activities that transforms inputs into outputs to deliver value to a customer. In Six Sigma, processes are the primary unit of analysis. Every defect originates from a process, and improvement efforts focus on understanding and optimizing processes rather than blaming individuals.

**Real-life applications:**
- The steps from receiving a customer order to shipping the product
- The procedure for diagnosing and treating a patient in a clinic
- The workflow for reviewing, approving, and paying vendor invoices
- The sequence of operations for manufacturing a circuit board

**Key concepts:** `process`, `inputs`, `outputs`, `transformation`, `process focus`

---

## Q43. What does "voice of the process" mean?

**Level:** Basic

Voice of the Process refers to the actual performance data produced by a process, showing what the process is capable of delivering. It contrasts with Voice of the Customer, which represents what the customer expects. When the Voice of the Process does not meet the Voice of the Customer, there is a gap that Six Sigma projects aim to close.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing actual delivery times to the delivery window promised to customers
- Measuring actual product dimensions against engineering specifications
- Evaluating actual call center wait times versus customer expectations
- Comparing actual error rates to the target error rate set by management

**Key concepts:** `Voice of the Process`, `VOP`, `process performance`, `capability gap`, `Voice of the Customer`

---

## Q44. What is a data collection plan?

**Level:** Basic

A data collection plan is a document that specifies what data will be collected, how it will be collected, when and where collection will happen, who will collect it, and how much data is needed. It ensures that data gathering is systematic, consistent, and sufficient for meaningful analysis.

**Real-life applications:**
- Planning to record defect types and counts at three inspection stations every hour
- Scheduling customer satisfaction surveys to be sent after every service interaction
- Assigning specific team members to measure cycle times at defined intervals
- Documenting the sample size needed to detect a meaningful difference in quality

**Key concepts:** `data collection plan`, `sampling`, `data integrity`, `systematic collection`, `Measure phase`

---

## Q45. What is the difference between accuracy and precision?

**Level:** Basic

Accuracy refers to how close a measurement is to the true value, while precision refers to how close repeated measurements are to each other. A measurement system can be precise but inaccurate if it consistently gives the same wrong answer. Both accuracy and precision are needed for a reliable measurement system.

**Real-life applications:**
- A scale that consistently reads 5 grams too heavy is precise but not accurate
- A thermometer that gives wildly different readings each time is neither accurate nor precise
- A caliper that measures a part correctly and consistently is both accurate and precise
- A survey question that different people interpret the same way shows good precision

**Key concepts:** `accuracy`, `precision`, `measurement system`, `reliability`, `calibration`

---

## Q46. What is a histogram?

**Level:** Basic

A histogram is a bar chart that shows the distribution of a dataset by grouping data points into ranges called bins. It helps teams visualize the shape, center, and spread of process data. Histograms reveal patterns such as whether the data is normally distributed, skewed, or has multiple peaks.

**Real-life applications:**
- Plotting the distribution of customer wait times to see where most values cluster
- Visualizing the range of product weights to check for centering issues
- Displaying the distribution of exam scores to identify common performance levels
- Showing the spread of delivery times to understand process variation

**Key concepts:** `histogram`, `distribution`, `frequency`, `data visualization`, `bins`

---

## Q47. What is the normal distribution and why is it important in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

The normal distribution is a bell-shaped curve where data is symmetrically distributed around the mean. Many natural and industrial processes produce data that follows this pattern. Six Sigma relies heavily on the normal distribution because its statistical tools, including sigma level calculations and control chart limits, assume normally distributed data.

**Real-life applications:**
- Checking whether product dimensions follow a bell curve before calculating capability
- Using normal distribution properties to set control limits at three standard deviations
- Predicting the percentage of output that will fall outside specification limits
- Verifying the distribution assumption before running hypothesis tests

**Key concepts:** `normal distribution`, `bell curve`, `mean`, `standard deviation`, `symmetry`

---

## Q48. What is the mean of a dataset?

**Level:** Basic

The mean is the arithmetic average of a dataset, calculated by adding all values and dividing by the number of values. In Six Sigma, the mean represents the center of a process distribution and is a key metric for understanding where the process is targeting. Shifts in the mean can indicate a process change.

**Real-life applications:**
- Calculating the average fill volume of bottles on a production line
- Determining the mean response time of a customer support team
- Finding the average number of defects per shift in a factory
- Computing the mean processing time for insurance claims

**Key concepts:** `mean`, `average`, `central tendency`, `process center`, `data summary`

---

## Q49. What is the median?

**Level:** Basic

The median is the middle value when a dataset is arranged in order from smallest to largest. It is useful as a measure of central tendency when data is skewed or contains outliers, because it is not affected by extreme values the way the mean is.

**Real-life applications:**
- Using median income instead of mean income to represent typical household earnings
- Reporting the median wait time when a few very long waits would skew the average
- Finding the median cycle time to understand typical process performance
- Choosing the median when reporting home prices to avoid distortion from luxury sales

**Key concepts:** `median`, `central tendency`, `middle value`, `skewed data`, `outlier resistance`

---

## Q50. What is standard deviation?

**Level:** Basic

Standard deviation is a statistical measure of how spread out data points are from the mean. A low standard deviation means data points are clustered closely around the average, while a high standard deviation means they are widely dispersed. In Six Sigma, reducing standard deviation is equivalent to reducing variation and improving quality.

**Real-life applications:**
- Measuring how consistently a machine produces parts of the same length
- Calculating the spread of transaction processing times in a banking system
- Evaluating the consistency of test scores across a group of students
- Assessing the variability of patient recovery times after a standard procedure

**Key concepts:** `standard deviation`, `spread`, `dispersion`, `variation`, `sigma`

---

## Q51. What is a specification limit?

**Level:** Basic

A specification limit is a boundary set by the customer or engineer that defines what is acceptable for a product or process characteristic. There is typically an upper specification limit and a lower specification limit. Any output that falls outside these limits is considered a defect.

**Real-life applications:**
- A bottle must contain between 495 mL and 505 mL of liquid
- A machined shaft must be between 10.00 mm and 10.05 mm in diameter
- A web page must load in less than 3 seconds
- A bank transaction must be processed within 24 hours

**Key concepts:** `specification limit`, `USL`, `LSL`, `tolerance`, `customer requirement`

---

## Q52. What is the difference between specification limits and control limits?

**Level:** Basic

Specification limits are set by customers or engineers and define what the output should be. Control limits are calculated from actual process data and define what the process is currently doing. Specification limits represent the voice of the customer, while control limits represent the voice of the process.

**Real-life applications:**
- A part specification says 10 mm plus or minus 0.05 mm, but the control chart shows the process varies by plus or minus 0.02 mm
- A service level agreement requires resolution within 4 hours, but the control chart shows the process averages 2 hours with control limits at 1 and 3 hours
- A food label specifies 500 mL, but the filling process control chart centers at 502 mL
- A bank's target is 24-hour processing, but the process control limits show it ranges from 8 to 18 hours

**Key concepts:** `specification limits`, `control limits`, `voice of the customer`, `voice of the process`, `process capability`

---

## Q53. What is a run chart?

**Level:** Basic

A run chart is a simple time-series graph that plots data points in the order they were collected, with a line connecting them and a center line showing the median. It is simpler than a control chart because it does not include statistically calculated control limits. Run charts are useful for detecting trends, shifts, and patterns over time.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking daily sales to identify seasonal trends
- Plotting patient wait times over several weeks to spot a gradual increase
- Monitoring weekly defect counts during the early stages of data collection
- Displaying monthly customer satisfaction scores to visualize improvement

**Key concepts:** `run chart`, `time series`, `trend`, `shift`, `pattern detection`

---

## Q54. What is a scatter diagram?

**Level:** Basic

A scatter diagram is a graph that plots two variables against each other to reveal potential relationships or correlations. Each point represents one observation, with one variable on the x-axis and the other on the y-axis. It helps teams see whether changes in one factor are associated with changes in another.

**Real-life applications:**
- Plotting machine speed against defect rate to see if faster speed increases defects
- Comparing advertising spend to sales revenue to identify correlation
- Examining the relationship between employee experience and error rates
- Checking whether ambient temperature correlates with equipment failure frequency

**Key concepts:** `scatter diagram`, `correlation`, `relationship`, `two variables`, `data visualization`

---

## Q55. What is hypothesis testing in simple terms?

**Level:** Basic

Hypothesis testing is a statistical method for making decisions based on data. The team starts with a null hypothesis that says there is no significant effect or difference, then uses sample data to determine whether there is enough evidence to reject it. If the data shows a statistically significant result, the team accepts the alternative hypothesis.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing whether a new training program significantly reduces error rates
- Determining whether two machines produce parts with different average dimensions
- Evaluating whether a process change led to a genuine improvement or just random fluctuation
- Checking whether customer satisfaction is significantly different between two regions

**Key concepts:** `hypothesis testing`, `null hypothesis`, `alternative hypothesis`, `statistical significance`, `p-value`

---

## Q56. What is a p-value?

**Level:** Basic

A p-value is the probability of observing results as extreme as the data collected, assuming the null hypothesis is true. A small p-value, typically below 0.05, indicates that the observed result is unlikely to have occurred by chance alone, leading the team to reject the null hypothesis and accept that the effect is real.

**Real-life applications:**
- A p-value of 0.02 in a defect comparison study supports the conclusion that the new process is better
- A p-value of 0.15 means there is not enough evidence to confirm a difference between two suppliers
- Reporting p-values in a tollgate review to justify root cause conclusions
- Using p-values to decide whether a pilot test result is statistically meaningful

**Key concepts:** `p-value`, `statistical significance`, `null hypothesis`, `probability`, `evidence`

---

## Q57. What is regression analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Regression analysis is a statistical technique that models the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. It helps teams understand how changes in input factors affect an output and can be used to predict outcomes. In Six Sigma, regression is commonly used in the Analyze and Improve phases.

**Real-life applications:**
- Predicting product quality based on temperature, pressure, and humidity settings
- Modeling the relationship between call volume and staffing on hold times
- Estimating how delivery distance affects delivery time
- Understanding how raw material properties influence finished product strength

**Key concepts:** `regression analysis`, `dependent variable`, `independent variable`, `prediction`, `relationship modeling`

---

## Q58. What is Design of Experiments (DOE)?

**Level:** Basic

Design of Experiments is a structured approach to testing multiple factors and their interactions simultaneously. Instead of changing one variable at a time, DOE allows teams to test combinations of factors efficiently and identify which factors and interactions have the greatest effect on the output.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing combinations of temperature, time, and pressure to optimize a chemical reaction
- Finding the best mix of ingredients for a food product
- Optimizing website layout, color scheme, and call-to-action placement for conversion rates
- Determining the ideal settings for a printing press to minimize color variation

**Key concepts:** `DOE`, `Design of Experiments`, `factors`, `interactions`, `optimization`

---

## Q59. What is Cp in process capability?

**Level:** Basic

Cp is a capability index that compares the width of the specification range to the natural spread of the process. It is calculated by dividing the specification width by six times the standard deviation. A Cp of 1.0 means the process barely fits within specifications, while a Cp of 2.0 means the specifications are twice as wide as the process spread.

**Real-life applications:**
- Calculating Cp for a machining process to verify it can meet dimensional tolerances
- Evaluating whether a filling machine has enough capability to meet volume requirements
- Comparing the capability of two competing pieces of equipment before purchasing
- Reporting Cp values to customers as part of quality assurance documentation

**Key concepts:** `Cp`, `process capability`, `specification width`, `process spread`, `capability index`

---

## Q60. What is Cpk and how does it differ from Cp?

**Level:** Basic

Cpk is a capability index that measures both the spread of the process and how well it is centered within the specification limits. While Cp assumes the process is perfectly centered, Cpk accounts for any shift of the mean toward one specification limit. A process can have a high Cp but a low Cpk if the mean is off-center.

**Real-life applications:**
- A filling process with Cp of 1.5 but Cpk of 0.9 because the mean is shifted toward the lower limit
- An automotive supplier reporting Cpk to a customer to show both capability and centering
- Detecting that a process is capable in theory but consistently drifts toward one specification limit
- Setting a Cpk target of 1.33 as the minimum for production approval

**Key concepts:** `Cpk`, `process centering`, `Cp`, `capability index`, `specification limits`

---

## Q61. What is the difference between DMAIC and DMADV?

**Level:** Basic

DMAIC is used to improve existing processes that are not meeting performance targets, while DMADV (Define, Measure, Analyze, Design, Verify) is used to design new processes or products from scratch. DMADV is part of Design for Six Sigma and is chosen when no process exists yet or when the existing process needs to be completely replaced.

**Real-life applications:**
- Using DMAIC to reduce defects in an existing manufacturing line
- Using DMADV to design a new customer onboarding process from the ground up
- Choosing DMADV when an existing process is fundamentally flawed beyond repair
- Applying DMAIC to optimize a service process that works but has too much variation

**Key concepts:** `DMAIC`, `DMADV`, `Design for Six Sigma`, `process improvement`, `new process design`

---

## Q62. What is a Champion in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

A Champion is a senior leader who sponsors and oversees a Six Sigma project. The Champion ensures that the project aligns with business strategy, removes organizational barriers, provides resources, and keeps the project on track. They serve as the link between the project team and executive leadership.

**Real-life applications:**
- A plant manager championing a quality improvement initiative on the shop floor
- A vice president removing a bureaucratic approval step that was delaying a project
- A director securing budget for additional training needed by the project team
- A regional head connecting a local project to the company's strategic quality goals

**Key concepts:** `Champion`, `sponsor`, `leadership`, `organizational support`, `strategic alignment`

---

## Q63. What is a Master Black Belt?

**Level:** Basic

A Master Black Belt is the highest level of Six Sigma expertise within an organization. Master Black Belts train and mentor Black Belts and Green Belts, develop the organization's Six Sigma methodology, and work with leadership to align improvement efforts with business strategy. They typically have extensive project experience and deep statistical knowledge.

**Real-life applications:**
- Designing and delivering Six Sigma training programs for new belts
- Coaching a Black Belt through a particularly complex statistical analysis
- Advising executives on which projects to prioritize for maximum business impact
- Standardizing tools and templates across the organization's Six Sigma program

**Key concepts:** `Master Black Belt`, `training`, `mentoring`, `methodology development`, `strategic leadership`

---

## Q64. What is a White Belt in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

A White Belt is a person who has received a basic introduction to Six Sigma concepts and terminology. White Belt training is typically short, often just a few hours, and is designed for all employees so they understand what Six Sigma is and how they might contribute to improvement efforts even if they do not lead projects.

**Real-life applications:**
- All new employees completing a half-day Six Sigma awareness course during onboarding
- A receptionist understanding basic terminology to better support a project team
- Frontline workers recognizing waste in their daily tasks after White Belt training
- Building organization-wide awareness of quality improvement culture

**Key concepts:** `White Belt`, `awareness training`, `basic concepts`, `organizational culture`, `Six Sigma introduction`

---

## Q65. What is a Yellow Belt in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

A Yellow Belt has more training than a White Belt and can actively participate as a team member on Six Sigma projects. Yellow Belts understand the DMAIC process, basic tools, and can contribute to data collection and small improvement tasks. They typically work under the guidance of a Green Belt or Black Belt.

**Real-life applications:**
- A warehouse worker collecting defect data for a Green Belt project
- An office administrator helping to map a process during the Define phase
- A sales representative participating in a VOC data gathering exercise
- A technician testing a pilot solution during the Improve phase

**Key concepts:** `Yellow Belt`, `team member`, `DMAIC basics`, `data collection`, `project support`

## Q66. What is benchmarking in Six Sigma?

**Level:** Basic

Benchmarking is the practice of comparing your process performance against the best practices or performance levels of other organizations, competitors, or internal units. It helps teams set realistic improvement targets and learn from others who have already achieved high performance in similar processes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing your factory's defect rate to the industry leader's published performance
- Studying how the top-performing branch handles customer complaints and adopting their methods
- Using industry reports to set a target sigma level for your process
- Visiting a best-in-class company to observe their manufacturing practices

**Key concepts:** `benchmarking`, `best practices`, `comparison`, `target setting`, `competitive analysis`

---

## Q67. What is a cause-and-effect diagram?

**Level:** Basic

A cause-and-effect diagram is another name for a fishbone or Ishikawa diagram. It visually organizes potential causes of a problem into categories, making it easier for teams to brainstorm comprehensively and avoid overlooking important factors. It is one of the most commonly used tools in the Analyze phase.

**Real-life applications:**
- Categorizing potential causes of late deliveries under transportation, staffing, systems, and weather
- Organizing reasons for high call abandonment rates by people, technology, and process
- Structuring a brainstorming session about product defects in a manufacturing setting
- Identifying potential causes of patient falls in a hospital

**Key concepts:** `cause-and-effect diagram`, `fishbone diagram`, `Ishikawa diagram`, `brainstorming`, `categorization`

---

## Q68. What is a check sheet?

**Level:** Basic

A check sheet is a simple data collection form designed to gather information in a systematic and organized way. It typically consists of a table or list where tally marks or counts are recorded as events occur. Check sheets make data collection easy and consistent, reducing the chance of recording errors.

**Real-life applications:**
- Recording the number of each defect type found during a shift inspection
- Tracking how many customers visit each department of a store by hour
- Counting the frequency of different complaint categories in a call center
- Logging equipment downtime events by cause and machine

**Key concepts:** `check sheet`, `data collection`, `tally`, `frequency`, `organized recording`

---

## Q69. What is the cost of quality (COQ)?

**Level:** Basic

The cost of quality is the total cost associated with ensuring quality and dealing with failures. It includes prevention costs for stopping defects before they happen, appraisal costs for inspection and testing, internal failure costs for catching defects before delivery, and external failure costs for defects found by customers. Six Sigma projects aim to shift spending from failure costs to prevention costs.

**Real-life applications:**
- Calculating the cost of warranty claims and returns as external failure costs
- Adding up inspection and testing expenses as appraisal costs
- Measuring the investment in training and process design as prevention costs
- Tracking scrap and rework expenses as internal failure costs

**Key concepts:** `cost of quality`, `prevention costs`, `appraisal costs`, `internal failure`, `external failure`

---

## Q70. What is continuous improvement?

**Level:** Basic

Continuous improvement is the ongoing effort to enhance products, services, and processes over time through incremental changes or breakthrough projects. It is a core philosophy underlying Six Sigma and Lean, emphasizing that no process is ever perfect and there is always room for improvement. It requires a culture where everyone looks for ways to do things better.

**Real-life applications:**
- A factory that runs quarterly Kaizen events to make small process improvements
- A software team that holds sprint retrospectives to identify and fix workflow issues
- A hospital that tracks quality metrics monthly and launches projects when targets are missed
- A restaurant chain that regularly updates recipes and procedures based on customer feedback

**Key concepts:** `continuous improvement`, `Kaizen`, `incremental change`, `culture`, `ongoing effort`

## 4. Formulas

### 4.1 DPMO + Sigma-Level Mapping
**Formula:** `DPMO = (Defects / (Units × Opportunities-per-unit)) × 1,000,000`
**Threshold:** <=3.4 = 6σ (world-class); ~6,210 = 4σ (typical); >66,807 = <3σ (poor).
**Numeric example (IT):** API processes 500,000 requests/day with 5 opportunities per request (auth, route, validate, execute, respond); 120 defects logged. DPMO = 120 / (500,000 × 5) × 1e6 = 48. That maps to ~5.4σ. Interpretation: strong, not yet 6σ.

### 4.2 Cp — Process Capability (potential)
**Formula:** `Cp = (USL − LSL) / (6σ)`
**Threshold:** Cp < 1.0 = incapable; 1.0–1.33 = marginal; 1.33–1.67 = capable; >=1.67 = Six Sigma-class.
**Numeric example (Product):** Checkout latency spec = 200–800ms (LSL–USL). Observed σ = 70ms. Cp = (800 − 200) / (6 × 70) = 600/420 = 1.43. Capable on spread, but says nothing about centering — use Cpk next.

### 4.3 Cpk — Centered Capability
**Formula:** `Cpk = min[ (USL − μ) / 3σ , (μ − LSL) / 3σ ]`
**Threshold:** Cpk >= 1.33 acceptable; >=1.67 target; <1.0 action needed; Cpk < Cp reveals centering gap.
**Numeric example (AI):** Model-inference latency spec LSL=50ms, USL=300ms. μ=220ms, σ=30ms. Upper side = (300−220)/(3×30) = 0.89. Lower side = (220−50)/(3×30) = 1.89. Cpk = min = 0.89 — incapable on upper side despite fine Cp. Action: tune p95 via batch-size tuning.

### 4.4 Mean, Median, Standard Deviation
**Formulas:** `μ = Σxᵢ / n` ; Median = middle value of sorted set ; `σ = sqrt( Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / (n−1) )`
**Threshold / rule:** If |mean − median| > 0.5σ suspect skew — consider median or log-transform.
**Numeric example (Consulting):** Ticket resolution times (hrs): [2, 3, 3, 4, 48]. Mean = 12, median = 3, σ ≈ 19.8. Mean grossly misleads because of one outlier — report median with IQR on executive dashboard, flag the 48-hr case as special-cause.

### 4.5 RPN — Risk Priority Number (FMEA)
**Formula:** `RPN = Severity × Occurrence × Detection` (each 1–10)
**Threshold:** RPN > 100 = must mitigate; >200 = immediate action; top 20% prioritized regardless.
**Numeric example (IT/AI):** FMEA row "silent model-drift on fraud classifier." S=9 (regulatory hit), O=5 (quarterly), D=8 (no drift monitor). RPN = 9×5×8 = 360 → immediate action: add PSI drift alarm; post-mitigation D=3, RPN = 135 — still high, add weekly eval harness.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| # | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Baseline before you improve (Measure phase) | Start changing code before you've measured |
| 2 | Validate measurement with MSA/Gage R&R | Trust a dashboard without checking how the data is captured |
| 3 | Use Pareto to pick the vital few defect classes | Spread thin across every defect type equally |
| 4 | Separate common vs special cause before reacting | Treat every latency spike as an incident worthy of RCA |
| 5 | Quote Cpk (centered) when defending capability | Quote Cp alone and declare victory |
| 6 | Tie CTQs to VOC in measurable units | Ship a "reduce defects" charter with no numeric target |
| 7 | Lock Control phase with SOP + SPC + handoff owner | Declare done at Improve and walk away — gains decay |
| 8 | Pilot before full rollout | Deploy a DOE-winning setting org-wide untested |
| 9 | Distinguish defect (issue) vs defective (unit) in reporting | Conflate the two and double-count DPMO |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios

### Scenario 1 — Green Belt interview walkthrough (AI/Product)
**Context:** Candidate interviews for Senior PM at a fintech. Interviewer: "Walk me through a quality project using DMAIC."
**Response:** "Our model-deployment pipeline had a 14% failure rate on rollout checks. **Define:** Charter capped scope to tier-1 models, CTQ = rollout-check pass rate >=98%. **Measure:** Pulled 6 months from Jira + Grafana; baseline = 86%, DPMO ≈ 140k, sigma ≈ 2.6. Did Gage R&R on the check pipeline — 18% study variation, acceptable. **Analyze:** Pareto showed 62% of failures came from schema mismatches, 18% from stale training data. Fishbone + 5 Whys landed on no contract test between feature store and scoring service. **Improve:** Piloted schema-registry gate on 3 models, DOE on retry settings; pilot pass rate 99.1%. **Control:** SOP in Confluence, p-chart in Looker, MBB review monthly. **Result:** Cpk on pass-rate went from 0.4 to 1.5; saved ~$240k/yr in rollback toil."
**Tools:** Jira, Grafana, Looker, Confluence.

### Scenario 2 — Common vs special cause on latency dashboard (Consulting)
**Context:** Consulting interview. Prompt: "Your client sees the p99 API latency jump to 1,200ms yesterday from a usual 400ms. Is this a Six Sigma problem?"
**Response:** "First I'd plot a control chart of daily p99 over the last 60 days with UCL = μ + 3σ. If yesterday's 1,200ms is outside UCL it's **special cause** — not inherent to the process; needs root-cause on that specific day (deploy? traffic spike? dependency outage?). If the system normally swings 300–1,300ms and yesterday sits inside UCL, it's **common cause** — the process itself is incapable, and no single-incident RCA will help. The right response is a capability project: measure σ, set CTQ (say p99 <=600ms), run DMAIC. Telling the client to escalate every swing when the process is incapable is the classic mistake — you churn the ops team without moving the mean." **Tools:** Grafana for control chart, Jira for incident linkage, Looker for weekly Cpk trend.

### Scenario 3 — ANTI-EXAMPLE: confusing spec vs control limits (IT)
**Context:** A Green-Belt-in-training SRE sees control chart UCL = 850ms for checkout latency and commits the team to an SLA of 850ms with the customer.
**What went wrong:** **Control limits** describe what the **process** does today (±3σ of observed μ); **spec limits** (USL/LSL) describe what the **customer** demands. Signing an SLA at UCL means any normal common-cause swing above UCL breaches SLA by definition, and the process has ~0% margin — Cpk ≈ 0.
**Quantified cost:** With μ=500ms, σ=120ms, UCL=860ms used as USL → Cpk = (860−500)/(3×120) = 1.0, DPMO ≈ 1,350 → ~1,350 SLA breaches per million requests. At 50M monthly requests and $0.40 SLA credit per breach, that's ~$27k/month = $324k/yr in avoidable credits. The fix: set USL from **VOC** (customer says 1,000ms tolerable), compute Cpk against that, then improve process until Cpk >= 1.33 before signing. **Tools:** Grafana (control chart), Jira (SLA-credit log), Confluence (SLA doc), Looker (monthly Cpk trend).

## 9. Quick-Recall Card

- **DMAIC** = Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control — and Control is where most projects die.
- **DPMO → Sigma:** 6,210 ≈ 4σ; 233 ≈ 5σ; 3.4 = 6σ. Yield is the complement.
- **Cp** asks "is the spread narrow enough?"; **Cpk** asks "and is it centered?" — always quote Cpk.
- **Common cause** = process itself (fix the system); **special cause** = one-off (fix the incident).
- **Defect** = one issue; **defective** = a unit with >=1 defect. DPMO uses defects, not defectives.
- **Role-lens question:** "Given your role (IT / AI / Product / Consulting), pick one CTQ you own this quarter — what's its current sigma level, and which DMAIC phase would most move the needle this sprint?"
