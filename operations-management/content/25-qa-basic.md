# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and concepts (Q1–Q70).

## Q1. What is Operations Management?

**Level:** Basic

Operations Management is the part of an organization that plans and controls how work is done to create and deliver products or services. It focuses on designing processes, using resources wisely, and improving performance so customers get value in the form of quality, speed, and fair cost.

**Real-life applications:**
- In a restaurant, it decides how orders flow from counter to kitchen to table.
- In a factory, it organizes machines, workers, and materials to produce goods.
- In hospitals, it helps reduce patient waiting and improve care flow.
- In schools, it supports smoother scheduling and resource use.

**Key concepts:** `operations`, `process`, `resources`, `output`, `value`

---

## Q2. What is the main goal of Operations Management?

**Level:** Basic

The main goal of Operations Management is to deliver products or services efficiently and reliably while meeting customer needs. This means balancing cost, quality, speed, and flexibility so the organization can perform well and remain competitive.

**Real-life applications:**
- A delivery company aims to deliver on time without raising costs too much.
- A clinic tries to serve more patients while maintaining safe care standards.
- A manufacturer targets fewer defects and faster production.
- A city office improves service speed for citizens with the same staff.

**Key concepts:** `efficiency`, `quality`, `speed`, `flexibility`, `competitiveness`

---

## Q3. What is a “process” in operations?

**Level:** Basic

A process is a set of steps that transforms inputs (like materials, time, labor, or information) into outputs (products or services). Processes can be simple or complex, but they always describe how work is done from start to finish.

**Real-life applications:**
- Online shopping includes steps from order placing to packing to delivery.
- A bank loan process includes application, verification, approval, and disbursal.
- A classroom process includes teaching, assignments, grading, and feedback.
- A repair shop process includes diagnosis, fixing, testing, and return.

**Key concepts:** `inputs`, `outputs`, `process steps`, `transformation`, `workflow`

---

## Q4. What are “inputs” and “outputs” in operations?

**Level:** Basic

Inputs are the resources used to do work, such as people, materials, machines, money, time, and information. Outputs are the results produced, such as finished goods, completed services, or delivered customer experiences.

**Real-life applications:**
- A bakery uses flour, ovens, and staff (inputs) to produce bread (output).
- A hospital uses doctors and equipment to provide treatment outcomes.
- A call center uses agents and scripts to solve customer issues.
- A construction team uses tools and labor to build a house.

**Key concepts:** `inputs`, `outputs`, `resources`, `goods`, `services`

---

## Q5. What is the difference between “operations” and “supply chain”?

**Level:** Basic

Operations focuses on how work is done inside the organization to produce and deliver. Supply chain includes the wider network of suppliers, transportation, warehouses, and retailers that move materials and products from source to customer.

**Real-life applications:**
- A phone factory’s assembly work is operations.
- Buying chips from suppliers and shipping phones to stores is supply chain.
- A café’s cooking is operations; ingredient sourcing is supply chain.
- A hospital’s care delivery is operations; medical supply sourcing is supply chain.

**Key concepts:** `operations`, `supply chain`, `suppliers`, `logistics`, `delivery`

---

## Q6. What is the “goods–services continuum”?

**Level:** Basic

The goods–services continuum explains that most offerings are not purely goods or purely services. Many products include service elements, and many services include physical goods, so offerings fall somewhere between the two.

**Real-life applications:**
- A smartphone sale includes warranty and support services.
- A restaurant provides food (good) and dining experience (service).
- A haircut is a service but uses products like shampoo.
- Online learning is a service supported by digital tools and content.

**Key concepts:** `goods`, `services`, `bundling`, `customer experience`, `continuum`

---

## Q7. What does “value” mean in Operations Management?

**Level:** Basic

Value is the benefit a customer receives compared to the time, effort, and money they give. Operations creates value by improving quality, reducing delays, cutting waste, and making the experience reliable.

**Real-life applications:**
- Fast checkouts increase value for shoppers.
- Reliable bus schedules increase value for commuters.
- Lower defect rates increase value for product users.
- Clear and quick government services increase value for citizens.

**Key concepts:** `value`, `customer needs`, `quality`, `time`, `waste`

---

## Q8. What is productivity in operations?

**Level:** Basic

Productivity is a measure of how effectively resources are used to produce outputs. In simple terms, it compares what you produce to what you use, helping managers see whether work is becoming more efficient over time.

**Real-life applications:**
- A factory tracks units produced per worker-hour.
- A hospital tracks patients served per day with available staff.
- A school tracks students supported per teacher or classroom.
- A warehouse tracks orders packed per hour.

**Key concepts:** `productivity`, `efficiency`, `resources`, `output rate`, `performance`

**Real-life applications:**

## Q11. What is “capacity” in operations?

**Level:** Basic

Capacity is the maximum amount of work a process, machine, or organization can complete in a given time. It helps managers plan resources so demand can be met without excessive waiting or waste.

**Real-life applications:**
- A cinema’s capacity is its seats per show.
- A clinic’s capacity is patients treated per day.
- A bakery’s capacity is loaves baked per hour.
- A university’s capacity is students taught per semester.

**Key concepts:** `capacity`, `demand`, `resources`, `output`, `planning`

---

## Q12. What does “utilization” mean?

**Level:** Basic

Utilization shows how much of available capacity is actually being used. High utilization can reduce costs, but if it becomes too high, it can increase delays, stress, and service problems.

**Real-life applications:**
- A fully booked salon may cause long customer waits.
- A factory running nonstop may face breakdown risks.
- A crowded bus system causes delays and discomfort.
- A call center at peak utilization increases hold times.

**Key concepts:** `utilization`, `capacity`, `workload`, `congestion`, `performance`

---

## Q13. What is a bottleneck?

**Level:** Basic

A bottleneck is the slowest step in a process that limits the overall output. Work tends to pile up before the bottleneck, and improving it often improves the entire system’s performance.

**Real-life applications:**
- A slow oven limits a bakery’s total daily output.
- One overworked nurse station creates queues in a hospital.
- A single payment counter slows a busy cafeteria.
- A slow approval desk delays document processing in offices.

**Key concepts:** `bottleneck`, `constraint`, `throughput`, `queues`, `flow`

---

## Q14. What is throughput?

**Level:** Basic

Throughput is the rate at which a process produces completed outputs over time. It tells how many units are finished per hour, day, or week, and is often limited by the bottleneck step.

**Real-life applications:**
- A factory measures cars completed per day.
- A support desk measures tickets closed per hour.
- A kitchen measures meals served per lunch period.
- A court measures cases resolved per month.

**Key concepts:** `throughput`, `output rate`, `flow`, `capacity`, `bottleneck`

---

## Q15. What is lead time?

**Level:** Basic

Lead time is the total time from a customer request (or order) to final delivery. It includes working time plus waiting time, and reducing lead time usually improves customer satisfaction.

**Real-life applications:**
- Online shoppers care about delivery lead time.
- Patients care about treatment lead time from registration to discharge.
- Manufacturers care about lead time to fulfill distributor orders.
- Students care about lead time for exam results and feedback.

**Key concepts:** `lead time`, `waiting`, `delivery`, `responsiveness`, `cycle time`

---

## Q16. What is work-in-process (WIP)?

**Level:** Basic

Work-in-process is the inventory of items that have started but are not yet finished. High WIP often means more waiting and longer lead times, especially if the process is unbalanced.

**Real-life applications:**
- Half-assembled furniture waiting at stations is WIP.
- Pending loan applications in review are WIP in banking.
- Patients waiting for tests are WIP in healthcare.
- Unfinished assignments in grading are WIP in education.

**Key concepts:** `WIP`, `inventory`, `flow`, `waiting`, `process balance`

---

**Real-life applications:**

- Hospitals standardize checklists to improve safety.

## Q20. What is “continuous improvement”?

**Level:** Basic

Continuous improvement is the habit of regularly looking for small ways to make processes better. Instead of waiting for big changes, teams make ongoing improvements to reduce waste, errors, and delays.

**Real-life applications:**
- A call center reviews weekly issues and fixes common causes.
- A factory team improves workstation layout to save time.
- A school improves timetable planning each term.
- A city office reduces form steps to serve citizens faster.

**Key concepts:** `continuous improvement`, `kaizen`, `waste reduction`, `learning`, `performance`

---

## Q21. What is “Lean” in Operations Management?

**Level:** Basic

Lean is an approach that aims to deliver value with less waste. It focuses on removing activities that do not help the customer, improving flow, and building simple, reliable processes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hospitals reduce unnecessary patient movement between departments.
- Retailers reduce extra stock that sits unsold.
- Factories reduce rework and waiting between stations.
- Students use lean thinking to streamline study routines and time use.

**Key concepts:** `lean`, `waste`, `flow`, `value`, `continuous improvement`

---

## Q22. What is “waste” in lean thinking?

**Level:** Basic

Waste is any activity that uses time, effort, or resources but does not add value from the customer’s point of view. Examples include waiting, rework, unnecessary movement, and excess inventory.

**Real-life applications:**
- Customers waiting in a long queue is waste.
- Fixing the same error repeatedly is waste.
- Moving materials across long distances is waste.
- Printing unnecessary documents wastes paper and time.

**Key concepts:** `waste`, `non-value-added`, `waiting`, `rework`, `excess inventory`

---

**Real-life applications:**

## Q35. What is the “critical path” in a project?

**Level:** Basic

The critical path is the sequence of tasks that determines the earliest possible completion date of a project. If a task on the critical path is delayed, the whole project finish date is delayed.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION:
Construction projects track critical tasks like foundation and approvals.
Software teams track critical tasks like core module completion.
Event planning tracks venue booking and permits as critical items.
Public projects track tendering and procurement milestones carefully.

**Key concepts:** `critical path`, `project timeline`, `dependencies`, `delays`, `milestones`

REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION:
Factories balance tasks to meet daily production targets.

Batch production means making items in groups rather than one-by-one or continuously. It often saves setup time but can increase waiting and inventory between batches.

Packaged snacks are made-to-stock for retail shelves.

## Q52. What is the bullwhip effect (simple definition)?

**Level:** Basic

The bullwhip effect is when small changes in customer demand cause bigger changes in orders and inventory as you move up the supply chain. It happens due to poor information sharing, forecasting errors, and batch ordering.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION:
Retail promotions cause distributors to overorder from factories.
Seasonal demand shifts create large swings for raw material suppliers.
Pharmacies overorder during panic buying periods.
Food supply chains face large upstream shocks from small consumer changes.

**Key concepts:** `bullwhip effect`, `demand variability`, `forecasting`, `ordering`, `supply chain`

---

Pharmacies aim for high service levels for critical medicines.
Retailers set service targets for fast-moving items.
Factories set service levels for spare parts to avoid downtime.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Operations Stack
**Purpose:** Organize the question bank into a manager’s working stack, from inputs to customer value.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Inputs -> process -> capacity -> quality/control -> flow -> delivery -> customer value
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: process design - how work is transformed.
Component 2: capacity and utilization - whether the system can absorb demand.
Component 3: quality and control - whether output is reliable.
Component 4: delivery and value - whether the customer actually experiences the promised result.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS team uses the stack to see that slow onboarding is not a "sales problem" alone; it may be a process, capacity, QA, or delivery problem. The decision is whether to redesign the workflow or only reassign ownership.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When a cross-functional problem is being described in silo language.

### Flow-Constraint Dashboard
**Purpose:** Link queueing, WIP, throughput, lead time, and bottlenecks into one control view.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Demand -> queue -> WIP -> bottleneck -> throughput -> lead time
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: demand and variability - what enters the system.
Component 2: WIP and queue length - what is waiting.
Component 3: bottleneck and throughput - what limits flow.
Component 4: lead time and service level - what the customer experiences.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI support team sees that ticket arrivals, not agent effort, are driving backlog growth. The dashboard shows whether to add heads, deflect demand, or automate the simplest cases.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team says "we are busy" but customers still wait.

### Operating-Model Choice Matrix
**Purpose:** Compare the main design choices in operations: goods vs services, make-to-stock vs make-to-order, push vs pull, product layout vs process layout, job shop vs assembly line.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
High variety / high customization -> job shop, make-to-order, process layout, pull
High volume / high standardization -> assembly line, make-to-stock, product layout, push
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: variety and customization - how different the demand is.
Component 2: volume and repetition - how standard the work is.
Component 3: layout and routing - how work moves physically.
Component 4: replenishment logic - whether production is forecast-driven or demand-driven.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A manufacturing plant with high volume and low variety should lean toward product layout and line balancing, while a consulting support team with unique cases behaves more like a job shop with routing and flexibility.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone is trying to force the wrong operating model onto the work.

### Improvement Ladder
**Purpose:** Show the order of improvement tools from basic discipline to more advanced control.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
standardization -> waste reduction -> QA/QC -> benchmarking -> Six Sigma -> TOC
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: standardization and training - remove variation in the basic method.
Component 2: waste reduction and simplification - remove non-value-added work.
Component 3: QA/QC and control charts - detect and prevent defects.
Component 4: benchmarking, Six Sigma, and TOC - improve against best practice and the bottleneck.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A services team first standardizes ticket handling, then uses a control chart to watch defects, then applies Six Sigma to the high-variance step, and finally attacks the bottleneck that still limits throughput.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is debating whether it needs better discipline, better quality control, or a more advanced improvement program.

## 4. Formulas
The source is a Q&A bank, so the formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source] and are included as practical manager-friendly checks.

### Formula 1: Productivity
Formula: `Productivity = output / inputs`
Variables:
Output = completed units, services, or tasks.
Inputs = labor, materials, time, or capital used.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the operation is producing more value per unit of resource.
How to interpret the output:
Low -> waste or underuse -> improve process.
Rising -> efficiency gain -> standardize the gain.
High -> strong efficiency -> verify quality and flexibility are still healthy.
Worked example with numbers: If a support team resolves 500 tickets with 100 labor hours, productivity is 5 tickets per hour. If it later resolves 650 in the same hours with no quality drop, that is a real gain.

### Formula 2: Utilization
Formula: `Utilization = used capacity / available capacity`
Variables:
Used capacity = actual work performed.
Available capacity = total possible work in the period.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the system is overloaded or underused.
How to interpret the output:
Below 70% -> slack or underuse.
70%-85% -> usually healthy.
Above 85% -> congestion and waiting risk rise.
Worked example with numbers: A clinic uses 34 of 40 available service hours, so utilization is 85%. That is efficient on paper, but it leaves little room for spikes or emergencies.

### Formula 3: Service Level
Formula: `Service level = fulfilled demand / total demand`
Variables:
Fulfilled demand = demand that was met without stockout or failure.
Total demand = all demand received.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the system is available when customers need it.
How to interpret the output:
Low -> stockout or availability problem.
Moderate -> acceptable but improvable.
High -> strong protection for customer experience.
Worked example with numbers: If 950 of 1,000 orders are fulfilled on time, service level is 95%. Decision: the replenishment system is working, but the team should check whether the remaining 50 cases are concentrated in a critical item class.

### Formula 4: On-Time Delivery
Formula: `On-time delivery = on-time deliveries / total deliveries`
Variables:
On-time deliveries = deliveries meeting the promised deadline.
Total deliveries = all deliveries in the period.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether dependability is being delivered.
How to interpret the output:
Below 90% -> reliability issue.
90%-95% -> workable but improvable.
Above 95% -> strong dependability.
Worked example with numbers: If a distribution center delivers 470 of 500 orders on time, the rate is 94%. Decision: keep watching the late-order root causes before scaling the network.

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Hospital Flow and Patient Waiting
Situation: A hospital is struggling with long waits, rework from missing records, and uneven patient flow. The leadership team needs to decide whether the real problem is scheduling, capacity, or quality control.
Applicable framework/metric: Flow-Constraint Dashboard and utilization.
Analysis: If utilization is above 85% and WIP keeps rising, the bottleneck is being overloaded. If rework and defects are high, quality assurance and control chart discipline matter as much as capacity.
Decision rule: If demand exceeds capacity, add or flex capacity; if quality failures drive the delay, fix the process before staffing more; if customer satisfaction is falling despite acceptable wait times, inspect service encounter and communication.
Action: Standardize intake, separate urgent from routine cases, and monitor wait time and defect rate on the same board.

### Scenario 2: E-Commerce Order Fulfillment
Situation: A retailer has low stockouts on top-selling items but excess inventory on slow movers. Finance wants lower holding cost, while operations wants to protect service level and on-time delivery.
Applicable framework/metric: Operating-Model Choice Matrix and service level.
Analysis: High-volume items may justify make-to-stock, while customized or slow-moving items may fit make-to-order or pull logic. If service level drops when inventory is cut, the team may be over-cutting the wrong items.
Decision rule: If service level falls below target, increase safety stock or improve replenishment; if excess inventory is concentrated in C items, simplify control there; if a few items cause most value or risk, give them A-class attention.
Action: Reclassify the inventory, tighten replenishment on critical SKUs, and use a distribution center layout that shortens picking and packing time.

### Scenario 3: Project and Workflow Control in a Consulting Team
Situation: A consulting team is running a temporary project with dependencies, milestones, and a long critical path. The team also has recurring internal workflows that look like a job shop, not an assembly line.
Applicable framework/metric: Improvement Ladder and project timeline / critical path.
Analysis: Delays on the critical path will delay the project finish, regardless of how much work is done elsewhere. If repeated handoff defects create rework, a control chart or poka-yoke step may be needed.
Decision rule: If dependencies dominate the timeline, protect the critical path; if repeated errors dominate, add QA/QC; if the bottleneck keeps moving, use TOC to focus the team.
Action: Re-sequence tasks, cut unnecessary handoffs, and create one owner for the bottleneck milestone.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build a shared glossary for the operations terms that matter in your team.
2. Map one real workflow end-to-end using process mapping or a service blueprint.
3. Add the core KPIs: productivity, utilization, service level, quality, and on-time delivery.
4. Classify the operating model: goods or services, make-to-stock or make-to-order, push or pull.
5. Find the bottleneck, the WIP pile, and the highest-defect step before changing headcount.
6. Standardize the repeatable parts, then apply lean, QA/QC, Six Sigma, or TOC where the data says it matters.
7. Revisit the plan quarterly because demand, volume, variety, and risk change.
