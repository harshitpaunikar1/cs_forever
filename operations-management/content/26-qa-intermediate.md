# Practice Q&A — Intermediate

Analysis, comparison, and applied reasoning (Q71–Q140).

## Q71. How do competitive priorities shape operations decisions?

**Level:** Intermediate

Competitive priorities (cost, quality, speed, dependability, flexibility) guide what operations should focus on. When a company chooses a priority, it influences process design, staffing, technology, inventory levels, and supplier choices so operations consistently supports what customers value most.

**Real-life applications:**
- A low-cost retailer designs simple processes and high-efficiency logistics.
- A premium brand invests more in quality checks and skilled labor.
- A fast-delivery service builds capacity buffers to meet speed promises.
- Public services prioritize dependability to maintain citizen trust.

**Key concepts:** `competitive priorities`, `alignment`, `trade-offs`, `process design`, `performance objectives`

---

## Q72. What are trade-offs in operations, and why can’t firms maximize everything?

**Level:** Intermediate

Trade-offs mean improving one performance area often makes another worse, such as increasing flexibility raising cost or increasing speed reducing customization. Firms can’t maximize everything because resources are limited and different goals require different designs, so they must choose what matters most for their market.

**Real-life applications:**
- A fast-food chain sacrifices customization to keep speed high.
- A custom tailor accepts longer lead times for higher flexibility.
- Hospitals balance speed with patient safety and quality standards.
- Governments balance low cost with service coverage and fairness.

**Key concepts:** `trade-offs`, `competitive priorities`, `resource limits`, `operational focus`, `strategy fit`

---

## Q73. Explain the difference between order qualifiers and order winners.

**Level:** Intermediate

Order qualifiers are the basic features a product or service must have just to be considered by customers, like acceptable quality or price. Order winners are the factors that actually make customers choose one provider over another, like fastest delivery or best design.

**Real-life applications:**
- In smartphones, basic battery life may be a qualifier, camera quality a winner.
- In hospitals, cleanliness is a qualifier, faster treatment may be a winner.
- In education, accredited curriculum is a qualifier, placement outcomes a winner.
- In public services, legality is a qualifier, speed and clarity can be winners.

**Key concepts:** `order qualifiers`, `order winners`, `market needs`, `customer choice`, `competitive advantage`

## Q74. How does product design influence process design?

Product design affects how work must be done, including materials, number of steps, skill needs, and equipment. A complex design often requires more time and control, while a simpler design can support faster flow, easier assembly, and fewer errors.

**Real-life applications:**
- A modular product design makes repairs and assembly quicker.
- Standard packaging design simplifies warehousing and shipping.
- A clinic’s service package design shapes patient flow and staffing.
- A digital form design reduces manual verification work in offices.

**Key concepts:** `product design`, `process design`, `complexity`, `standardization`, `DFMA`

## Q75. Why is designing for manufacturability and assembly important?

**Level:** Intermediate

Designing for manufacturability and assembly means creating products that are easy to make and put together. It reduces parts, simplifies steps, lowers defects, shortens lead time, and saves cost because production becomes smoother and less error-prone.

**Real-life applications:**
- Reducing part types lowers purchasing and inventory complexity.
- Simple assemblies reduce training time for new workers.
- Lower defect risk reduces warranty and returns costs.
- Faster assembly helps meet peak demand without expanding facilities.

## Q76. How does capacity planning differ between services and manufacturing?

**Level:** Intermediate

Manufacturing can store inventory to buffer demand, but services usually cannot store service output, so service capacity must be closely matched to demand in real time. Services also face higher customer variability, making staffing flexibility and scheduling critical.

**Real-life applications:**
- Factories build inventory before festivals; clinics add staff during peak hours.
- Airlines adjust prices and schedules instead of “storing” seats.
- Restaurants manage capacity through reservations and staffing shifts.
- Citizen service centers extend hours during peak application periods.

**Key concepts:** `capacity planning`, `perishability`, `demand variability`, `staffing flexibility`, `services vs manufacturing`

## Q77. What is capacity cushioning, and when is it useful?

**Level:** Intermediate

Capacity cushioning means keeping extra capacity above average demand to handle variability, peak periods, or unexpected disruptions. It is useful in services and high-uncertainty environments where delays are costly or customer waiting harms satisfaction.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hospitals keep extra beds for emergencies.
- Call centers keep extra agents for outage spikes.
- Airports keep spare counters open during travel surges.
- IT helpdesks keep backup capacity during system upgrades.

**Key concepts:** `capacity cushion`, `variability`, `service quality`, `risk buffer`, `responsiveness`

## Q78. How do economies and diseconomies of scale affect capacity decisions?

**Real-life applications:**
- A large warehouse lowers unit shipping cost but increases coordination needs.
- Mega hospitals reduce equipment cost per patient but may face overcrowding.
- Very large factories risk quality issues if supervision becomes hard.
- Big universities gain scale but face administrative complexity.

**Key concepts:** `economies of scale`, `diseconomies`, `unit cost`, `complexity`, `capacity strategy`

## Q80. Why can very high utilization cause longer waiting times?

**Level:** Intermediate

When utilization is very high, there is little spare capacity to handle random fluctuations and variability. Even small surges create queues, and the system takes longer to recover, causing waiting times to increase sharply.

**Real-life applications:**
- A fully booked clinic cannot handle walk-ins without long waits.
- Overloaded public transport creates crowding and delays.
- Call centers near full capacity see long hold times during spikes.
- Overutilized machines create backlogs that delay deliveries.

**Key concepts:** `utilization`, `variability`, `congestion`, `queues`, `recovery time`

Process mapping shows the actual sequence of steps, delays, handoffs, and rework, revealing hidden causes of poor performance. It replaces assumptions with visibility so improvement efforts focus on real bottlenecks and waste rather than guesses.

**Real-life applications:**

- A hospital finds patients wait between departments due to scheduling gaps.

## Q82. Explain the relationship between bottlenecks, WIP, and lead time.

**Level:** Intermediate

Bottlenecks slow flow, causing work to pile up before them as WIP. Higher WIP increases waiting time, which increases lead time. Reducing bottleneck delays or controlling WIP usually shortens lead time and improves delivery reliability.

**Real-life applications:**
- Factories see piles of semi-finished items before a slow machine.
- Clinics see patient queues before the doctor with highest demand.
- Support desks see ticket backlogs before a specialized team.
- Government offices see piles of applications before final approval.

**Key concepts:** `bottleneck`, `WIP`, `lead time`, `queues`, `flow time`

## Q84. What is the purpose of buffering in process design?

**Level:** Intermediate

Buffering protects a process from variability by using inventory buffers, time buffers, or extra capacity. It helps prevent starvation and blocking, but too much buffering increases cost and reduces flow efficiency.

**Real-life applications:**
- Warehouses keep buffer stock to handle supplier delays.
- Hospitals use extra appointment slots to handle overruns.
- Factories use small WIP buffers to prevent line stoppages.
- Schools schedule short breaks to handle timetable disruptions.

**Key concepts:** `buffering`, `variability`, `inventory buffer`, `capacity buffer`, `starvation and blocking`

## Q85. Why is Little’s Law useful for analyzing processes conceptually?

**Level:** Intermediate

Little’s Law links work-in-process, throughput, and flow time in a stable system. Conceptually, it highlights that if throughput stays similar, reducing WIP reduces flow time, and if flow time is too high, WIP is often too high or flow is blocked.

**Real-life applications:**
- A clinic reduces waiting by limiting too many patients in the system at once.
- A factory controls WIP to reduce lead times.
- A call center reduces backlog to shorten resolution time.
- Universities reduce administrative backlog to speed approvals.

**Key concepts:** `Little’s Law`, `WIP`, `throughput`, `flow time`, `stability`

- Food delivery emphasizes fast response and capacity matching.

## Q94. Why does variability make “average-based” planning risky?

**Level:** Intermediate

Average-based planning ignores peaks and randomness. Even if average demand equals average capacity, variability can cause congestion and long waits during busy periods, while low periods create idle time. Planning must account for variation, not just averages.

**Real-life applications:**
- Clinics staffed only for average arrivals face severe queues during surges.
- Public transport planned for averages becomes overcrowded at rush hour.
- IT support planned for average tickets collapses during outages.
- Retail stores staffed for average sales lose customers during promotions.

**Key concepts:** `variability`, `averages`, `peak demand`, `congestion`, `capacity buffers`

## Q99. Compare prevention-based quality and inspection-based quality approaches.

**Level:** Intermediate

Prevention-based quality builds quality into the process through good design, training, and error-proofing, while inspection-based quality checks output after work is done. Prevention reduces defects earlier and often costs less over time than relying mainly on inspection and rework.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION:
Barcode medication checks prevent errors before giving drugs.
Standard work prevents assembly mistakes rather than catching them later.
Online form validation prevents missing data instead of fixing later.
Schools prevent cheating through design, not only post-exam checks.

**Key concepts:** `prevention`, `inspection`, `quality assurance`, `rework`, `error-proofing`

## Q119. How does Theory of Constraints differ from “local efficiency” thinking?

**Level:** Intermediate

TOC focuses on improving the system’s overall performance by managing the constraint, while local efficiency tries to keep every resource busy. Keeping non-bottleneck resources busy can increase WIP and lead time without improving total throughput.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION:
A factory reduces work release to avoid piles before the bottleneck.
Hospitals avoid overbooking diagnostics if treatment capacity is constrained.
Service centers avoid creating more tickets than resolution teams can handle.

**Key concepts:** `Theory of Constraints`, `local efficiency`, `throughput`, `WIP control`, `system thinking`

## Q120. What does “drum-buffer-rope” mean conceptually in TOC?

REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION:
Factories release work only when the constraint can handle it.
Hospitals pace patient intake based on critical resource availability.
Warehouses pace inbound receiving based on processing capacity.
Public offices pace appointments to match processing limits.

### Operations Strategy Stack
**Purpose:** Turn business strategy into operational choices.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
competitive priorities -> order winners/qualifiers -> process design -> staffing/capacity/inventory/technology
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: priorities - cost, quality, speed, dependability, flexibility.
Component 2: customer signals - qualifiers versus winners.
Component 3: operating design - process, layout, inventory, staffing, technology.
Component 4: performance objectives - what the firm must consistently deliver.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS product team decides that speed and dependability are the order winners for enterprise clients. That means tighter release governance, more capacity cushioning during launches, and less tolerance for "feature first, fix later" behavior.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When operations decisions are being made without a clear market priority.

### Capacity-Flow-Control Map
**Purpose:** Diagnose whether the real problem is capacity, flow, or variability.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
arrival -> WIP -> bottleneck -> lead time -> customer experience
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: utilization - how hard the system is being pushed.
Component 2: bottleneck - the slowest step that governs flow.
Component 3: batching/buffering - the methods used to absorb variability.
Component 4: lead time - the customer-visible delay created by the system.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A support org can have 95% agent utilization and still deliver terrible customer experience because tickets pile up before a specialized approval step. The map shows that the fix is not more hustle; it is bottleneck relief and WIP control.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When people blame delay on "not enough effort" instead of flow design.

### Planning System Ladder
**Purpose:** Show how forecasting and control layers connect.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
forecasting -> S&OP -> aggregate planning -> inventory policy -> MRP -> scheduling -> shop-floor control
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: forecast quality - how accurate the demand signal is.
Component 2: planning horizon - strategic, monthly, weekly, daily.
Component 3: inventory policy - make-to-stock, make-to-order, fixed-order, fixed-time.
Component 4: execution control - priorities, Gantt charts, drum-buffer-rope.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A hardware startup uses S&OP to align product launches, then MRP to place component orders, and then priority rules to handle the assembly bottleneck. The ladder prevents sales from promising what operations cannot ship.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the organization is treating forecasting, procurement, and scheduling as unrelated work.

### Improvement Method Selector
**Purpose:** Pick the right analysis tool for the symptom.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
symptom -> process map / blueprint / VSM / simulation / Six Sigma / TOC
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product operations team uses process mapping when approvals are the problem, simulation when queue behavior is unclear, and Six Sigma when defects dominate. The selector keeps the team from using a hammer when the issue is a bottleneck, not a defect.

## 4. Formulas

No explicit numeric formulas appear in the source. The formulas below are decision heuristics synthesized from the source themes [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Utilization
Formula: `Utilization = demand / capacity`
Variables:
demand = workload entering the system
capacity = total output the process can produce in the same period
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the process has enough room to absorb variability.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.6 -> likely underused -> redeploy capacity or simplify the flow
Value 0.6-0.85 -> healthy zone -> monitor bottlenecks
Value > 0.85 -> queue risk rises -> add cushion or reduce demand peaks
Worked example with numbers: If a help desk receives 180 tickets and can close 200 per day, utilization is 90%. Decision: expect queues and protect peak-hour service.

### Formula 2: Little’s Law
Formula: `WIP = throughput × flow time`
Variables:
WIP = work in process
throughput = completed work per unit time
flow time = time work spends in the system
Why this formula exists: It answers why WIP and lead time rise together.
How to interpret the output:
Value is low -> tight flow -> good for responsiveness
Value is moderate -> normal operating zone -> manage by segment
Value is high -> too much work is stuck -> reduce WIP or remove bottlenecks
Worked example with numbers: If throughput is 50 orders per day and average flow time is 4 days, WIP is about 200 orders. Decision: cut WIP before adding more demand.

### Formula 3: Cost of Quality
Formula: `COQ = prevention + appraisal + internal failure + external failure`
Variables:
prevention = money spent avoiding defects
appraisal = money spent detecting defects
internal failure = cost before the customer sees the problem
external failure = cost after the customer sees the problem
Why this formula exists: It answers whether quality spending is actually reducing total cost.
How to interpret the output:
Prevention too low -> defects leak through -> increase training/design control
Appraisal too high -> inspection is doing too much work -> fix the process
Failure costs high -> urgency to redesign the process
Worked example with numbers: If a plant spends $20k on prevention, $10k on appraisal, $15k on internal failure, and $40k on external failure, total COQ is $85k. Decision: invest more in prevention because external failures are the expensive part.

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

The scenarios below are synthesized applications [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Scenario 1: AI Support Launch
Situation: A SaaS company launches a new AI feature and support tickets triple for two weeks. The team is torn between hiring more agents, changing the workflow, or batching responses.
Applicable framework/metric: Operations Strategy Stack and Capacity-Flow-Control Map.
Analysis: Utilization rises to 92%, but the bottleneck is a specialized approval queue, not the front-line agents. WIP before approval doubles, and lead time jumps from 8 hours to 30 hours.
Decision rule: If utilization is high and the bottleneck is downstream, relieve the constraint first. If wait time is caused by capacity rather than flow, add a temporary cushion.
Action: Add a launch-only approval lane, use a service blueprint to redesign the handoff, and track lead time by step.

### Scenario 2: Manufacturing Mix Shift
Situation: A contract manufacturer shifts from one product family to many low-volume custom variants. Changeovers rise, line balance worsens, and the team wants to know whether a U-shaped cell or a new line layout would help.
Applicable framework/metric: Improvement Method Selector and Planning System Ladder.
Analysis: The symptom is both flow and flexibility. Line balancing can reduce balance delay, and DFMA can reduce the number of parts that trigger long setups. A simulation can test whether the new layout actually shortens total flow time.
Decision rule: If the bottleneck is visible, redesign the flow first. If the impact of layout is uncertain, simulate before spending on a new facility.
Action: Pilot one U-shaped cell, simplify the highest-setup SKU, and compare lead time before and after.

### Scenario 3: Demand Forecast Error and Bullwhip
Situation: A consumer electronics team sees forecast error at retail causing over-ordering upstream. Inventory swells in one month and stockouts happen the next. Sales, planning, and procurement each believe the other function caused the problem.
Applicable framework/metric: Planning System Ladder and Cost of Quality.
Analysis: Forecast error travels into S&OP, then into aggregate planning and MRP, where it becomes excess inventory or missed service. The bullwhip effect is not just a supply-chain issue; it is a planning-control issue.
Decision rule: If forecast error is rising and inventory swings are widening, tighten the forecast process and align the plan across functions. If the problem is mostly local, fix the data flow before adding more inventory.
Action: Rebuild the weekly S&OP review, segment SKUs with ABC logic, and track forecast error versus inventory turns.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Classify the problem first: strategy, capacity, flow, quality, planning, or supply chain.
2. Map the current process end to end, including handoffs, queues, and rework.
3. Measure utilization, WIP, lead time, and defect cost before changing the process.
4. Pick the narrowest tool that fits the symptom: blueprint, VSM, Six Sigma, simulation, or TOC.
5. Connect forecast, procurement, and scheduling through one planning cadence.
6. Review bottlenecks weekly and service priorities monthly.
7. Standardize the fix only after the new metric actually moves.
