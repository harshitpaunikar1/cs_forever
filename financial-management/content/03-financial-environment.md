
# Financial Management & Financial Environment

## Overview

Financial management is how a company plans, gets, and uses money. The “financial environment” is everything around the company—banks, investors, markets, laws, and the economy.

## Why It Matters

A firm can have strong sales but still fail if it runs out of cash or funds growth badly. Understanding the environment helps managers raise money at a good cost and avoid major risks.

## Key Principles

- Focus on long-term value
- Balance risk and return
- Maintain liquidity to pay bills
- Understand markets, rates, and rules

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Financial markets** | Places where money and securities are traded |
| **Financial institutions** | Banks, mutual funds, insurers that move money |
| **Cost of money** | Return investors demand for providing funds |

## Use Case

Choosing between a bank loan, issuing shares, or using retained profits for expansion.

## Scenario

> A growing company needs funds. Interest rates rise, so borrowing becomes costly. It compares funding options and chooses a cheaper mix.

## Examples

- A firm delays debt because rates are high and uses internal cash instead.
- A startup raises equity to avoid heavy monthly loan payments early on.

# Derivatives Overview (Basics)

## Overview

Derivatives are contracts whose value depends on something else—like USD/INR, gold, oil, or interest rates.

## Why It Matters

They help companies protect against price changes. Used well, they reduce uncertainty; used badly, they can increase losses.

## Key Principles

- Use for hedging, not gambling
- Match contract size to real exposure
- Understand worst-case outcomes
- Monitor and update positions

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Derivative** | Value comes from an underlying price |
| **Hedge** | Protection against unwanted price movement |
| **Speculation** | Taking risk to profit from price movement |

## Use Case

An importer hedges USD payments to avoid currency becoming expensive.

## Scenario

> A firm must pay dollars next month. It locks an exchange rate today using a contract, so its rupee cost won’t surprise it.

## Examples

- Airline hedges fuel prices to stabilize ticket pricing.
- Exporter hedges foreign currency receipts to avoid losses if currency falls.

# Financial Statements, Cash Flow & Taxes

## Overview

Financial statements show what a company owns, owes, earns, and spends. Cash flow shows actual cash movement. Taxes reduce what the company keeps.

## Why It Matters

Profit is not the same as cash. Decisions become safer when managers know the real cash situation and tax impact.

## Key Principles

- Separate profit from cash
- Track operating, investing, financing cash flows
- Plan taxes as part of decisions
- Focus on cash available to investors

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Balance sheet** | Assets, liabilities, equity (snapshot) |
| **Income statement** | Revenue minus expenses (performance) |
| **Cash flow statement** | Cash in/out (reality check) |
| **Free cash flow (FCF)** | Cash available after running and investing in the business |

## Use Case

Assessing whether the company can afford a new machine without hurting cash.

## Scenario

> A company shows profit but struggles to pay salaries. Cash flow reveals customers are paying late.

## Examples

- High profit, low cash due to rising receivables.
- Tax planning changes a project’s “after-tax” benefit.

# Financial Statement Analysis (Ratios & DuPont)

## Overview

This is reading financial statements using ratios to understand liquidity, efficiency, debt level, and profitability.

## Why It Matters

Ratios help spot problems early—like too much debt or weak cash position—and compare performance with competitors.

## Key Principles

- Compare across time (trend)
- Compare with peers (benchmark)
- Use a mix of ratios, not one
- Understand limits (accounting choices can distort)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Liquidity ratios** | Can we pay short-term bills? |
| **Profitability ratios** | How much profit from sales/assets? |
| **Leverage ratios** | How much debt risk? |
| **DuPont analysis** | Breaks ROE into drivers (profit margin, efficiency, leverage) |

## Use Case

Bank reviews ratios before approving a loan.

## Scenario

> A retailer’s profits look fine, but its liquidity ratio falls. Analysis shows inventory is piling up.

## Examples

- Current ratio drops → possible cash stress.
- ROE rises mainly due to higher debt → risk increases.

# Time Value of Money (TVM)

## Overview

Money today is worth more than money later because today’s money can grow with interest.

## Why It Matters

TVM is the base of valuation—loans, bonds, projects, and company value all depend on it.

## Key Principles

- Compounding grows money over time
- Discounting converts future money to today
- Rate and time both matter
- Cash timing matters as much as cash amount

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **PV** | Present value (today) |
| **FV** | Future value (later) |
| **Annuity** | Equal payments over time |
| **Perpetuity** | Payments forever |
| **Continuous compounding** | Interest added constantly (conceptually) |

## Use Case

Comparing “₹1 lakh now” vs “₹1.2 lakh in 3 years”.

## Scenario

> A person chooses between two job offers with different bonus timings and uses TVM to compare fairly.

## Examples

- ₹1,000 at 10% becomes ₹1,100 in 1 year.
- ₹1,100 next year at 10% discount equals ₹1,000 today.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Framework 1: Three-Statement Walkthrough
**Purpose:** Read a company's financials in under 10 minutes; find top-3 red flags.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 Step 1: INCOME STATEMENT (10 min on P&L)
   - Revenue growth YoY? Sustainable?
   - Gross margin trend?
   - Operating margin trend?
   - Below-line surprises (interest, taxes, one-offs)?
   - Net income / EPS growth?

 Step 2: BALANCE SHEET
   - Current ratio; quick ratio (liquidity)
   - Debt / Equity (leverage)
   - AR aging (receivables health)
   - Inventory growth vs sales growth
   - Goodwill + intangibles (M&A overhang)
   - Cash + short-term investments (runway)

 Step 3: CASH FLOW STATEMENT
   - CFO positive + growing?
   - CapEx as % of revenue?
   - FCF = CFO − CapEx → how much goes to investors?
   - Financing activities: issuing equity, debt, buybacks, dividends?

 Red flags to look for:
   • Growing AR > growing revenue (channel stuffing risk)
   • Net income up but CFO flat (earnings quality issue)
   • Current ratio declining trend
   • Leverage ratio rising sharply
   • FCF negative with no clear growth story
```

Components:
- 3 statements, linked
- Cash is truth; earnings can be decorated

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** Scanning a SaaS competitor's 10-K: Revenue +40% YoY, great. CFO flat → earnings quality issue. Deferred revenue up 50% but AR also up 60% → some customers paying late. FCF negative, but explained by heavy R&D. Conclusion: growth real but operating discipline slipping; watch AR.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Competitor analysis; M&A diligence; investor briefings.

### Framework 2: DuPont ROE Decomposition
**Purpose:** Break ROE into profitability × efficiency × leverage — reveals the driver.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 3-Factor DuPont:
   ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
         (Net Inc / Rev) × (Rev / Assets) × (Assets / Equity)

 Interpretation:
   High ROE from high Net Margin → differentiation
   High ROE from high Asset Turnover → operational efficiency
   High ROE from high Leverage → risk (more debt)

 5-Factor DuPont (extended):
   ROE = Operating Margin × Asset Turnover × Interest Burden
         × Tax Burden × Equity Multiplier
```

Components:
- ROE = Margin × Turnover × Leverage (basic)
- Leverage-driven ROE rise = hidden risk

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** Two SaaS companies both ROE 20%:
- Company A: Net Margin 25%, Asset Turnover 0.8, Leverage 1 → high-margin differentiator
- Company B: Net Margin 5%, Asset Turnover 0.4, Leverage 10 → highly levered, low-margin

Same ROE; vastly different risk. B's 20% ROE is leverage-driven; recession exposure is huge.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Equity analysis; competitive benchmarking; board reviews.

### Framework 3: Ratio Review Matrix
**Purpose:** Cover all 4 dimensions with min set of ratios.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 Dimension       │ Key Ratios                       │ Healthy
 ──────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┼─────────────
 Liquidity       │ Current; Quick                    │ > 1.5; > 1
                 │ Cash Ratio (Cash / CL)            │ > 0.2

                 │ Operating Margin                   │
                 │ Net Margin                        │
                 │ ROA (NI / Assets)                 │ > 5% SaaS
                 │ ROE (NI / Equity)                  │ > 15%

                 │ Interest Coverage                  │ > 3

```

Components:
- 10 core ratios across 4 dimensions
- Industry-relative; trend matters

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** Audit of a target company: Gross 75% (excellent SaaS), Operating 2% (bad — spending too much), Debt/Equity 0.2 (healthy), DSO 70 (too long). Diagnosis: good product, operational inefficiency + working capital issue.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Investment committee; pre-M&A; due diligence.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Free Cash Flow
**Formula:** `FCF = Operating Cash Flow (CFO) − CapEx`

Variants:
- `FCFF = EBIT × (1 − Tax) + D&A − CapEx − ΔWC` (to firm)
- `FCFE = FCFF − Interest × (1 − Tax) + Net Borrowing` (to equity)

**Why this formula exists:** FCF is the valuation-relevant cash; superior to net income.

**How to interpret the output:**
- Positive + growing → healthy
- Negative but explained by growth → acceptable early-stage
- Negative + mature company → concerning

**Worked example:** CFO $50M; CapEx $20M → FCF $30M. Stock trades at $600M market cap → FCF yield = 5%. Reasonable for growing SaaS.

**Data source:** CFS + investor filings.

---

### Formula 2: 3-Factor DuPont
**Formula:** `ROE = (Net Income / Revenue) × (Revenue / Total Assets) × (Total Assets / Equity)`

**Worked example:** Company with NI $10M, Rev $100M, Assets $80M, Equity $40M.
- Net Margin = 10%; Asset Turnover = 1.25; Equity Multiplier = 2.0
- ROE = 10% × 1.25 × 2.0 = **25%**

Composition reveals leverage driving half of ROE.

**Data source:** IS + BS.

---

### Formula 3: Interest Coverage Ratio
**Formula:** `Interest Coverage = EBIT / Interest Expense`

**Why this formula exists:** Debt-service capacity.

**How to interpret the output:**
- > 5 → strong
- 3–5 → adequate
- < 3 → risk
- < 1.5 → distress

**Worked example:** EBIT $30M; Interest $8M → Coverage 3.75 → adequate but not comfortable.

**Data source:** IS.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Look at net income alone | Net income + FCF together |
| Use single ratio | Use 4-dimension ratio set |
| Compare ratios without industry context | Industry benchmarks mandatory |
| Ignore trend | Multi-year trend + YoY |
| Assume leverage-driven ROE is good | DuPont decompose; watch risk |
| Skip cash flow statement | CFS is reality check |
| Forget deferred taxes / revenue | Understand non-cash items |
| Treat financial statements as audit-only | Managers reading FS catch issues early |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: AI SaaS Pre-Acquisition Diligence
**Situation:** PE firm evaluating a $100M-ARR AI SaaS for acquisition.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Three-Statement Walkthrough + Ratios + DuPont.

**Analysis:**
- Revenue growth 35% YoY (good); Gross margin 72% (good SaaS)
- Operating margin −15% (burn); FCF −$15M (burn)
- AR up 60% vs revenue up 35% → collection issue
- ROE not meaningful; run on burn multiple (see `product-management-npd/10`)

**Decision rule:** SaaS acquisition OK if burn multiple < 2, gross margin > 70%, retention > 110%.

**Action (Monday morning):** Deep dive on AR aging; verify deferred revenue quality; model exit on $ mature EBITDA.

---

### Scenario 2: Consulting Advising on Ratio-Based Loan Underwriting
**Situation:** Bank client's loan covenants include interest-coverage > 3 and D/E < 1.2.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Ratio Review + Covenant Monitoring.

**Analysis:**
- Quarterly check; auto-alert if ratios drift
- Bank adds DuPont analysis to quarterly reviews to catch leverage-driven ROE

**Decision rule:** Covenant breach → restructuring conversation before default.

**Action:** Dashboard setup; quarterly client reviews.

---

### Scenario 3 (Anti-example): High ROE Masking Leverage Risk
**Situation:** Portfolio manager celebrates 22% ROE; recession hits; company defaults.

**Analysis (what goes wrong):**
- DuPont not decomposed
- 22% ROE came from Leverage 8.0 (others normal)
- Recession → EBIT drops; interest still due; bankruptcy

**Cost of this mistake:** Portfolio loss; investor complaint.

**Decision rule:** Any ROE > 20% triggers DuPont decomposition before approval.

**Action:** Mandatory DuPont for equity picks.
