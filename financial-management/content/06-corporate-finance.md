
# Dividends & Share Repurchases

## Overview

Companies return cash to shareholders either by paying dividends or buying back shares.

## Why It Matters

Distribution choices affect investor expectations, cash availability, and sometimes share price signals.

## Key Principles

- Pay only what company can sustain
- Consider growth needs first
- Repurchases are flexible; dividends are expected regularly
- Distributions should fit long-term strategy

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Dividend** | Cash paid per share |
| **Repurchase** | Company buys back its shares |
| **Payout policy** | Plan for returning cash |

## Use Case

Choosing between paying dividends or buying back shares when cash is high.

## Scenario

> A firm has extra cash but also future expansion plans. It does a partial buyback instead of raising dividends permanently.

## Examples

- Mature firms often pay steady dividends.
- Firms with uncertain cash flows prefer buybacks.

# Capital Structure Decisions (Debt vs Equity)

## Overview

Capital structure is how a company funds itself using debt and equity.

## Why It Matters

More debt can increase returns but also increases bankruptcy risk. Finding a sensible mix can increase firm value.

## Key Principles

- Debt is cheaper but adds fixed obligations
- Too much debt increases financial distress risk
- Tax benefits of debt matter
- Match funding with business stability

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Leverage** | Using debt to increase potential returns |
| **Financial risk** | Risk from fixed interest payments |
| **MM theory** | Ideas about debt-equity and firm value (with/without taxes) |

## Use Case

Deciding whether to issue bonds or new shares.

## Scenario

> A stable utility firm can safely use more debt than a volatile startup.

## Examples

- High leverage boosts ROE in good years.
- High leverage causes distress when sales fall.

# Working Capital & Supply Chain Finance

## Overview

Working capital management is managing inventory, receivables, payables, and cash so daily operations run smoothly.

## Why It Matters

It reduces cash shortages and improves profitability by freeing “stuck cash” from operations.

## Key Principles

- Reduce cash conversion cycle
- Manage inventory smartly
- Speed up collections
- Use supplier credit wisely

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Cash conversion cycle** | Time from paying suppliers to collecting from customers |
| **Trade credit** | Supplier allows delayed payment |
| **Supply chain finance** | Financing that improves payments across buyer-supplier chain |

## Use Case

Using invoice financing to get cash earlier without waiting for customers.

## Scenario

> A company sells on 60-day credit. It uses supply chain finance so it receives cash earlier and pays suppliers on time.

## Examples

- Faster billing reduces receivable days.
- Better inventory planning reduces overstock cash lock-up.

# Multinational Financial Management

## Overview

This is managing money across countries—especially exchange rates, global funding, and international investment decisions.

## Why It Matters

Currency changes can turn profits into losses. Global rules and risks make planning more complex.

## Key Principles

- Understand FX exposure (cash flows in foreign currencies)
- Consider inflation differences
- Use hedging when needed
- Evaluate projects with country risk

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Exchange rate** | Price of one currency in another |
| **PPP** | Purchasing power parity (long-run price idea) |
| **IRP** | Interest rate parity (rates and FX link) |
| **FX risk** | Risk from currency movement |

## Use Case

An Indian company exporting to the US hedges USD receipts.

## Scenario

> USD weakens; export revenue in INR drops. Hedging reduces the hit.

## Examples

- Borrowing in the same currency as revenues reduces FX mismatch.
- A project in a risky country uses higher discount rate.

# Public & Private Financing (IPO/SEO, Banks, Rights)

## Overview

Firms raise funds privately (banks, private investors) or publicly (IPO/SEO) often with help from investment banks.

## Why It Matters

Choosing the right financing affects ownership, cost, flexibility, and reputation.

## Key Principles

- Match financing type to company stage
- Public issues require disclosure and compliance
- Timing and market conditions matter
- Consider dilution (ownership reduction)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **IPO** | First time selling shares to public |
| **SEO** | Selling more shares after IPO |
| **Investment bank** | Helps raise funds and price issues |
| **Rights issue** | Offer to existing shareholders to buy new shares |

## Use Case

Deciding whether to go public to fund expansion.

## Scenario

> A fast-growing firm needs big funds. It chooses IPO to raise capital and improve visibility.

## Examples

- Private placement is faster but may be expensive.
- Rights issue raises equity while giving existing owners first chance.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Framework 1: Distribution Policy Decision
**Purpose:** Choose dividend vs buyback based on signalling, tax, and flexibility.

**Text Diagram:**
```
                        CASH SURPLUS STABILITY
                 STABLE                    VOLATILE / ONE-OFF
             ┌──────────────────────┬──────────────────────┐
 COMPANY     │                      │                      │
 STAGE       │  DIVIDENDS            │  BUYBACKS            │
 MATURE      │  (stable payout;     │  (buy when           │
              │   expected by       │   price weak; no     │
              │   income investors)  │   permanent          │
              │                      │   commitment)        │
              ├──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┤
 GROWTH      │                      │                      │
              │  NO PAYOUT           │  SELECTIVE BUYBACKS  │
              │  (reinvest cash)     │  (return excess      │
              │                      │   cash flexibly)     │
              └──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┘

 Tax consideration:
   Dividends: often higher tax in receiver's hand
   Buybacks:  taxed as capital gain
```

Components:
- Stage × cash-flow stability
- Signalling implications

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** Mature SaaS with consistent cash flow: $30M excess cash. Option A: raise dividend $3 → $4. Option B: $500M buyback over 2 years. Dividend signals confidence but locks in permanent cash outflow; buyback is flexible. Choose buyback + small dividend increase.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Board treasury decisions; investor-relations strategy.

### Framework 2: Optimal Capital Structure (Trade-off)
**Purpose:** Find D/E minimising WACC.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 WACC
  ▲
  │                            ┌── bankruptcy cost
  │                           /    dominates
  │                          /
  │                         /
  │                        / ← Optimal D/E
  │               ________/
  │              /        \
  │             / tax shield
  │            /  dominates
  │           /
  │__________/
  └─────────────────────────────► Debt / Equity

  Optimal: where marginal tax shield = marginal distress cost
  Industry-dependent:
    - Utilities / stable: higher D/E (30-60%)
    - Tech / volatile: lower D/E (<20%)
    - PE / LBO: very high D/E (70-90%, short-term)
```

Components:
- Trade-off theory
- Industry benchmarks
- Stability of cash flows → higher leverage capacity

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** AI SaaS with volatile cash flows: target D/E 15% (conservative). Utility with stable CFs: target D/E 50%. Same firm can't use same target.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Capital-structure reviews; re-financing decisions.

### Framework 3: FX Exposure Management
**Purpose:** Identify + manage 3 types of FX risk.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 Type                     │ Example                         │ Horizon       │ Hedge approach
 ────────────────────── ─┼─────────────────────────────── ─┼─────────────┼─────────────────
 TRANSACTIONAL           │ USD invoice due in 60 days      │ Short         │ Forwards / options
 TRANSLATION             │ USD subsidiary in INR books     │ Reporting     │ Usually don't hedge
 ECONOMIC                 │ INR weakens → US competitors     │ Long-term      │ Operational (match
                         │ undercut Indian exporter         │                 │  revenue + cost cur)
```

Components:
- Different exposures warrant different responses
- Operational/natural hedge often best long-term

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** Indian SaaS: transaction exposure on USD receivables (hedge with forwards); translation on US sub's INR-equivalent earnings (don't hedge); economic exposure to rupee volatility (match USD debt to USD revenue as natural hedge).

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** MNC treasury reviews; cross-border expansion.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: MM Propositions (Simplified)
**Without taxes (MM I):** `V_levered = V_unlevered` (capital structure irrelevant)
**With taxes (MM II):** `V_levered = V_unlevered + (Tax × Debt)`

**Worked example:** Unlevered firm value $100M. Add $30M debt at 30% tax. Levered value = 100 + 0.3 × 30 = $109M.

Caveat: Real world has bankruptcy costs → trade-off theory.

**Data source:** Valuation model.

---

### Formula 2: Interest Coverage Capacity (for Debt Sizing)
**Formula:** `Max Debt ≈ EBITDA / (Target Interest Coverage × Interest Rate)`

**Worked example:** EBITDA $20M; target coverage 4×; rate 7%. Max Debt ≈ 20 / (4 × 0.07) = **$71M**.

**Data source:** EBITDA forecast + current rates; target coverage from credit rating target.

---

### Formula 3: PPP + IRP
**PPP:** Spot_{t+1} / Spot_t = (1 + infl_home) / (1 + infl_foreign)
**IRP (Covered):** Forward / Spot = (1 + r_home) / (1 + r_foreign)

**Worked example:** INR inflation 5%; USD inflation 2%. Next year's expected USD/INR from spot 83: 83 × (1.05/1.02) = **85.4**.

**Data source:** IMF + central-bank data.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Cut dividend unexpectedly | Communicate + reduce gradually (if needed) |
| Over-lever in volatile industry | Match leverage to cash-flow stability |
| Ignore FX exposure on cross-border deals | Identify 3 types; hedge transactional; natural-hedge economic |
| Do IPO in poor market conditions | Timing matters; delay if multiples weak |
| Skip credit-rating maintenance | Rating affects all future debt cost |
| Default to debt because it's cheap | Consider distress probability + covenants |
| Forget buyback vs dividend tax trade-off | Structure based on shareholder tax context |
| Price IPO without underwriter input | Use investment banks for pricing + demand-building |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: SaaS Deciding on First Buyback
**Situation:** Mature SaaS with $500M market cap; $50M excess cash.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Distribution Policy + Payout Capacity.

**Analysis:**
- Reinvestment options exhausted at current ROIC
- Option 1: $50M buyback over 18 months (reduces share count ~10%)
- Option 2: $2/share dividend (creates expected future commitment)
- Decision: buyback preferred for flexibility + EPS accretion

**Decision rule:** For growth-stage with volatile cash, buyback > dividend.

**Action (Monday morning):** Announce buyback programme; time purchases at weak-price windows.

---

### Scenario 2: Indian AI Firm Managing USD Exposure
**Situation:** Indian AI company: 70% revenue USD; INR costs. Planning US debt issuance.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Capital Structure + FX Exposure.

**Analysis:**
- USD debt = natural hedge (USD revenue services USD debt)
- Rate arbitrage: USD rates lower than INR
- Tax consideration: interest deductible in India at spot rate

**Decision rule:** For material USD revenue, consider USD-denominated debt.

**Action:** Issue USD bonds; reduces FX exposure + achieves rate arbitrage.

---

### Scenario 3 (Anti-example): Over-Leverage in Cyclical Business
**Situation:** Construction firm takes D/E 3:1 during boom; industry crashes; can't service debt.

**Analysis (what goes wrong):**
- Leverage amplified upside in boom
- Cash flows disappeared in downturn
- Fixed interest obligations unchanged
- Bankruptcy

**Cost of this mistake:** Equity wiped out; jobs lost.

**Decision rule:** Cyclical industries should target below-industry-average leverage.

**Action:** Counter-cyclical capital structure policy; build cash cushion in boom times.
