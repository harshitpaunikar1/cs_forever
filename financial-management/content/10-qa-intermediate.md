## Q72. Why can a fast-growing company face cash shortages even if it is profitable?

**Level:** Intermediate

A fast-growing company may need to buy more inventory, hire staff, and offer more customer credit, which increases cash outflows before cash inflows arrive. Profit is recorded when sales occur, but cash may be collected later, creating a timing gap. Growth can therefore increase working capital needs, making cash tight even when reported profits are positive.

**Real-life applications:**

- A retailer grows sales but must stock more goods upfront.
- A B2B firm expands but waits 60 days for customer payments.
- Companies use credit lines to cover growth-related cash gaps.
- Cash forecasting helps prevent growth from turning into a liquidity crisis.

**Key concepts:** `Growth` `Working capital` `Cash flow timing` `Receivables` `Liquidity risk`

## Q73. How does the cash conversion cycle influence the need for short-term borrowing?

**Level:** Intermediate

The cash conversion cycle measures how long cash is tied up from paying suppliers until collecting from customers. If the cycle is long, the firm must finance that waiting period, often through short-term borrowing. Shortening the cycle by selling inventory faster, collecting receivables sooner, or negotiating longer supplier credit reduces borrowing needs and interest costs.

**Real-life applications:**

- A manufacturer borrows to cover payroll while waiting for customer payments.
- Firms improve billing and collections to reduce loan dependence.
- Retailers use faster inventory turnover to free cash.
- Supplier negotiations can reduce the need for expensive overdrafts.

**Key concepts:** `Cash conversion cycle` `Short-term financing` `Inventory turnover` `Receivables days` `Payables management`

## Q74. How do liquidity ratios and solvency ratios measure different financial risks?

**Level:** Intermediate

Liquidity ratios measure a firm’s ability to meet short-term obligations using current assets, focusing on near-term cash safety. Solvency ratios evaluate long-term financial stability by showing how much the company relies on debt compared to equity and its ability to handle long-term obligations. A firm can be liquid but not solvent, or solvent but temporarily illiquid.

**Real-life applications:**

- A company with heavy long-term debt may be solvent riskier even if it has cash today.
- A profitable firm may be solvent but face a short-term liquidity crunch.
- Banks check liquidity for working capital loans and solvency for long-term loans.
- Investors use solvency to judge bankruptcy risk over time.

**Key concepts:** `Liquidity ratios` `Solvency ratios` `Short-term risk` `Long-term risk` `Leverage`

## Q75. How does the DuPont framework help explain changes in ROE?

**Level:** Intermediate

The DuPont framework breaks return on equity (ROE) into drivers such as profit margin, asset efficiency, and financial leverage. It helps managers see whether ROE improved because the firm became more profitable, used assets more efficiently, or took on more debt. This improves decision-making because some ROE increases are healthy while others may increase risk.

**Real-life applications:**

- A firm discovers ROE rose mainly due to higher debt, not better operations.
- Managers focus on improving margins rather than increasing leverage.
- Investors use DuPont to compare business models across industries.
- Boards use it to understand performance drivers before approving strategy shifts.

**Key concepts:** `DuPont analysis` `ROE drivers` `Profit margin` `Asset turnover` `Financial leverage`

## Q76. Why is free cash flow often more informative than net income for valuation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Free cash flow focuses on actual cash available after operating needs and necessary investments, while net income includes non-cash items and accounting choices. Valuation depends on cash that can be distributed to investors over time. A company may show high net income but weak free cash flow if it requires heavy reinvestment or has slow collections.

**Real-life applications:**

- Investors avoid firms that report profits but struggle to generate cash.
- Companies use free cash flow to decide dividends and buybacks.
- Lenders prefer borrowers with stable operating cash flows.
- Managers track free cash flow to fund expansion without new debt.

**Key concepts:** `Free cash flow` `Net income` `Valuation` `Non-cash items` `Capital investment`

## Q77. How do depreciation and capital expenditures affect cash flow differently?

**Level:** Intermediate

Depreciation reduces accounting profit but does not directly use cash in the current period, so it is added back in cash flow analysis. Capital expenditures are real cash outflows used to buy or upgrade long-term assets. This difference matters because a company can have low profit due to depreciation but strong cash flow, or strong profit but weak cash flow due to heavy spending on assets.

**Real-life applications:**

- A factory shows lower profits due to depreciation but still generates cash.
- A growing company spends heavily on equipment, reducing cash despite profits.
- Analysts separate accounting charges from cash investment needs.
- Budgeting decisions focus on cash impact of new asset purchases.

**Key concepts:** `Depreciation` `Capital expenditures` `Non-cash expense` `Investing cash flow` `Free cash flow`

## Q78. What role do taxes play in comparing debt financing and equity financing?

**Level:** Intermediate

Taxes matter because interest on debt is often tax-deductible, reducing the effective cost of borrowing. Equity returns, such as dividends, are usually not deductible for the company. This creates a tax advantage for debt, but excessive debt increases financial risk, so companies balance tax benefits against potential distress costs.

**Real-life applications:**

- A firm uses moderate debt to benefit from tax savings on interest.
- High-debt companies may face trouble during sales declines despite tax benefits.
- CFOs compare after-tax borrowing costs when choosing financing options.
- Policy changes to tax rules can shift corporate financing behavior.

**Key concepts:** `Tax shield` `Debt financing` `Equity financing` `After-tax cost` `Financial risk`

## Q79. Why can payback period be misleading as a project decision tool?

**Level:** Intermediate

Payback period focuses only on how quickly the initial investment is recovered and ignores cash flows after payback. It also does not properly account for the time value of money unless modified. As a result, a project with a fast payback but low long-term benefits could be chosen over a slower-payback project that creates much more value.

**Real-life applications:**

- A firm may reject a valuable long-life project because it pays back slowly.
- Managers may prefer quick-payback projects to reduce perceived risk.
- Capital budgeting teams use NPV to capture long-term value better.
- Public projects may need long horizons that payback cannot judge fairly.

**Key concepts:** `Payback period` `Time value of money` `Long-term value` `Project screening` `Decision bias`

## Q80. How do NPV and IRR sometimes lead to different project rankings?

**Level:** Intermediate

NPV measures value added in currency terms, while IRR measures a percentage return. When projects differ in size, timing of cash flows, or have unusual patterns, IRR can rank projects differently than NPV. NPV is generally preferred for maximizing value, while IRR is useful for communicating returns but can be misleading in some comparisons.

**Real-life applications:**

- A large project may create more total value even with a lower IRR.
- Managers compare both metrics to avoid wrong conclusions.
- Investment committees prioritize NPV to align with shareholder value goals.
- Firms use additional checks when IRR and NPV conflict.

**Key concepts:** `NPV` `IRR` `Project ranking` `Scale differences` `Cash flow timing`

## Q81. How does the cost of capital act as a decision “hurdle” in project selection?

**Level:** Intermediate

The cost of capital represents the minimum return required by investors given the company’s risk. It acts as a hurdle because projects should generate returns at least equal to this cost to avoid reducing firm value. Using a hurdle rate helps managers filter out investments that do not compensate for risk and opportunity cost.

**Real-life applications:**

- A firm rejects projects that cannot meet investor expectations.
- Banks and investors use similar required-return ideas in funding decisions.
- Companies adjust hurdle rates for riskier business units.
- Public agencies use discount rates to compare long-term benefits and costs.

**Key concepts:** `Cost of capital` `Hurdle rate` `Required return` `Opportunity cost` `Value creation`

## Q84. What is the practical difference between operating cash flow and investing cash flow?

**Level:** Intermediate

Operating cash flow comes from the company’s main business activities, such as collecting from customers and paying suppliers. Investing cash flow relates to buying or selling long-term assets like equipment, buildings, or investments. A healthy business generally aims for positive operating cash flow, while investing cash flow is often negative during growth periods.

**Real-life applications:**

- A firm funds equipment purchases using cash generated from operations.
- A growing company shows strong sales but negative investing cash flow due to expansion.
- Investors check if negative investing cash flow is strategic or wasteful.
- Public projects may show negative investing flows during construction phases.

**Key concepts:** `Operating cash flow` `Investing cash flow` `Core operations` `Capital investment` `Cash flow categories`

## Q88. Why do bond prices generally fall when market interest rates rise?

**Level:** Intermediate

When market interest rates rise, new bonds are issued with higher coupons or yields, making older bonds with lower coupons less attractive. To compete, the price of existing bonds falls so that their overall yield matches the new market rate. This inverse relationship between rates and bond prices is a key principle of fixed income investing.

**Real-life applications:**

- Investors holding bonds may face paper losses when rates rise.
- Banks manage bond portfolios to reduce exposure before rate hikes.
- Companies may prefer borrowing before rates increase.
- Retirees relying on bond funds may see portfolio values fluctuate with rates.

**Key concepts:** `Bond pricing` `Interest rate changes` `Yield adjustment` `Market rates` `Fixed income valuation`

## Q89. How does diversification reduce unsystematic risk but not systematic risk?

**Level:** Intermediate

Unsystematic risk is specific to a company or industry, such as a product failure or management issue, and diversification reduces it by spreading investments across many different firms. Systematic risk affects the entire market, such as recessions or interest rate shocks, and cannot be eliminated by diversification. Investors are usually compensated mainly for systematic risk.

**Real-life applications:**

- An investor avoids heavy loss from one company by holding many stocks.
- A market crash still affects diversified portfolios to some extent.
- Funds diversify holdings to reduce company-specific surprises.
- Corporate planners diversify product lines but still face economy-wide downturns.

**Key concepts:** `Diversification` `Unsystematic risk` `Systematic risk` `Market risk` `Portfolio theory`

## Q90. What does beta measure and how is it used in corporate finance decisions?

**Level:** Intermediate

Beta measures how sensitive a stock’s returns are to movements in the overall market. A higher beta indicates higher market risk. In corporate finance, beta is used to estimate the required return on equity, which then affects the company’s cost of capital and project evaluation decisions.

**Real-life applications:**

- A firm uses beta to estimate investor-required returns for equity funding.
- Riskier businesses typically have higher betas and higher cost of equity.
- Project discount rates may be adjusted if a project is riskier than the firm average.
- Analysts compare betas to understand sector risk differences.

**Key concepts:** `Beta` `Market risk` `Required return` `Cost of equity` `CAPM concept`

## Q92. How can behavioral biases create mispricing even when markets are generally efficient?

- Social media hype can push prices above reasonable levels.

- Managers may time buybacks when they believe prices are irrationally low.

## Q93. How do the free cash flow model and dividend growth model differ in valuing equity?

Using both can provide a cross-check, but the choice depends on business stage and payout behavior.

**Real-life applications:**

- Mature firms with steady payouts are often valued using dividend-based thinking.

## Q95. How do options provide “asymmetric” protection compared to futures?

**Level:** Intermediate

Options provide the right but not the obligation to transact at a fixed price, which means the buyer can limit downside while keeping upside potential. Futures create an obligation, so both gains and losses can occur as prices move. This asymmetry makes options useful when a firm wants insurance-like protection with flexibility, though it must pay a premium.

**Real-life applications:**

- An exporter buys a currency option to protect against unfavorable moves while benefiting if rates move favorably.
- A business uses futures to lock costs but gives up upside if prices fall.
- Risk managers choose options when uncertainty is high and flexibility matters.
- Option premiums are treated like an insurance cost in budgeting.

**Key concepts:** `Options` `Futures` `Asymmetric payoff` `Hedging` `Premium`

## Q101. How does scenario analysis differ from sensitivity analysis?

**Level:** Intermediate

Sensitivity analysis changes one variable at a time to see its impact, while scenario analysis considers multiple variables changing together to represent realistic situations like best-case, base-case, and worst-case outcomes. Scenario analysis provides a more complete view of how combined changes affect results, helping managers plan for uncertainty.

**Real-life applications:**

- A firm models a recession scenario with lower sales and tighter credit.
- Managers plan contingency actions if the worst-case scenario occurs.
- Scenario analysis helps boards understand downside risk before approval.
- Governments use scenarios for budgeting under uncertain revenue projections.

**Key concepts:** `Scenario analysis` `Sensitivity analysis` `Best/base/worst case` `Uncertainty planning` `Risk assessment`

## Q102. Why is the concept of “incremental cash flows” important in project evaluation?

Incremental cash flows are the additional cash flows that occur because a project is undertaken, compared to not doing it. - A company ignores sunk costs when evaluating whether to continue a project.

- Managers avoid overstating value by excluding unrelated overhead allocations.
- This helps make fair comparisons between alternative project options.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Cash Conversion Loop
**Purpose:** Show how operating decisions convert into cash pressure or cash release.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Inventory -> Receivables -> Cash in
   |            |
 Payables      collections
   \___________/
    Cash conversion cycle
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Inventory: cash tied up in stock.
Receivables: cash tied up after sale.
Payables: cash the firm delays paying.
CCC: the operating cash gap the firm must finance.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A B2B software company with long enterprise payment terms may show strong bookings but still need a credit line because receivables are funding the customer, not the business.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When growth is fast but the bank balance is falling.

### Capital Structure Ladder
**Purpose:** Decide how much debt the firm can safely carry.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Tax shield -> lower after-tax cost
    |
Debt adds covenant and distress risk
    |
Target capital structure -> WACC trade-off
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Debt cost: after-tax borrowing cost.

## 4. Formulas
The source is mostly conceptual, so the formulas below are model-knowledge heuristics that make the chapter's decision logic usable. `[verified from model knowledge, not source]`

### Formula 1: Cash Conversion Cycle `[verified from model knowledge, not source]`
Formula: `CCC = DIO + DSO - DPO`
Variables:
`DIO` = days inventory outstanding
`DSO` = days sales outstanding
`DPO` = days payables outstanding
Why this formula exists: It shows how long cash is trapped in operations.
How to interpret the output:
Value falls -> liquidity improves -> less short-term borrowing
Value rises -> cash is trapped longer -> tighten inventory, collection, or supplier terms
Worked example with numbers: DIO 40 + DSO 50 - DPO 30 = 60 days. Decision: the firm must finance about two months of operating cash gap.

### Formula 2: WACC `[verified from model knowledge, not source]`
Formula: `WACC = (E/V) * Re + (D/V) * Rd * (1 - T)`
Variables:
`E` = market value of equity
`D` = market value of debt
`V` = E + D
`Re` = cost of equity
`Rd` = cost of debt
`T` = tax rate
Why this formula exists: It gives the hurdle rate for capital budgeting.
How to interpret the output:
Lower WACC -> value creation becomes easier
Higher WACC -> projects need stronger cash returns
Worked example with numbers: E/V 60%, D/V 40%, Re 14%, Rd 8%, tax 25% gives WACC 10.4%. Decision: only projects that can clear roughly 10.4% should be funded.

### Formula 3: NPV `[verified from model knowledge, not source]`
Formula: `NPV = sum(CF_t / (1 + r)^t) - initial_investment`
Variables:
`CF_t` = cash flow at time t
`r` = discount rate or WACC
`initial_investment` = upfront cash outlay
Why this formula exists: It measures whether an investment adds value in today's money.
How to interpret the output:
NPV < 0 -> destroy value -> reject
NPV = 0 -> break-even -> only proceed for strategic reasons
NPV > 0 -> add value -> consider funding
Worked example with numbers: A project costs $1.0M and returns discounted cash flows worth $1.3M, so NPV = $300k. Decision: fund if the risk assumptions are credible.

### Formula 4: DuPont ROE Decomposition `[verified from model knowledge, not source]`
Formula: `ROE = Net income / Equity = Profit margin * Asset turnover * Financial leverage`
Variables:
`Profit margin` = net income / sales
`Asset turnover` = sales / assets
`Financial leverage` = assets / equity
Why this formula exists: It shows whether ROE improved because of operations, asset use, or debt.
How to interpret the output:
Margin-driven -> operational improvement
Turnover-driven -> asset efficiency improvement
Leverage-driven -> higher risk; check debt quality
Worked example with numbers: Margin 8% * turnover 1.5 * leverage 2.0 = 24% ROE. Decision: verify that leverage is not the only driver.

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Fast-Growing B2B Company With a Cash Gap
**Situation:** A consulting-tech business is growing revenue 35 percent year over year, but the bank balance is shrinking because enterprise clients pay in 60 days while payroll and cloud costs are due now.
**Applicable framework/metric:** Cash Conversion Loop + CCC.
**Analysis:** DIO 25 + DSO 60 - DPO 20 = 65 days. The firm is effectively financing more than two months of working capital.
**Decision rule:** If CCC rises faster than sales growth, tighten receivables and inventory; if cash buffer falls below one CCC cycle, raise short-term financing.
**Action:** Shorten billing cycles, negotiate better supplier terms, and set a weekly cash forecast dashboard for the CFO.

### Scenario 2: SaaS Firm Choosing Debt or Equity
**Situation:** A stable SaaS company wants to fund an AI product expansion. The CFO is deciding between a debt raise and a secondary equity issue.
**Applicable framework/metric:** Capital Structure Ladder + WACC.
**Analysis:** After-tax debt cost = 6.0%, cost of equity = 14.0%, and the current WACC is 10.4%. Debt would reduce WACC slightly but covenant headroom is thin.
**Decision rule:** If debt lowers WACC without pushing leverage beyond covenant comfort, use it; if leverage materially raises distress risk, preserve flexibility and use equity.
**Action:** Run a covenant sensitivity table, cap leverage, and keep at least one quarter of liquidity buffer.

### Scenario 3: Mature Product Company Choosing Dividends or Buybacks
**Situation:** A mature product company has excess cash, slow growth, and a stable shareholder base. Management is deciding whether to pay a dividend or buy back shares.
**Applicable framework/metric:** Financing and Payout Architecture + signaling.
**Analysis:** Dividend payout would signal recurring cash generation, while buybacks offer flexibility and can lift per-share metrics without committing to a permanent payout. The clientele effect suggests some investors prefer one over the other.
**Decision rule:** If flexibility is strategic, favor buybacks; if stable cash flow and investor signaling are the goal, favor dividends.
**Action:** Model the EPS impact, announce the capital-return logic clearly, and keep retained earnings sufficient for the next expansion window.
