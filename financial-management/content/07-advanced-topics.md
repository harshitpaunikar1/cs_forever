
# Lease Financing (Lease vs Buy)

## Overview

Leasing lets a company use an asset (like equipment) while paying rent-like payments instead of buying it.

## Why It Matters

Leasing can preserve cash and add flexibility, but may cost more over time.

## Key Principles

- Compare lease cost vs loan/buy cost
- Include tax and accounting effects
- Consider flexibility and maintenance
- Think about asset obsolescence risk

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Operating lease** | More like renting |
| **Financial lease** | More like financing a purchase |
| **Lease vs buy analysis** | Comparing total cost |

## Use Case

Leasing vehicles instead of purchasing to avoid large upfront cost.

## Scenario

> A company needs machines that may become outdated fast. Leasing avoids being stuck with old technology.

## Examples

- Airline leases planes to manage huge costs.
- Small firm leases printers to avoid repair responsibility.

# Hybrid Financing (Preferred, Warrants, Convertibles)

## Overview

Hybrid financing mixes features of debt and equity—like preferred stock or convertible bonds.

## Why It Matters

Hybrids can raise funds with different risk and control trade-offs and can be attractive to specific investors.

## Key Principles

- Use hybrids when pure debt/equity is not ideal
- Understand dilution potential
- Price in optionality (conversion/warrants)
- Align with cash flow strength

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Preferred stock** | Fixed dividend-like payments, usually no voting |
| **Warrant** | Right to buy shares at set price later |
| **Convertible** | Debt that can convert into equity |

## Use Case

A company issues convertibles to get lower interest today with possible equity later.

## Scenario

> Investors want safety plus upside. Convertibles give interest now and potential share gains later.

## Examples

- Warrants added to bonds make the bond more attractive.
- Preferred stock helps raise capital without immediate dilution.

# Dynamic Capital Structures & Valuation (APV)

## Overview

If debt levels change over time, valuation must account for changing interest tax benefits and financing effects.

## Why It Matters

Real companies don’t always keep constant debt ratios. APV helps value projects/firms when financing plans change.

## Key Principles

- Separate business value from financing value
- Add tax shield benefits when appropriate
- Include financing side effects (costs, distress risk)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **APV** | Adjusted Present Value (base value + financing effects) |
| **Tax shield** | Tax savings from interest expense |

## Use Case

Valuing a leveraged buyout where debt is high early and falls later.

## Scenario

> A firm borrows heavily for acquisition and plans to repay over 5 years; APV captures that changing debt benefit.

## Examples

- Constant debt ratio → WACC method fits well.
- Changing debt plan → APV can be clearer.

# Mergers & Corporate Control

## Overview

Mergers combine companies. Corporate control deals with who has power to make decisions (friendly or hostile takeovers).

## Why It Matters

M&A can create value through synergies—but can also destroy value if overpaid or poorly integrated.

## Key Principles

- Value the target realistically
- Identify real synergies (cost savings, growth)
- Decide bid price carefully
- Plan integration early

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Synergy** | Extra value from combining firms |
| **Takeover** | One firm buys control of another |
| **Hostile takeover** | Target resists the deal |

## Use Case

Buying a competitor to reduce costs and expand market share.

## Scenario

> A company overpays due to excitement. Later it cannot achieve promised synergies and shareholders suffer.

## Examples

- Cost synergy: shared warehouses reduce expenses.
- Revenue synergy: cross-selling products to combined customers.

# Enterprise Risk Management (ERM)

## Overview

ERM is managing all major risks together—financial, operational, legal, cyber, safety, and strategic.

## Why It Matters

Big losses often come from ignored risks. ERM reduces surprises and protects cash flows and reputation.

## Key Principles

- Identify risks across the company
- Measure likelihood and impact
- Decide responses: avoid, reduce, transfer, accept
- Monitor continuously

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Risk event** | Something that can cause loss |
| **Risk appetite** | How much risk the firm is willing to take |
| **Risk transfer** | Shifting risk via insurance/hedging |

## Use Case

Creating a risk dashboard for top 10 company risks.

## Scenario

> A firm discovers it depends on one supplier. ERM process creates backup suppliers to prevent shutdown risk.

## Examples

- FX hedging reduces currency risk.
- Insurance reduces disaster-related loss.

# Bankruptcy, Reorganization & Liquidation

## Overview

Bankruptcy happens when a firm can’t pay obligations. It may reorganize (try to survive) or liquidate (sell assets and close).

## Why It Matters

Understanding distress helps lenders, investors, and managers make safer decisions and negotiate solutions early.

## Key Principles

- Act early when distress signals appear
- Negotiate restructuring if possible
- Protect essential operations during reorganization
- Liquidation focuses on asset value recovery

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Financial distress** | Trouble meeting payments |
| **Reorganization** | Restructuring to continue business |
| **Liquidation** | Selling assets to pay creditors |

## Use Case

Renegotiating loan terms to avoid shutdown.

## Scenario

> Sales collapse; the firm renegotiates debt, cuts costs, and sells non-core assets to survive.

## Examples

- Debt restructuring lowers interest burden.
- Liquidation sells inventory and property to repay creditors.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Framework 1: Lease vs Buy Decision
**Purpose:** Compare lifetime cost + flexibility.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 Dimension                │ LEASE                     │ BUY
 ──────────────────────┼────────────────────────────┼────────────────
 Upfront cash            │ Low / none                 │ High
 Ownership                │ No                          │ Yes
 Flexibility               │ High (upgrade easily)     │ Low
 Tax treatment             │ Lease payment deductible  │ Depreciation shield
 Obsolescence risk         │ Lessor bears              │ Lessee bears
 Maintenance                │ Often included            │ Lessee responsibility
 Long-term cost            │ Higher (premium)          │ Lower
 Balance-sheet impact       │ Operating: off BS          │ Asset + debt on BS
                            │ Finance: on BS            │
 
 Decision rule:
  - Short usage + high obsolescence → LEASE
  - Long usage + stable asset → BUY
  - Run NPV both ways
```

Components:
- 8-dimension comparison
- NPV decides when close

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** AI company needs GPU compute. Options:
- Lease cloud GPU at $X/hour (high flexibility, pay-per-use)
- Buy GPU cluster for $Y (fixed, own, can sell later)
- Lease wins for volatile workload; buy wins for stable + heavy load.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Hardware / equipment / real-estate decisions.

### Framework 2: Hybrid Instrument Selection
**Purpose:** Pick between convertible, warrant, preferred.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 Instrument         │ When to use                        │ Trade-off
 ─────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────
 Convertible Bond    │ Want lower interest today +       │ Dilution on conversion
                     │ investor upside                    │ 
 Warrant attached    │ Sweeten a debt/equity issue       │ Dilution if exercised
                     │                                    │
 Preferred Stock     │ Raise capital without dilution   │ Fixed dividend
                     │ of common equity                    │ cash outflow
 Mezzanine Debt      │ Subordinated high-yield debt     │ Expensive; flexible
                     │ between senior debt + equity        │
```

Components:
- Optionality pricing
- Dilution considerations
- Cash-flow implications

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** Growth-stage AI startup needs $30M. Options:
- Pure equity: 20% dilution at current valuation
- Convertible note: 5% discount + 20% premium; converts at next round; lower current-day dilution
- Venture debt with warrants: less dilutive; cash-flow covenant required

Choose convertible if next round expected; venture debt if revenue supports coverage.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Series extensions; bridge financing.

### Framework 3: M&A Deal Structure + Defence
**Purpose:** Understand friendly/hostile mechanics + defences.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 FRIENDLY PATH:
   Agreed offer → Due diligence → Signing → Shareholder vote → Close

 HOSTILE PATH:
   Initial offer → Rejected → Tender offer to shareholders
        OR → Proxy fight for board seats
        Target defences activate

 HOSTILE DEFENCES:
   - Poison pill (shareholder rights plan): dilutes attacker
   - Staggered board: slow takeover
   - Golden parachute: makes ousting mgmt expensive
   - White knight: alternative friendly acquirer
   - Crown jewels: sell prized assets to deter
   - Pac-Man: target makes offer for acquirer

 PREMIUM TYPICAL:
   Friendly:  20-30% over market
   Hostile:   30-50% over market
```

Components:
- Friendly path much cheaper; hostile path costly and risky
- Defences are classic corporate-governance topic

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** Large software firm makes hostile bid for competitor. Target activates poison pill; proxy fight ensues; deal eventually closes at 40% premium. PE-firm white knight also circled. Complex 9-month saga.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Board strategy; competitive bid situations.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: APV
**Formula:** `APV = Unlevered NPV + PV(Tax Shields) − PV(Distress Costs)`

**Why this formula exists:** Works when capital structure changes over time (e.g., LBO where debt is paid down).

**Worked example:** LBO target: Unlevered NPV $100M; Tax shields PV $30M; Distress costs PV $10M.
- APV = 100 + 30 − 10 = **$120M**

vs. WACC method which assumes constant D/E.

**Data source:** Project CF forecasts + debt schedule + tax rate.

---

### Formula 2: Lease vs Buy NPV
**Formula:** `NPV(Buy) − NPV(Lease) = ?`

**Worked example:** Asset cost $200k; 5-year life; lease $50k/year; tax 30%.
- Buy: CF₀ −200k; tax savings from depreciation (say $12k/yr × 30%) = $3.6k/yr × 5 yrs PV ≈ $15k; salvage $40k PV
- Lease: CF per year −50k + tax saving 50 × 0.3 = −35k × 5 PV
- Compare NPVs

**Data source:** Vendor quotes + finance's tax + discount-rate assumptions.

---

### Formula 3: Altman Z-Score (Distress Prediction)
**Formula (Public Manufacturing, original):**
`Z = 1.2 × (WC/Assets) + 1.4 × (Retained Earnings/Assets) + 3.3 × (EBIT/Assets) + 0.6 × (Market Cap/Book of Debt) + 1.0 × (Sales/Assets)`

**How to interpret the output:**
- Z > 2.99 → Safe
- 1.81 < Z < 2.99 → Grey zone
- Z < 1.81 → Distress likely within 2 years

**Worked example:** Company with declining ratios; Z = 1.5 → distress-likely zone. Credit team intervenes.

**Data source:** Public financials; modified for private firms (Z' for private, Z'' for non-manufacturer).

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Default to buy for all assets | Lease vs buy NPV analysis |
| Ignore optionality in convertibles | Price the conversion option |
| Use WACC when D/E varies significantly | Use APV for dynamic structures |
| Underestimate hostile-deal defences | Assume premium 30-50% in hostile scenarios |
| Assume ERM = insurance | ERM is holistic (identify, measure, respond, monitor) |
| Wait until bankruptcy to restructure | Restructure early; easier + cheaper |
| Skip Altman Z tracking for distressed positions | Monitor key metrics; set early triggers |
| Treat M&A as financial exercise | 70% fail on integration; align culture, ops, systems |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: AI Startup Choosing Hybrid Financing
**Situation:** Series-B AI startup needs $20M; wants to minimise dilution.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Hybrid Instrument Selection.

**Analysis:**
- Pure equity: 18% dilution at $100M pre-money
- Convertible: 5% discount + 25% cap; current-round dilution minimised
- Venture debt + warrants: less dilutive; needs predictable cash flow

**Decision rule:** Convertible if next round > 12 months away; equity if runway tight.

**Action (Monday morning):** Issue convertible note; next round within 12 months.

---

### Scenario 2: PE Firm Valuing LBO with APV
**Situation:** PE firm valuing a target. Debt $300M at close; paid down to $100M in 5 years.

**Applicable framework/metric:** APV.

**Analysis:**
- Unlevered NPV: $500M (DCF at unlevered cost of equity)
- Tax shield PV: $80M (declining as debt paid down)
- Distress cost PV: $30M (higher in early years)
- APV: 500 + 80 − 30 = **$550M**

WACC method would miss the changing debt ratio; APV captures.

**Decision rule:** Use APV when D/E changes materially across years.

**Action:** Present APV analysis to investment committee; compare to multiples-based exit.

---

### Scenario 3 (Anti-example): Ignoring Distress Signals
**Situation:** Manufacturer's Z-score drifts from 2.8 to 1.9 over 2 quarters; management doesn't act; Q4 Z-score 1.3; bankruptcy filed.

**Analysis (what goes wrong):**
- Distress signals ignored
- Restructuring opportunity window missed
- Bank leverage in forced restructuring worse

**Cost of this mistake:** Equity wiped out; creditors recover 40¢/$.

**Decision rule:** Z-score drop + credit-metric deterioration → immediate restructuring plan.

**Action:** Early engagement with creditors; cost cuts; divestiture of non-core assets.
