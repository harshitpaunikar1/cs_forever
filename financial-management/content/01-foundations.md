
# Goals and Functions of Finance

## Overview

Financial management is about planning, getting, and using money in a smart way so a business can run smoothly and grow.

## Why It Matters

A company can have great products but still fail if it handles money poorly. Good finance decisions help the business stay profitable, survive tough times, and expand confidently.

## Key Principles

- Aim to increase the value of the business
- Balance profit vs. risk
- Ensure enough cash to pay bills on time
- Make decisions based on future impact, not just today

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Financial Management** | Managing money decisions in a business |
| **Profitability** | Ability to earn more than you spend |
| **Liquidity** | Ability to pay short-term bills |
| **Value Maximization** | Making decisions that increase business worth |

## Use Case

Deciding whether to buy new machinery now or keep renting to save cash.

## Scenario

> A small factory earns profits but often misses supplier payments. The finance manager creates a plan to keep enough cash available while still investing in growth.

## Examples

- A retailer chooses to keep extra cash before a festival season to manage high inventory purchases.
- A startup decides not to expand too fast because it may run out of cash in 3 months.

# Financial Planning and Forecasting

## Overview

This means planning future money needs and predicting future results using estimates (like future sales, costs, and profits).

## Why It Matters

Planning helps a company avoid cash shortages and make better decisions. Forecasting gives an early warning if the business may face losses or funding needs.

## Key Principles

- Use realistic assumptions (sales, costs, prices)
- Build plans for different possibilities (best/normal/worst case)
- Regularly compare plan vs. actual results
- Forecast cash, not just profit

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Financial Plan** | A roadmap of money goals and actions |
| **Forecast** | Best estimate of future financial results |
| **Budget** | Detailed plan of income and spending |
| **Projected Financial Statements** | Estimated future income statement, balance sheet, cash flow |

## Use Case

A company forecasts it will need extra cash in 4 months, so it arranges a bank credit line today.

## Scenario

> A cafe owner expects higher sales in winter. She forecasts higher ingredient costs too, and plans extra cash to avoid borrowing at high interest later.

## Examples

- A firm forecasts sales will drop 15%, so it reduces unnecessary expenses early.
- A business projects growth and plans to hire staff gradually, not all at once.

# Working Capital and the Operating/Cash Cycle

## Overview

Working capital is the money used for daily operations—like buying inventory, paying salaries, and collecting from customers.

## Why It Matters

Even profitable businesses can fail if they can’t manage daily cash needs. Working capital keeps the business running without payment delays.

## Key Principles

- Keep enough inventory—but not too much
- Collect customer payments faster
- Pay suppliers smartly (not too early, not too late)
- Shorten the time between paying cash and receiving cash

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Working Capital** | Current assets − current liabilities |
| **Current Assets** | Cash, inventory, receivables |
| **Current Liabilities** | Short-term payables, short-term loans |
| **Operating Cycle** | Time from buying inventory to collecting cash from sales |
| **Cash Cycle** | Time between paying for inventory and collecting cash from customers |

## Use Case

A wholesaler adjusts inventory and credit terms to reduce cash stuck in operations.

## Scenario

> A furniture seller buys wood today, sells furniture next month, and gets paid after 30 days. That long wait creates cash pressure—this is the cash cycle problem.

## Examples

- A grocery store keeps fast-moving stock to avoid money stuck in slow inventory.
- A manufacturer negotiates 45-day supplier credit to match customer payment timing.

# Cash Budgeting and Cash Management

## Overview

Cash budgeting means planning cash in and cash out so the company doesn’t run out of cash.

## Why It Matters

Cash is what pays bills. A business can show “profit” on paper but still fail if cash isn’t available at the right time.

## Key Principles

- Track cash inflows (sales collections, loans)
- Track cash outflows (rent, salaries, suppliers)
- Keep a minimum safety cash balance
- Invest extra cash wisely (not idle)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Cash Budget** | A monthly/weekly plan of cash receipts and payments |
| **Cash Shortage** | When cash available is less than cash needed |
| **Cash Surplus** | Extra cash beyond needs |
| **Float** | Time delay in banking/processing payments |

## Use Case

Planning cash needs before paying annual insurance, taxes, or big supplier bills.

## Scenario

> A company must pay ₹10 lakh in salaries on the 1st. The cash budget shows collections will come on the 5th. So it arranges a short-term loan.

## Examples

- A seasonal business saves surplus cash during peak months to survive off-season months.
- A firm schedules vendor payments in two parts to avoid a single-day cash crunch.

# Managing Receivables and Credit Policy

## Overview

Receivables are money customers owe you. Managing them means setting rules for credit sales and collecting money on time.

## Why It Matters

Too much credit can increase sales but may create late payments and bad debts. Good receivables management improves cash flow and reduces losses.

## Key Principles

- Decide who gets credit and how much
- Set clear payment terms and due dates
- Follow up quickly on late payments
- Balance sales growth with payment risk

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Receivables** | Unpaid customer invoices |
| **Credit Policy** | Rules for offering credit (who, how long, how much) |
| **Credit Period** | Time allowed to pay |
| **Bad Debt** | Amount that may never be collected |
| **Collection Period (DSO)** | Average time to collect payments |

## Use Case

A distributor changes credit terms from “60 days” to “30 days” to reduce cash blockage.

## Scenario

> A mobile shop offers credit to customers. Sales increase, but cash becomes tight. The owner tightens credit checks and offers small discounts for early payment.

## Examples

- “2% discount if paid in 10 days” encourages faster collections.
- Stopping credit to repeat late payers reduces future losses.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Framework 1: Goals Hierarchy (Profit → Value → Sustainability)
**Purpose:** Frame financial goals rigorously.

**Text Diagram:**
```
 LEVEL 3 (mature) │ Stakeholder / ESG value
                   │   Long-term financial + social + environmental
 LEVEL 2 (modern)  │ Enterprise value / NPV maximisation
                   │   Future cash flows discounted
 LEVEL 1 (classical)│ Short-term profit / EPS
                   │   Accounting earnings

 Moving up: more comprehensive + more aligned with long-term health
```

Components:
- Classical profit: narrow, short-term
- NPV / enterprise-value: corporate-finance gold standard
- Stakeholder / ESG: modern evolution

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A SaaS PE-owned company decides to optimise for NPV over 5 years rather than quarterly EPS. Investments in R&D + customer success rise; quarterly margin dips but 5-yr NPV 2.5× projections. PE firm rewarded at exit.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Board strategy; investor communications; incentive design.

---

### Framework 2: Working Capital Cycle + Levers
**Purpose:** Visualise CCC + identify levers.

**Text Diagram:**
```
   Buy inventory   Pay supplier       Sell          Collect cash
        │              │                │                │
        ▼              ▼                ▼                ▼
  ──────T0 ────── T_pay ────────── T_sell ─────── T_collect ──────► Time

  Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO):  T0 → T_sell
  Days Sales Outstanding (DSO):       T_sell → T_collect
  Days Payable Outstanding (DPO):    T0 → T_pay

  Operating Cycle = DIO + DSO
  Cash Cycle (CCC) = DIO + DSO − DPO  (negative for Amazon-like firms)

  Levers to reduce CCC:
   ↓ DIO: faster inventory turnover; JIT; SKU rationalisation
   ↓ DSO: tighter credit, early-pay discounts, factoring, AR automation
   ↑ DPO: negotiate longer terms (without hurting suppliers / SCF)
```

Components:
- 3 levers with explicit direction
- Every day saved = cash freed

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** SaaS company with DIO 0 (no inventory), DSO 55, DPO 30 → CCC 25 days. $40M revenue → $40M × 25/365 = **$2.7M** locked in working capital. Reduce DSO to 40 via billing automation → CCC 10 days → $1.1M freed.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Cash-flow crises; finance transformation; post-IPO scaling.

---

### Framework 3: Receivables Credit-Policy Triangle
**Purpose:** Balance sales growth, bad-debt risk, and cash conversion.

**Text Diagram:**
```
                     SALES
                      /\
                     /  \
                    /    \
                   /      \
          BAD DEBT ───── CASH FLOW
          RISK

  Tight credit  →  less sales, less bad debt, faster cash
  Loose credit  →  more sales, more bad debt, slower cash

  Policy levers:
   - Credit scoring / check
   - Credit period (30 / 60 / 90 days)
   - Early-pay discounts
   - Late-payment penalties
   - Collection process + SLAs
   - Stop-sale thresholds
```

Components:
- Three objectives in tension
- Policy design = deliberate trade-off

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** Distributor with 60-day terms; 8% bad debt. Shift to 30-day terms + 2% early-pay discount. Result: DSO 40 (was 55); bad debt 4%; sales −3% (lost some weak-credit customers). Net: cash-flow positive + healthier book.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Credit-policy reviews; CFO-CRO debates about sales growth vs risk.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Working Capital + Current Ratio + Quick Ratio
**Formula:**
- Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities
- Current Ratio = CA / CL
- Quick Ratio = (CA − Inventory) / CL

**Why this formula exists:** Standard liquidity diagnostics.

**How to interpret the output:**
- Current ratio < 1 → illiquid
- 1.0–1.5 → tight
- 1.5–3 → healthy
- > 3 → possibly idle cash

Quick ratio (excluding inventory) > 1 → can pay short-term without selling stock.

**Worked example:** CA = $5M (including $2M inventory); CL = $3M.
- WC = $2M; Current = 1.67; Quick = (5 − 2)/3 = 1.0 → healthy.

**Data source:** Balance sheet.

---

### Formula 2: Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)
**Formula:** `CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO`

**Variables:** See `business-analytics/11-financial-analytics.md` for full.

**Worked example:** DIO 45, DSO 55, DPO 30 → CCC 70 days. 70 days × $100M revenue / 365 = **$19M cash locked** in operations.

**Data source:** Balance sheet + P&L.

---

### Formula 3: Bad Debt Ratio + AR Turnover
**Formula:**
- Bad Debt Ratio = Uncollectible AR / Total Credit Sales
- AR Turnover = Credit Sales / Average AR

**Why this formula exists:** Measures collection efficiency.

**How to interpret the output:**
- Bad debt > 3% → policy too loose or industry high-risk
- AR turnover 6–12x typical; higher = faster collection

**Worked example:** $30M credit sales; Avg AR $5M; Write-off $900k.
- AR Turnover = 30/5 = 6; DSO ≈ 365/6 = 60 days
- Bad Debt Ratio = 900k/30M = **3%** — borderline; tighten policy.

**Data source:** CRM + finance.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Optimise only for profit | Optimise for NPV / long-term value |
| Forecast only P&L | Forecast cash explicitly; cash ≠ profit |
| Assume single-scenario forecast | Build best/normal/worst; rolling forecast |
| Chase sales without credit discipline | Tight credit policy with exceptions by approval |
| Leave CCC unmanaged | Monthly CCC metric; owner per lever |
| Pay suppliers too early | Match DPO to operating needs; don't be abusive |
| Ignore aging schedule | Weekly AR aging review; collections SLA |
| Sit on idle cash | Short-term investment policy or shareholder return |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: SaaS Firm Freeing $3M via CCC Optimisation
**Situation:** $40M ARR SaaS has DSO 60 + 30-day payment terms from customers. Enterprise onboarding is slow.

**Applicable framework/metric:** CCC + Receivables policy.

**Analysis:**
- DSO 60 vs typical SaaS 40 → $2.2M excess AR
- Automate billing; send at milestone completion; early-pay discount
- Tighter credit check for new enterprise

**Decision rule:** DSO > 40 for SaaS → process intervention.

**Action (Monday morning):** Audit AR aging; install billing automation; weekly collection reviews.

---

### Scenario 2: Consulting Advising on Cash-Crunch Prevention
**Situation:** Manufacturer runs into monthly cash crunches despite being profitable.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Cash Budget + CCC.

**Analysis:**
- DIO 75 (inventory-heavy); DPO 20 (paying suppliers fast); DSO 50
- CCC = 105 days → $8M locked
- Interventions: JIT inventory (target DIO 45); negotiate DPO to 45 days; early-pay discount to customers for DSO 40
- Target CCC 40 days → frees $5M

**Decision rule:** CCC > 60 days for mid-market manufacturer → transformation programme.

**Action:** Cash-cycle optimisation project; SCF programme; 6-month rollout.

---

### Scenario 3 (Anti-example): Profit Growth with Hidden Cash Disaster
**Situation:** Retailer grows sales 30% YoY but hits cash crunch; 60-day supplier terms used up; bank credit line maxed.

**Analysis (what goes wrong):**
- Growth funded by extended terms + credit line
- No cash budget or rolling forecast
- CCC deteriorated with scale

**Cost of this mistake:** Emergency factoring at 15% discount; supplier trust damage.

**Decision rule:** Forecast cash, not profit.

**Action:** Install rolling 13-week cash forecast; CCC targets per month; quarterly cash review.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. **Rolling 13-week cash forecast** — updated weekly; owned by Treasury.
2. **Monthly CCC tracking** — DIO, DSO, DPO; action if deteriorating.
3. **Credit policy + scoring** — documented; exceptions require approval.
4. **Aging review weekly** — collections team SLA on > 30 days.
5. **Cash budget with scenarios** — best/normal/worst.
6. **Driver-based forecasting** — not just line-item extrapolation.
7. **Idle-cash investment policy** — short-term instruments.
8. **Quarterly liquidity review at exec level** — trends + triggers.
