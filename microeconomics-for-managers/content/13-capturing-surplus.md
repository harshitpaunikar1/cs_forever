# Capturing Surplus (Price Discrimination, Bundling, Tying, Two-Part Tariffs, Advertising)


## Overview

Firms earn more by charging different prices to different customers or by selling smart packages—capturing more of what customers are willing to pay.


## Why It Matters

This is practical pricing strategy. It can raise profit without changing costs much, if done legally and ethically.


## Key Principles

- 1st degree: charge each customer their max willingness to pay (rare).
- 2nd degree: quantity discounts/versioning.
- 3rd degree: segment pricing (student vs regular).
- Bundling/tying can increase total willingness to pay.
- Two-part tariffs: membership fee + usage fee.


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Price Discrimination** | Different prices for same/similar product. |
| **Bundling** | Sell goods together. |
| **Mixed Bundling** | Bundle + separate options. |
| **Tying** | Must buy A to buy B. |
| **Two-Part Tariff** | Fixed fee + per-unit price. |


## Use Case

Streaming service sets mobile-only, standard, and premium tiers.


## Scenario

> An airline charges different fares for the same seat depending on time of booking, flexibility, and customer segment.


## Examples

- Gym membership fee + per-class charges.
- Shampoo + conditioner bundle priced to increase total purchase.

## 2. Jargon & Terminology

| Term | Full Form | Plain-English Meaning | Why It Exists | How It's Measured | Where You'll Hear It |
|------|-----------|----------------------|---------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| `price discrimination` | - | Charging different customers different effective prices for the same or similar offer. | It lets a firm capture more of the value customers assign to the product. | Price paid by segment, margin by segment, conversion by segment. | Pricing, revenue management, platform monetization. |
| `first-degree price discrimination` | - | Charging each customer as much as they are willing to pay. | It is the theoretical limit of surplus capture. | Individual willingness to pay versus realized price. | Economics classes, auctions, bespoke enterprise deals. |
| `second-degree price discrimination` | - | Customers self-select through quantity, version, or feature tiers. | It avoids needing perfect information about each buyer. | Plan mix, upgrade rate, attach rate, margin by tier. | SaaS plans, telecom bundles, software licensing. |
| `third-degree price discrimination` | - | Different prices for observable customer segments. | It uses segment-level differences in demand elasticity. | Student, geographic, channel, or enterprise price gaps. | Airline pricing, education, B2B account pricing. |
| `willingness to pay` | WTP | The most a buyer would pay before walking away. | It is the raw material of pricing strategy. | Surveys, conjoint analysis, historical bids, conversion tests. | Product research, sales calls, pricing teams. |
| `bundling` | - | Selling two or more products together. | It can lift total value and reduce cherry-picking. | Bundle take-rate, attach rate, bundle margin. | Software suites, telecom plans, retail kits. |
| `mixed bundling` | - | Offering the bundle and also separate items. | It gives customers choice and protects conversion. | Share choosing bundle versus standalone. | Subscription plans, enterprise software, consumer goods. |
| `tying` | - | Requiring purchase of one product to buy another. | It can protect complements or control the ecosystem. | Tie-in adoption, complement usage, compliance rate. | Platforms, printers and cartridges, app ecosystems. |
| `two-part tariff` | - | A fixed fee plus a per-use or per-unit price. | It separates access value from usage value. | Fixed fee revenue, usage revenue, average revenue per user. | Memberships, gyms, APIs, cloud services. |
| `advertising` | - | Paid communication to increase awareness, trust, or demand. | It can shift demand, signal quality, or support segmentation. | CAC, ROAS, conversion lift, brand lift, retention. | Growth marketing, media planning, product launches. |
| `surplus capture` | - | Keeping more of the value created in the transaction. | It is the business goal behind the pricing tools in this chapter. | Profit, gross margin, consumer surplus extracted, LTV/CAC. | Revenue strategy, consulting work, product monetization. |

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Price Discrimination Ladder
**Purpose:** Choose the least leaky way to charge different customers different effective prices.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Perfect info -> 1st degree
Observable segments -> 3rd degree
Self-selection tiers -> 2nd degree
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Information quality: how much the firm knows about each buyer's willingness to pay.
Segment observability: whether buyers can be sorted by role, geography, usage, or channel.
Arbitrage control: whether customers can resell, share, or route around the price wall.
Implementation burden: how much operations, billing, and policy work is required.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company with enterprise, mid-market, and startup plans uses third-degree pricing for visible segments, then adds usage tiers and feature gates for self-selection [verified from model knowledge, not source]. The decision is whether to pursue more precise capture with custom enterprise deals or keep pricing simple enough to scale.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team wants to know whether to segment by customer type, feature tier, or pure usage.

### Bundle and Tying Design Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether to sell items separately, together, or as a required package.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                     Customer WTP dispersion
                  Low                         High
Complements  +--------------------+----------------------+
Strong       | Tying / bundle     | Mixed bundling       |
Weak         | Standalone pricing | Pure bundling        |
              +--------------------+----------------------+
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Product complementarity: whether the items are used together in the same workflow.
WTP dispersion: whether different buyers value the components very differently.
Cannibalization risk: whether separate sales destroy bundle economics.
Choice architecture: whether customers need a separate option to convert at all.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI analytics platform sells the core dashboard and premium forecast module as a bundle to enterprise clients, but keeps stand-alone access for small teams [verified from model knowledge, not source]. The decision is to use mixed bundling so the firm captures high willingness to pay without losing buyers who only need one component.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When product, sales, and finance cannot agree on a suite versus stand-alone plan.

### Two-Part Tariff and Advertising Loop
**Purpose:** Separate access value from usage value, then use advertising to fill the funnel.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Awareness -> trial -> access fee -> usage fee -> repeat use -> higher captured surplus
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Access fee: the fixed charge for being allowed into the system.
Usage fee: the variable charge tied to actual consumption.
Demand expansion: advertising can increase the number of users who enter the funnel.
Signal quality: advertising can also tell the market the product is serious, premium, or trustworthy.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud API platform charges a monthly platform fee plus per-call usage, then runs targeted developer advertising to increase trials and active accounts [verified from model knowledge, not source]. The decision is whether advertising is pulling in the right users and whether the fixed fee is high enough to monetize committed customers.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the business has both a recurring access component and a metered consumption component.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below are standard decision tools that operationalize the chapter's pricing ideas. They are expanded from model knowledge, not the source. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: Two-Part Tariff Revenue
Formula: `TR = F*N + p*Q`
Variables:
`TR` = total revenue
`F` = fixed fee per customer
`N` = number of customers
`p` = per-unit usage price
`Q` = total units consumed
Why this formula exists: It answers how much revenue comes from access versus usage.
How to interpret the output:
Value dominated by `F` -> access is the main value -> protect onboarding and retention
Value balanced between `F` and `p` -> healthy hybrid monetization -> monitor usage mix
Value dominated by `p` -> usage drives the business -> manage demand elasticity carefully
Worked example with numbers: If 1,000 customers pay a $20 monthly fee and collectively consume 50,000 units at $0.40 each, revenue is $20,000 + $20,000 = $40,000. Decision: if usage is spiky but fixed membership is stable, raise the access fee only carefully.

### Formula 2: Consumer Surplus Per Buyer
Formula: `consumer surplus = WTP - price`
Variables:
`WTP` = willingness to pay
`price` = amount charged
Why this formula exists: It answers how much value the customer keeps after purchase.
How to interpret the output:
Value near 0 -> firm captures most of the value -> strong monetization, but watch trust
Value moderately positive -> buyer sees some win -> healthy conversion and repeatability
Value very high -> money left on the table -> opportunity for better segmentation or packaging
Worked example with numbers: If an enterprise customer would pay $12,000 for a workflow tool but buys at $9,000, consumer surplus is $3,000. Decision: explore premium support, add-ons, or a usage tier if the customer segment can pay more. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 3: Advertising Contribution
Formula: `advertising contribution = incremental gross profit - ad spend`
Variables:
`incremental gross profit` = extra gross profit generated by the campaign
`ad spend` = money spent on the campaign
Why this formula exists: It answers whether advertising is economically creating value or just buying vanity metrics.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> campaign destroys value -> cut or rework
Value = 0 -> break-even -> only keep if strategic learning is valuable
Value > 0 -> campaign adds value -> scale while monitoring payback
Worked example with numbers: If a launch campaign creates $80,000 of extra gross profit and costs $50,000, contribution is $30,000. Decision: scale the campaign only if the marginal audience still converts at similar efficiency. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat every customer as if they have the same willingness to pay. | Segment by role, usage, geography, or deal size before setting price. |
| Force one flat price on a product with clearly different buyer value. | Use tiers, versions, or bundles to let buyers self-select. |
| Bundle products without checking whether customers value the components together. | Use bundling when valuations are uneven and the package raises total captured value. |
| Use tying just because you can. | Tie only when the complement, ecosystem, or compliance logic supports it. |
| Spend on advertising without measuring incremental profit. | Judge campaigns by contribution, conversion, and payback, not impressions alone. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: SaaS plan architecture
Situation: A software company sells a workflow tool to startups, mid-market teams, and large enterprises. Startups convert best on a low monthly price, while enterprises want security, support, and admin controls. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Price Discrimination Ladder + WTP.
Analysis: If startup WTP is $40 per seat, mid-market WTP is $75, and enterprise WTP is $180, one flat price at $75 leaves enterprise value unclaimed and may still be too high for startups. Tiered pricing captures more of the spread while preserving conversion.
Decision rule: If segment WTP differs meaningfully, use segmented pricing. If WTP is similar, keep pricing simple. If arbitrage is hard to control, limit discounts and resale rights.
Action: Publish good-better-best plans, add enterprise controls to the top tier, and test whether startup pricing needs a lighter entry plan.

### Scenario 2: Bundle versus stand-alone sell-through
Situation: A product team sells an AI note-taking app and a separate meeting intelligence module. Some buyers only want notes, while others want the combined workflow. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Bundle and Tying Design Matrix + attach rate.
Analysis: If 35% of buyers value only the note-taking app, pure bundling would block conversion. A mixed bundle lets the firm sell both products together and separately, increasing the average order value without losing the single-product buyers.
Decision rule: If a meaningful share wants one component only, use mixed bundling. If almost everyone wants both, bundle aggressively. If one product is useless without the other, consider tying.
Action: Launch the bundle, keep the individual SKU available, and watch attach rate and bundle margin weekly.

### Scenario 3: Paid acquisition for an API platform
Situation: A developer platform launches a paid campaign to bring in new API users. The team wants to know whether the campaign is a growth expense or a value-creating investment. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Two-Part Tariff and Advertising Loop + advertising contribution.
Analysis: The campaign generates $80,000 of incremental gross profit against $50,000 of spend, so contribution is $30,000. If new users mostly choose the usage-based plan, the platform may still need a higher fixed fee to cover support and onboarding costs.
Decision rule: If contribution is positive and payback is acceptable, scale. If contribution is negative, stop or redesign. If fixed-fee revenue is weak, tighten access pricing.
Action: Split the funnel by channel, compare CAC by segment, and adjust the base fee before increasing spend.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Map every revenue lever in the offer: access fee, usage fee, add-ons, support, and advertising.
2. Segment customers by willingness to pay, usage intensity, and resale or sharing risk.
3. Test whether a flat price is leaving money on the table or blocking conversion.
4. Choose a bundle structure and check whether the bundle protects or cannibalizes stand-alone demand.
5. Build a two-part tariff if the product has both fixed access value and variable usage value.
6. Measure advertising by incremental gross profit, not just impressions or clicks.
7. Review legal, channel, and trust constraints before scaling any aggressive pricing move.
