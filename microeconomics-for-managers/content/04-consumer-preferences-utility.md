# Consumer Preferences and Utility


## Overview

Preferences describe what people like and how they rank choices. Utility is a way to represent those preferences and explain satisfaction.


## Why It Matters

Managers can design products and bundles that better match what customers value (convenience, status, features).


## Key Principles

- People can rank bundles (A vs B vs C).
- Indifference curves show “different bundles, same satisfaction.”
- Marginal utility often falls (extra unit gives less extra happiness).


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Preferences** | Likes/dislikes that guide choice. |
| **Utility** | A score that represents satisfaction. |
| **Indifference Curve** | Bundles giving equal satisfaction. |
| **MRS (Marginal Rate of Substitution)** | How much of Y someone gives up for 1 more of X. |
| **Perfect Substitutes/Complements** | Goods that replace each other fully / goods used together. |


## Use Case

Creating a “good-better-best” product line (basic, standard, premium).


## Scenario

> Some customers want low price; others want top camera quality. The firm offers multiple models to fit different preferences.


## Examples

- Perfect complements: Left and right shoes.
- Perfect substitutes: Two brands of sugar for many buyers.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Indifference Map
**Purpose:** Show which bundles are equally good and which bundles are strictly better. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Utility higher
     ^
     |      IC3  (higher utility)
     |    /
     |  IC2
     | /
     |/ IC1  (lower utility)
     +----------------------> Good X
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: good X, the feature or product attribute you can increase.
Component 2: good Y, the feature or product attribute you may give up.
Component 3: indifference curves, which show equal satisfaction bundles.
Component 4: higher curves, which mean the customer prefers those bundles if affordable.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company maps bundles of storage and AI credits. Customers on a higher indifference curve get more total satisfaction, so the product team sees whether premium tiers are actually preferred or just more expensive. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team needs to understand what customers trade off rather than what they say they want.

### MRS vs Price Ratio
**Purpose:** Check whether the consumer's willingness to trade matches the market's price trade-off. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

**Text Diagram:**
```text
If MRS_xy > Px / Py -> buy more X
If MRS_xy < Px / Py -> buy more Y
If MRS_xy = Px / Py -> optimal interior choice
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: MRS, which tells you the customer's subjective trade-off.
Component 2: price ratio, which tells you the market trade-off.
Component 3: budget, which limits the feasible bundle.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A customer values an extra premium feature more than its price ratio suggests, so the bundle should keep that feature in the premium tier rather than the basic tier. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When pricing or packaging must answer "should we add, remove, or reprice this feature?"

### Substitutes vs Complements Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether the business should compete on price, bundle, or compatibility. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    Relationship
               Substitute              Complement
Response   +-------------------+   +------------------------+
to feature | price competition  |   | bundle / ecosystem play |
change     | and easy switching |   | and joint demand        |
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: substitutability, which means customers can switch easily.
Component 2: complementarity, which means customers use both goods together.
Component 3: product strategy, which becomes price competition for substitutes and ecosystem design for complements.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: Storage from two cloud vendors is a substitute, while a laptop and its docking station are complements. The first calls for price discipline; the second calls for bundle design. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When leadership is deciding whether to bundle, unbundle, or differentiate.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below expand the source into standard consumer-theory rules for business use. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: Utility Function
Formula: `U = f(x, y)`
Variables:
`x` = quantity of good X.
`y` = quantity of good Y.
Why this formula exists: It answers, "How do the goods combine into overall satisfaction?"
How to interpret the output:
Higher U -> preferred bundle -> more likely to be chosen if affordable
Same U -> indifferent bundles -> equal satisfaction
Lower U -> worse bundle -> avoid unless price is much lower
Worked example with numbers: If `U = x*y` and a bundle has x = 4 and y = 5, utility = 20. Another bundle with x = 2 and y = 12 gives 24, so the second bundle is preferred if both are affordable. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 2: Marginal Utility
Formula: `MU = Delta U / Delta Q`
Variables:
`Delta U` = change in utility.
`Delta Q` = change in quantity consumed.
Why this formula exists: It answers, "What is the extra satisfaction from one more unit?"
How to interpret the output:
MU high -> add more of the item -> feature or bundle is still valuable
MU falling but positive -> diminishing marginal utility -> keep adding only if price is low enough
MU near zero or negative -> stop adding -> user is saturated
Worked example with numbers: If the first premium AI credit adds 10 utility points and the second adds 6, marginal utility is falling. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 3: Marginal Rate of Substitution
Formula: `MRS_xy = MUx / MUy = -Delta y / Delta x`
Variables:
`MUx` = marginal utility of good X.
`MUy` = marginal utility of good Y.
`-Delta y / Delta x` = how much Y the consumer gives up for one more X on the same indifference curve.
Why this formula exists: It answers, "How much does the customer value one good relative to the other?"
How to interpret the output:
MRS > price ratio -> the customer values X more than the market does -> shift spend toward X
MRS = price ratio -> optimal interior bundle -> current mix is efficient
MRS < price ratio -> shift spend toward Y -> X is overpriced relative to value
Worked example with numbers: If a customer would give up 2 units of storage for 1 unit of AI credits, but the market price ratio is only 1 unit of storage for 1 unit of AI credits, the firm should push more AI credits in the bundle. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 4: Utility Maximization Condition
Formula: `MUx / Px = MUy / Py` and `PxX + PyY = Income`
Variables:
`Px`, `Py` = prices of goods X and Y.
`X`, `Y` = chosen quantities.
`Income` = budget available.
Why this formula exists: It answers, "What mix gives the highest utility within the budget?"
How to interpret the output:
Left side equal -> balanced spend -> interior optimum
One ratio much higher -> spend more on the better-value good
No feasible intersection -> choose a corner solution
Worked example with numbers: If storage gives 4 utility per dollar and AI credits give 6 utility per dollar, the customer should buy more AI credits until the ratios move closer together. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Build bundles without asking what customers trade off. | Map preferences and MRS before packaging. |
| Assume more features always mean more value. | Check marginal utility and look for saturation. |
| Treat perfect substitutes like a premium ecosystem. | Compete on price or convenience when switching is easy. |
| Sell complements separately when users consume them together. | Bundle complementary goods and reduce friction. |
| Ignore the budget constraint when designing tiers. | Test the utility-maximizing mix against real prices. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Good-better-best product line
Situation: A smartphone company is designing three tiers. Basic buyers care about price, mid-tier buyers care about battery life, and premium buyers care about camera quality and storage. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Indifference map and MRS vs price ratio.
Analysis: If the premium buyer's MRS for camera versus price is above the firm’s price ratio, the extra camera feature belongs in the premium tier. If the MRS is below the price ratio, the firm is overbuilding the premium bundle. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If MRS > price ratio, add or keep the feature. If MRS is near the ratio, maintain the tier. If MRS < price ratio, remove the feature or move it to an add-on.
Action: Redesign the good-better-best stack so each tier has a distinct utility jump, not just a higher price.

### Scenario 2: Subscription bundle with complements
Situation: A SaaS vendor sells collaboration software and cloud storage. Usage data shows customers who buy both have far lower churn than customers who buy only one. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Substitutes vs complements matrix and utility maximization.
Analysis: Because the goods are complements, the bundle reduces friction and increases total utility. If the combined bundle raises retained revenue without depressing conversion, bundling beats separate sale. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If joint usage is high, bundle the goods. If usage is independent, keep them separate. If substitution is strong, price them as alternatives.
Action: Offer a joint plan, add an onboarding flow that activates both features, and test whether churn falls after bundling.

### Scenario 3: Commodity choice between substitutes
Situation: A grocery platform sees that customers switch freely between two sugar brands when the price difference changes by even a small amount. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Perfect substitutes and marginal utility.
Analysis: If the MRS is roughly 1 and switching is easy, the products are close substitutes. That means the platform should expect price-sensitive switching rather than loyalty-based demand. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If goods are near-perfect substitutes, compete on price or convenience. If they are complements, sell them as a bundle. If one good’s marginal utility collapses after the first unit, avoid overpacking the offer.
Action: Tighten price monitoring, separate commodity SKUs from differentiated ones, and do not spend premium marketing on a near-substitute.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. List the customer bundles you actually sell, not just the features you can build.
2. Segment customers by what they value most: price, convenience, status, or performance.
3. Estimate whether the goods are substitutes, complements, or mixed bundles. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
4. Compute the MRS or a proxy from choice data before finalizing pricing tiers. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
5. Check whether the bundle maximizes utility within the real budget, not the hypothetical one. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
6. Design a good-better-best stack or bundle menu that reflects distinct utility steps.
7. Re-test after launch because preferences and willingness to pay can move.
