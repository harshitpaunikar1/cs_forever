# Theory of Demand (Income/Substitution Effects, Surplus, Market Demand, Labor–Leisure, Price Indices)


## Overview

This explains why the demand curve slopes down: when price changes, people substitute and their “real buying power” changes. It also explains how individual demand becomes market demand.


## Why It Matters

Managers predict how customers respond to price changes, income changes, and competitor moves—more accurately than guesswork.


## Key Principles

- Price change causes:
- Substitution effect: switch to relatively cheaper goods.
- Income effect: purchasing power changes.
- Consumer surplus measures buyer benefit.
- Market demand is the sum of many consumers.
- Labor supply can bend backward when higher wages change leisure choices.
- Price indices track cost of living changes.


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Substitution Effect** | Switch toward cheaper relative option. |
| **Income Effect** | Change due to higher/lower real purchasing power. |
| **Consumer Surplus** | Willingness to pay − price paid. |
| **Market Demand** | Horizontal sum of individual demands. |
| **CPI (Consumer Price Index)** | Measure of average price level. |


## Use Case

Predicting what happens if you raise price but competitor stays the same.


## Scenario

> Price of brand A increases. Some buyers switch to brand B (substitution), and some reduce total spending (income effect).


## Examples

- If petrol price rises, some shift to public transport (substitution).
- If salary increases, people buy more restaurant meals (income effect for normal goods).

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Price Change Decomposition Tree
**Purpose:** Separate the total effect of a price change into substitution and income effects. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Price change
   |
   +--> Substitution effect -> switch toward the relatively cheaper option
   |
   +--> Income effect -> real purchasing power changes
   |
   +--> Total quantity change -> observed demand response
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: substitution effect, which captures relative-price switching.
Component 2: income effect, which captures the real-buying-power change.
Component 3: total effect, which is the observed change in quantity demanded.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A subscription price increase pushes some users to a cheaper plan and makes others cut usage because they feel poorer. The decomposition tree tells the product team whether the drop is mostly switching or mostly affordability. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When management sees demand fall and wants to know whether price or affordability is driving it.

### Market Demand Aggregation Ladder
**Purpose:** Convert individual choices into the market curve the firm actually sells into. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Consumer 1 demand
Consumer 2 demand   -> horizontal sum -> market demand
Consumer 3 demand
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: individual demand, which varies by income, tastes, and substitution options.
Component 2: price, which is common across the market at a moment in time.
Component 3: horizontal sum, which adds quantities not prices.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud product has three segments with different willingness to pay. The ladder shows that the market curve is not an average of prices; it is the sum of what each segment buys at each price. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone incorrectly averages customer demand instead of aggregating it.

### Labor-Leisure Supply Curve Matrix
**Purpose:** Explain why wages do not always raise labor hours linearly. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                   Wage level
                 Low                    High
Hours   +--------------------+   +------------------------+
worked  | more wage -> more  |   | backward bend possible |
        | hours worked       |   | if leisure is valued   |
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: wage level, which changes the value of time spent working.
Component 2: labor hours, which are the quantity supplied to the market.
Component 3: leisure, which becomes more attractive as wages rise for some workers.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm raises overtime pay, but senior experts choose more time off after a threshold because the leisure value rises. The matrix explains why the firm may need different incentive design for different labor segments. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When higher wages do not produce the expected number of work hours.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below expand the source into standard demand-analysis rules for business use. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: Consumer Surplus
Formula: `consumer surplus = willingness to pay - price paid`
Variables:
`willingness to pay` = the maximum amount a buyer would pay.
`price paid` = the actual transaction price.
Why this formula exists: It answers, "How much extra value does the customer get from the transaction?"
How to interpret the output:
Value > 0 -> positive buyer benefit -> pricing has room
Value = 0 -> buyer is indifferent at the margin -> price is near the limit
Value < 0 -> deal does not happen -> price is too high
Worked example with numbers: If a buyer is willing to pay 500 for a software plan and pays 420, consumer surplus is 80. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 2: Market Demand
Formula: `Q_market(P) = sum_i q_i(P)`
Variables:
`Q_market(P)` = total market quantity demanded at price P.
`q_i(P)` = quantity demanded by consumer i at price P.
Why this formula exists: It answers, "How many units will the whole market buy at each price?"
How to interpret the output:
Higher quantity at a given price -> larger addressable volume -> better scale potential
Lower quantity at a given price -> smaller market -> tighter pricing discipline needed
Flat demand response -> price-insensitive market -> test value-based pricing
Worked example with numbers: If three segments buy 100, 250, and 150 units at a given price, market demand is 500 units. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 3: Real Purchasing Power
Formula: `real income = nominal income / price index`
Variables:
`nominal income` = income in current rupees or dollars.
`price index` = CPI or another cost-of-living index.
Why this formula exists: It answers, "How much can people actually buy after inflation?"
How to interpret the output:
Real income falls -> affordability pressure -> likely income effect on demand
Real income flat -> demand should shift less from income alone
Real income rises -> normal goods gain volume
Worked example with numbers: If income is 100,000 and the price index is 125, real income is 80,000 in base-year units. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 4: Total Effect of a Price Change
Formula: `total effect = substitution effect + income effect`
Variables:
`substitution effect` = change caused by relative-price switching.
`income effect` = change caused by real purchasing power.
Why this formula exists: It answers, "Why did demand change after the price moved?"
How to interpret the output:
Both effects same direction -> strong response -> pricing needs care
Effects partly offset -> muted response -> price may move without huge volume loss
Income effect dominates -> affordability is the bigger issue -> discount or tiering may help
Worked example with numbers: If a price increase causes quantity to fall by 8 units through substitution and another 4 through income loss, the total effect is -12 units. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat every quantity drop as a pure price response. | Separate substitution and income effects. |
| Average customer demand instead of adding it up. | Build market demand as the horizontal sum of segments. |
| Ignore CPI when wages or prices move. | Normalize nominal figures into real purchasing power. |
| Assume higher wages always mean more labor hours. | Check for a backward-bending labor supply response. |
| Price products without measuring buyer surplus. | Use consumer surplus to judge how much room the market has. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Brand price increase with competitor unchanged
Situation: Brand A raises price from 100 to 115 while Brand B stays flat. Some customers switch to Brand B, while others simply buy less overall. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Price change decomposition tree.
Analysis: If quantity sold falls from 10,000 to 8,800, the 1,200-unit drop is the total effect. Part of that drop is substitution to Brand B and part is reduced real purchasing power. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If substitution dominates, rework price relative to competitors. If income effect dominates, offer a lower tier or smaller pack. If both are small, the price increase is defensible.
Action: Segment loyal versus price-sensitive customers, then test a tiered price ladder instead of one blunt increase.

### Scenario 2: Income rise for normal and inferior goods
Situation: A region gets a salary bump, and a food-delivery platform wants to know whether restaurant orders will rise or whether low-cost staples will lose share. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Market demand aggregation ladder and real purchasing power.
Analysis: If restaurant meals are a normal good, higher real income raises demand. If a budget staple is inferior, demand may fall even as total spending rises. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If the good is normal, expect growth after income rises. If inferior, expect customers to move away as affordability improves. If mixed, segment the market.
Action: Update forecast models with income sensitivity and separate premium versus budget demand in planning.

### Scenario 3: Wage rise and labor hours
Situation: An AI consulting firm increases hourly pay for senior contractors, but some contractors reduce overtime because they value leisure more once pay crosses a threshold. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Labor-leisure supply curve matrix.
Analysis: At lower wages, more pay can buy more hours. At higher wages, the backward-bending segment may appear, so hours worked can fall even as wages rise. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If hours rise with wages, use pay to expand supply. If hours flatten or fall, redesign incentives or add non-cash rewards. If hours are highly elastic, use variable staffing.
Action: Build a wage-hours table by segment, then redesign the contract mix instead of assuming one pay rate solves supply.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Identify the price, income, wage, or competitor shock you need to explain.
2. Split the observed response into substitution, income, and any labor-leisure effects.
3. Aggregate segment-level demand into market demand using horizontal sum logic. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
4. Normalize nominal figures with CPI or another price index before drawing conclusions. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
5. Estimate consumer surplus for the current price to see how much room remains. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
6. Check whether the good is normal or inferior, and whether labor supply is bending backward.
7. Update pricing, tiering, or staffing decisions using the decomposed effect, not the raw headline change.
