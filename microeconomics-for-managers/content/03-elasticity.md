# Elasticity (Price, Income, Cross, Supply)


## Overview

Elasticity measures how strongly quantity changes when price (or income or another price) changes.


## Why It Matters

It tells you whether raising price will increase or decrease revenue, and how sensitive customers are.


## Key Principles

- Elastic demand: customers react a lot to price changes.
- Inelastic demand: customers react a little.
- Elasticity often differs in short run vs long run.


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Price Elasticity of Demand** | %ΔQ / %ΔP |
| **Income Elasticity** | %ΔQ / %ΔIncome |
| **Cross-Price Elasticity** | %ΔQ of X / %ΔP of Y |
| **Price Elasticity of Supply** | %ΔQ supplied / %ΔP |
| **Short Run vs Long Run** | Response can grow with time as people adjust. |


## Use Case

Setting discount size for a festival sale without destroying profit.


## Scenario

> A smartphone brand considers a 5% price cut. If demand is very elastic, sales jump a lot; if inelastic, revenue may fall.


## Examples

- Cross elasticity: Tea and coffee are substitutes—coffee price up → tea demand up.
- Long run more elastic: Over time, people can switch brands or habits more easily.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Elasticity tells you how strongly quantity reacts when price, income, or another product's price changes.  
For IT/AI/Product/Consulting leaders, it is the core tool for pricing, discounting, demand forecasting, and capacity planning.  
The main decision it supports is whether a price move, promotion, or capacity change will raise revenue or damage it.

## 2. Jargon & Terminology

| Term | Full Form | Plain-English Meaning | Why It Exists | How It's Measured | Where You'll Hear It |
|------|-----------|----------------------|---------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| price elasticity of demand | PED | How much demand changes when price changes. | Tells pricing teams whether a price cut or hike will backfire. | `% change in quantity demanded / % change in price`. | Pricing, revenue management, consulting. |
| income elasticity | YED | How much demand changes when income changes. | Shows whether the good behaves like a necessity or a luxury. | `% change in quantity demanded / % change in income`. | Product strategy, category planning, macro analysis. |
| cross-price elasticity | XED | How demand for one product changes when another product's price changes. | Reveals substitutes and complements. | `% change in quantity demanded of X / % change in price of Y`. | Assortment planning, competitive pricing. |
| price elasticity of supply | PES | How much supply changes when price changes. | Helps operations see how quickly capacity can respond. | `% change in quantity supplied / % change in price`. | Manufacturing, procurement, operations. |
| elastic demand / inelastic demand | - | Demand that changes a lot versus a little when price changes. | Separates revenue-growing price moves from risky ones. | Absolute elasticity above or below 1. | Pricing reviews, A/B tests. |
| short run / long run | - | Response today versus response after people adapt. | Explains why elasticity often grows with time. | Compare same metric across time windows. | Strategy, forecasting, policy. |

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices
The frameworks below translate the chapter into pricing and capacity decisions [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Elasticity Revenue Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether a price change will likely raise revenue.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
elastic demand -> price cut can raise revenue
unit elastic -> revenue is sensitive but balanced
inelastic demand -> price increase can raise revenue
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: demand sensitivity - how strongly quantity reacts to price.
Component 2: revenue direction - whether the move likely increases or decreases revenue.
Component 3: margin effect - what happens after unit economics are included.
Component 4: strategic fit - whether the move aligns with the brand or product position.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS product discounts annual plans. If demand is elastic, a lower price can bring enough new subscribers to raise total revenue. If demand is inelastic, the same cut may only shrink margin.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone suggests a price cut or hike with no elasticity evidence.

### Short-Run vs Long-Run Adjustment Curve
**Purpose:** Show why elasticity usually changes over time.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
short run: sticky behavior
mid run: partial adjustment
long run: switching, substitution, and habit change
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: time horizon - how long customers have to react.
Component 2: switching options - how easy it is to change brands, habits, or suppliers.
Component 3: operational slack - how quickly supply can adjust.
Component 4: strategic implication - whether to optimize now or redesign for later.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud vendor raises price. Churn is low this month, but over six months customers migrate to competitors and renegotiate contracts. The long-run elasticity is higher than the short-run reading.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team assumes today's response will hold forever.

### Substitute-Complement Map
**Purpose:** Use cross-price effects to infer the competitive landscape.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
price of Y up -> demand for X up = substitutes
price of Y up -> demand for X down = complements
price of Y up -> no clear effect = weak link
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: own product X - the product whose demand is being observed.
Component 2: related product Y - the product whose price changes.
Component 3: sign of response - positive, negative, or near zero.
Component 4: business meaning - rivalry, bundling, or independence.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: If coffee prices rise and tea demand rises, the category is acting like a substitute set. If software add-ons rise and core plan usage falls, the bundle may be too tightly coupled.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When pricing or portfolio teams want to know what else the customer can switch to.

## 4. Formulas
### Formula 1: Price Elasticity of Demand
Formula: `PED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in price`
Variables:
quantity demanded = units customers buy
price = amount charged per unit
Why this formula exists: It answers whether a price move helps or hurts revenue.
How to interpret the output:
Absolute value < 1 -> inelastic demand -> price increases may raise revenue
Absolute value = 1 -> unit elastic -> revenue is roughly unchanged by small moves
Absolute value > 1 -> elastic demand -> price cuts may raise revenue
Worked example with numbers: If price falls 10% and quantity rises 15%, PED = -1.5. Decision: the product is elastic, so a targeted price cut can be profitable if margin still works.

### Formula 2: Income Elasticity
Formula: `YED = % change in quantity demanded / % change in income`
Variables:
quantity demanded = units customers buy
income = consumer purchasing power
Why this formula exists: It answers whether demand rises with prosperity or falls when income changes.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> inferior good -> may decline as income rises
Value 0-1 -> necessity -> grows slower than income
Value > 1 -> luxury -> grows faster than income
Worked example with numbers: If income rises 8% and demand rises 4%, YED = 0.5. Decision: the product behaves like a necessity and may be resilient in downturns.

### Formula 3: Cross-Price Elasticity
Formula: `XED = % change in quantity demanded of X / % change in price of Y`
Variables:
X = product being measured
Y = related product whose price changes
Why this formula exists: It answers whether products compete or complement each other.
How to interpret the output:
Value > 0 -> substitutes -> competitive pressure
Value < 0 -> complements -> bundle or ecosystem logic
Value around 0 -> weak relationship
Worked example with numbers: If coffee price rises 5% and tea demand rises 10%, XED = 2.0. Decision: tea is a strong substitute and deserves a competitive pricing watchlist.

### Formula 4: Price Elasticity of Supply
Formula: `PES = % change in quantity supplied / % change in price`
Variables:
quantity supplied = units producers can supply
price = amount received per unit
Why this formula exists: It answers how quickly operations can scale output when price changes.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 1 -> supply is sticky -> capacity is hard to expand quickly
Value 1-2 -> supply is moderately flexible
Value > 2 -> supply is highly responsive
Worked example with numbers: If price rises 10% and supply rises 5%, PES = 0.5. Decision: the bottleneck is capacity, not willingness to sell.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Cut price because the team wants growth. | Check demand elasticity and unit margin first. |
| Assume short-run response equals long-run response. | Re-estimate after customers have time to switch and adapt. |
| Ignore the price of related products. | Test cross-price effects for substitutes and complements. |
| Plan supply as if capacity is infinitely flexible. | Measure supply elasticity and physical constraint slack. |
| Treat a festival sale like a normal week. | Recalculate elasticity under peak conditions and promotion depth. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Smartphone festival discount
Situation: A smartphone brand is considering a 5% price cut during a festival sale. Product wants more volume, finance wants margin protection, and operations wants no stock chaos.
Applicable framework/metric: Price Elasticity of Demand and Elasticity Revenue Matrix.
Analysis: If quantity rises 12%, PED is -2.4, so demand is elastic. That suggests the cut can lift revenue if margin loss is controlled. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If absolute PED > 1, a discount may scale. If absolute PED is near 1, revenue may be flat. If absolute PED < 1, raising price may be safer.
Action: Run a controlled discount test, watch gross margin, and separate festival uplift from baseline demand.

### Scenario 2: Tea and coffee substitution
Situation: A beverage brand is deciding whether to raise coffee prices after bean costs rise. The category manager wants to know whether tea will absorb the displaced demand.
Applicable framework/metric: Cross-Price Elasticity and Substitute-Complement Map.
Analysis: If coffee price rises 4% and tea demand rises 8%, XED is 2.0. Tea is a substitute, so coffee pricing needs a competitive response plan. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If XED > 0, watch substitutes. If XED < 0, manage bundles. If XED is near 0, price independently.
Action: Track competitor pricing, update assortment, and test whether bundles blunt the switching effect.

### Scenario 3: Capacity response after a price increase
Situation: A manufacturer raises price and wants to know whether the supply network can react without backlogs. The ops lead says the plant is already close to full.
Applicable framework/metric: Price Elasticity of Supply and Short-Run vs Long-Run Adjustment Curve.
Analysis: If price rises 10% and supply rises only 3%, PES is 0.3. That means the supply side is sticky in the short run and cannot expand much without lead time. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If PES < 1, increase price cautiously and protect service. If PES is 1-2, use moderate scaling. If PES > 2, the supply network can absorb more demand.
Action: Reserve overtime, secure materials earlier, and treat the first quarter as a capacity stress test.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Estimate own-price elasticity for the top products or services before changing price.
2. Add cross-price checks for the nearest substitute and complement in the portfolio.
3. Separate short-run tests from long-run readouts so teams do not overinterpret the first week.
4. Build a simple pricing dashboard showing revenue, margin, and volume together.
5. Check supply elasticity before promising a promotion or a large launch spike.
6. Run small controlled tests instead of big-bang price changes whenever possible.
7. Review results by segment, because elasticity is rarely uniform across the customer base.
