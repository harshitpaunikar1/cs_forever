# Externalities and Public Goods (Coase, Free Rider, Market Failure)


## Overview

Externalities happen when a decision affects others who aren’t part of the transaction (pollution). Public goods are shared goods that people can use without paying easily (streetlights).


## Why It Matters

Markets may fail to produce efficient outcomes. Managers deal with regulation, sustainability, CSR, and industries affected by external costs/benefits.


## Key Principles

- Negative externalities lead to “too much” of the harmful activity.
- Positive externalities lead to “too little” of the beneficial activity.
- Solutions include taxes, subsidies, regulation, property rights, and bargaining.
- Public goods face the free-rider problem.


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Externality** | Side effect on others not priced in the market. |
| **Negative/Positive Externality** | Harm/benefit spillovers. |
| **Coase Theorem** | With clear property rights and low bargaining costs, parties can negotiate efficient outcomes. |
| **Public Good** | Non-excludable and non-rival (often). |
| **Free Rider** | Benefits without paying. |


## Use Case

A factory reduces pollution because regulation/taxes make pollution costly, and brand reputation matters.


## Scenario

> A river gets polluted by multiple factories. Without rules, each factory dumps waste to save cost. Government introduces emissions fees; firms invest in cleaner tech.


## Examples

- Vaccination has positive externalities (protects others).
- Air pollution is a negative externality (health costs on society).

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Externalities are spillovers that the market does not price correctly; public goods are shared benefits that are hard to exclude people from.  
For IT/AI/Product/Consulting leaders, this topic decides when to price the harm, subsidize the upside, or create property rights and governance.  
The business question is whether to internalize a spillover, fund a shared asset, or regulate behavior before the economics break.

|------|-----------|----------------------|---------------|-------------------|---------------------|

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices
The frameworks below translate the chapter into a control system for spillovers, shared assets, and regulation [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Externality Sign Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether the business problem is about harm, benefit, or shared access.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                     Social effect
              Harmful                    Helpful
Private  +-----------------------------------------------+
activity | Negative externality      | Positive externality
          | price the harm            | fund the upside
Shared    | congestion / overload     | public good / free rider
access    | regulate usage            | subsidize contribution
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: private activity - the action a firm or user takes.
Component 2: social effect - whether the spillover helps or harms others.
Component 3: pricing response - tax, fee, subsidy, or access rule.
Component 4: governance response - regulation, rights, or funded shared assets.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A mobile app that pushes too many notifications creates attention spam for users and support load for the platform. That is a negative externality, so the fix is not only more growth but better throttling, user controls, or fees [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is optimizing one metric while creating harm or value elsewhere.

### Coase Bargaining Map
**Purpose:** Test whether negotiation can solve the problem before you default to regulation.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Clear rights + low bargaining cost = Coase works
Clear rights + high bargaining cost = partial fix
Unclear rights + low bargaining cost = rights first, then bargain
Unclear rights + high bargaining cost = regulation is usually needed
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: property rights clarity - who owns the asset or the damage right.
Component 2: bargaining cost - legal, search, and coordination cost.
Component 3: enforcement strength - whether agreements can be monitored and enforced.
Component 4: outcome - private deal, hybrid governance, or public regulation.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: If an AI platform shares a benchmark dataset across partners, clear usage rights and low transaction cost can make a consortium work. If rights are vague and the coordination burden is high, the project needs a policy layer or central governance [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone says, "Can't the parties just work it out?"

### Policy Tool Selector
**Purpose:** Match the spillover to the least-bad intervention.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Negative spillover -> tax or regulation
Positive spillover -> subsidy or co-funding
Shared asset -> access rules or governance
Low bargaining cost -> Coase-style negotiation
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: external cost or benefit - the direction of the spillover.
Component 2: transaction cost - how hard it is to bargain or monitor.
Component 3: policy tool - fee, subsidy, rule, or rights assignment.
Component 4: management implication - redesign the incentive rather than just the KPI.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud team that overuses compute during peak hours can be charged an internal carbon or capacity fee, while teams building internal observability dashboards may need funding because the benefit spreads across the whole organization [verified from model knowledge, not source].

When to pull this out in a meeting: When a spillover is obvious but the intervention is still being debated.

## 4. Formulas
The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are practical ways to size the spillover and choose the intervention [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Social Cost
Formula: `Social Cost = Private Cost + External Cost`
Variables:
Private Cost = cost borne by the decision-maker
External Cost = cost imposed on others
Why this formula exists: It answers why the market price is too low when harm is pushed onto outsiders.
How to interpret the output:
Value near Private Cost -> little spillover -> market may be close to efficient
Value much higher than Private Cost -> serious negative externality -> tax or regulation needed
Value below Private Cost after credits -> subsidy or support may be justified
Worked example with numbers: If a batch job costs the firm $8 but creates $12 of grid and environmental cost, social cost is $20. That means the workload should be throttled or charged a corrective fee [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 2: Pigouvian Tax
Formula: `Pigouvian Tax = Marginal External Cost`
Variables:
Marginal External Cost = extra harm caused by one more unit of activity
Why this formula exists: It answers what tax or fee would make the decision-maker face the true cost.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> small correction may be enough
Value moderate -> internal fee or cap-and-trade can work
Value high -> stronger regulation or redesign is needed
Worked example with numbers: If one more ton of emissions imposes $45 of harm, an internal carbon fee of $45 per ton is the correct starting point. If the fee is only $10, the harmful activity stays too attractive [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 3: Net Social Surplus
Formula: `Net Social Surplus = Private Benefit + External Benefit - Private Cost - External Cost`
Variables:
Private Benefit = value captured by the decision-maker
External Benefit = value created for others
Private Cost = cost to the decision-maker
External Cost = cost to others
Why this formula exists: It answers whether a positive externality is being underfunded because the creator cannot capture all the value.
How to interpret the output:
Value negative -> activity should stop
Value slightly positive but unfunded -> subsidy, grant, or co-investment may be needed
Value strongly positive -> scale the activity and design governance
Worked example with numbers: If open-source documentation costs one team $30,000 but saves the wider developer ecosystem $80,000 and also saves the company $10,000 in support cost, net social surplus is $60,000. The right move is to fund it, not treat it as overhead [verified from model knowledge, not source].

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat spillovers as if they were zero. | Measure the external cost or benefit explicitly. |
| Assume market price captures all value. | Add social cost or social benefit before making the call. |
| Rely on voluntary funding for shared assets only. | Expect free riding and build contribution rules. |
| Use regulation before checking bargaining rights. | Test whether Coase-style negotiation is feasible first. |
| Fund positive spillovers ad hoc. | Create a subsidy, budget line, or shared-service model. |
| Ignore governance for shared data or platforms. | Assign property rights, access rules, and auditability. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Cloud compute and carbon externality
Situation: A product team schedules large training jobs during peak grid hours. The job is profitable for the team, but it raises carbon emissions and infrastructure stress for the company.
Applicable framework/metric: Social Cost and Pigouvian Tax.
Analysis: If the job creates $8 of internal value but $12 of external harm per run, the true social cost is $20. An internal fee near $12 changes the decision so the team only runs jobs that are worth the full cost [verified from model knowledge, not source].
Decision rule: If external cost is greater than private benefit, throttle or redesign. If the two are close, price the spillover. If the external cost is small, keep the job but monitor it.
Action: Add an internal carbon fee, shift jobs to off-peak windows, and publish the cost in the product planning review.

### Scenario 2: Open-source benchmark dataset
Situation: A consulting firm and a product team co-build a benchmark dataset that helps the whole ecosystem, but neither wants to pay full cost because competitors can also use it.
Applicable framework/metric: Net Social Surplus and Free Rider.
Analysis: If dataset creation costs $40,000 and avoids $15,000 of duplicated work for three partner firms, total external benefit is $45,000. Net social surplus is +$5,000, but free riding means nobody individually wants to fund it [verified from model knowledge, not source].
Decision rule: If social surplus is positive but private payoff is weak, co-fund it or create a consortium. If it is negative, stop.
Action: Set up a shared budget, define usage rights, and measure downstream adoption.

### Scenario 3: Public API or knowledge base
Situation: A platform team considers releasing an internal API spec and documentation so partner teams can integrate faster. The benefit spreads to many teams, but the authors bear the implementation cost.
Applicable framework/metric: Positive Externality and Coase Bargaining Map.
Analysis: If documentation costs $20,000 and saves five partner teams 8 hours each per week, the ecosystem benefit quickly exceeds the authoring cost. Because the rights are internal and bargaining cost is low, a Coase-style agreement can work if ownership and support duties are clear [verified from model knowledge, not source].
Decision rule: If coordination cost is low, negotiate. If the benefit is widespread and hard to bill, fund centrally. If the benefit is small, keep it as a local improvement.
Action: Assign an owner, publish contribution standards, and treat the doc set as a shared platform asset.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Inventory the spillovers in your product, platform, or operating model and label each as harmful or helpful.
2. Estimate the private cost or benefit and the external cost or benefit for the top three cases.
3. Decide whether the fix is a tax, subsidy, regulation, property-rights change, or negotiation.
4. Add an internal fee or budget code for activities that create hidden harm.
5. Create a funding model for shared assets that many teams use but no single team can capture.
6. Define ownership, access rules, and audit logs for shared data or infrastructure.
7. Re-check whether the policy still fits after the process, market, or technology changes.
