# Monopoly and Monopsony


## Overview

Monopoly: one main seller with pricing power. Monopsony: one main buyer with buying power (e.g., dominant employer).


## Why It Matters

Market power changes pricing, wages, output, and regulation risk. Managers must understand how to price without triggering backlash or losing long-term demand.


## Key Principles

- Monopoly chooses output where MR = MC, then sets price from demand.
- Monopolist produces on elastic part of demand.
- Market power creates deadweight loss.
- Monopsony can push input prices (like wages) down.


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Marginal Revenue (MR)** | Extra revenue from one more unit. |
| **Deadweight Loss** | Lost total surplus from inefficiency. |
| **Lerner Index** | Measure of pricing power. |
| **Monopsony** | Powerful buyer setting lower purchase price. |


## Use Case

A dominant platform sets seller fees (monopsony-like power over sellers).


## Scenario

> A large employer in a small town offers lower wages because workers have few alternatives.


## Examples

- Patent-protected drug pricing (monopoly).
- Single large buyer of crops in a region (monopsony).

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Monopoly Price-Setting Spine
**Purpose:** Decide how much a dominant seller should produce and what price it can sustain.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Demand curve -> choose output where MR = MC -> read price from demand curve
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: demand curve - shows what buyers will pay at each output level.
Component 2: MR - revenue from one more unit, which falls faster than price in monopoly.
Component 3: MC - extra cost of one more unit.
Component 4: chosen output - the quantity where profit is highest.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud-software vendor with a unique compliance module faces sticky enterprise demand. It chooses a lower volume and higher price than a commodity SaaS vendor because MR=MC happens before the price falls to marginal cost. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When leadership asks whether the business should raise price or ship more volume.

### Monopsony Buying-Power Spine
**Purpose:** Decide how a dominant buyer should set input volume and what it will do to supplier or labor prices.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Input supply -> buyer chooses quantity where MFC = MRP -> resulting wage / purchase price falls below competitive level
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: input supply - how sellers or workers respond to higher pay.
Component 2: MFC - marginal factor cost, the extra cost of another unit of input.
Component 3: MRP - marginal revenue product, the value of the next unit of input.
Component 4: input price - wage or supplier payout after the buyer applies power.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A dominant marketplace buyer sets seller fees and payment terms that leave suppliers with less bargaining power. The decision is not only about lowering cost; it also changes supplier participation and long-run ecosystem quality. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When procurement, HR, or platform teams are negotiating against a highly concentrated counterparty.

### Market Power Risk Matrix
**Purpose:** Classify whether the problem is seller power, buyer power, or both.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                 Low buyer power     High buyer power
Low seller power      normal market        monopsony risk
High seller power     monopoly risk        bilateral power / heavy regulation risk
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: seller power - ability to raise price above competitive levels.
Component 2: buyer power - ability to push input prices below competitive levels.
Component 3: welfare loss - deadweight loss or supplier harm.
Component 4: governance response - antitrust, price caps, sourcing redesign, or labor-policy review.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A dominant app store can resemble monopoly on the seller side and monopsony on the developer side at the same time. The matrix helps separate pricing abuse from procurement abuse so the response fits the actual market failure. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: When one market actor controls both the selling and buying side of a platform.

## 4. Formulas

The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are the standard managerial relationships that make the chapter actionable. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: Monopoly Output Rule
Formula: `MR = MC`
Variables:
`MR` = marginal revenue
`MC` = marginal cost
Why this formula exists: It answers the profit-maximizing output choice for a seller with pricing power.
How to interpret the output:
If `MR > MC` -> increase output
If `MR = MC` -> stop at that output
If `MR < MC` -> reduce output
Worked example with numbers: If MR at 1,000 units is $48 and MC is $48, the firm should hold at that level. If selling one more unit drops MR to $45 while MC stays at $48, expansion destroys value.

### Formula 2: Lerner Index
Formula: `L = (P - MC) / P`
Variables:
`L` = Lerner Index
`P` = price
`MC` = marginal cost
Why this formula exists: It answers how much pricing power a firm has relative to the price it charges.
How to interpret the output:
Value near 0 -> weak pricing power -> competitive behavior
Value moderate -> visible markup -> some power
Value high -> strong markup -> watch regulation and entry
Worked example with numbers: If price is $100 and MC is $70, the Lerner Index is 0.30. Decision: the firm has a 30 percent markup over marginal cost, which is meaningful but also visible to competitors and regulators.

### Formula 3: Deadweight Loss
Formula: `DWL ≈ 1/2 × (Q_c - Q_m) × (P_m - P_c)` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
`Q_c` = competitive quantity
`Q_m` = monopoly quantity
`P_c` = competitive price
`P_m` = monopoly price
Why this formula exists: It answers how much total surplus disappears when market power reduces trade.
How to interpret the output:
Small DWL -> limited welfare damage or small market
Large DWL -> serious welfare loss and stronger intervention case
Worked example with numbers: If competitive quantity is 100, monopoly quantity is 70, competitive price is $20, and monopoly price is $35, DWL is about $225. Decision: if the welfare loss is persistent, a regulator or board may need to intervene.

### Formula 4: Monopsony Hiring Rule
Formula: `MFC = MRP`
Variables:
`MFC` = marginal factor cost
`MRP` = marginal revenue product of the input
Why this formula exists: It answers the input level a dominant buyer should choose when it controls demand for labor or another input.
How to interpret the output:
If `MRP > MFC` -> hire more input
If `MRP = MFC` -> stop at that input level
If `MRP < MFC` -> reduce input demand
Worked example with numbers: If an extra engineer produces $1,200 of value and the marginal factor cost is $1,100, hire more. If the next engineer costs $1,400 in marginal factor cost, stop or redesign the sourcing model.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat a dominant seller like a competitive price taker. | Use MR=MC and then read price from the demand curve. |
| Assume lower input prices are always harmless savings. | Check whether monopsony power is damaging supplier quality or labor supply. |
| Ignore regulation risk when markup is visibly high. | Test whether the Lerner Index and market share will attract scrutiny. |
| Cut volume without quantifying welfare loss. | Estimate the deadweight loss and decide whether intervention is justified. |
| Negotiate only on unit price. | Negotiate total value, service levels, supply continuity, and ecosystem health. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Cloud platform with market power
Situation: A dominant B2B SaaS product has a sticky install base and can raise price without immediate churn. The product team wants to know whether to increase ARPU or expand volume with discounts. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Monopoly Price-Setting Spine and Lerner Index.
Analysis: If price is $100 and MC is $70, Lerner Index is 0.30. If MR falls to MC at 80,000 seats, that is the output level that maximizes profit. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If MR > MC, grow volume; if MR = MC, hold; if MR < MC, cut back. If the markup remains high and defensible, increase price carefully; if churn rises fast, use price segmentation instead.
Action: Run a price-elasticity test, segment enterprise accounts, and keep a regulator-ready rationale for any increase.

### Scenario 2: Dominant buyer in a labor market
Situation: A large AI-services company is the main employer in a local specialist labor market. HR wants to know whether a wage cut will improve economics or simply reduce hiring quality. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Monopsony Buying-Power Spine and Monopsony Hiring Rule.
Analysis: If the marginal revenue product of the next engineer is $1,200 and marginal factor cost is $1,100, hiring more is justified. If a wage cut raises turnover and pushes MFC above MRP, the apparent savings will reverse. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If MFC < MRP, hire more; if MFC = MRP, hold; if MFC > MRP, stop or redesign the offer.
Action: Compare pay, retention, and hiring lead time before changing compensation.

### Scenario 3: Marketplace take-rate dispute
Situation: A platform buyer sets higher seller fees and longer payout cycles, arguing that margin helps fund trust and support. Sellers claim the platform has buyer power and is squeezing the ecosystem. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Market Power Risk Matrix and Deadweight Loss.
Analysis: If the fee change increases platform margin by $15 million but causes seller exit and a welfare loss roughly equal to a $225 triangle per market segment, the short-term gain may not survive regulator or ecosystem backlash. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If the market power is creating persistent deadweight loss or supplier exit, redesign the fee structure; if the gain is small and demand is elastic, lower the take rate; if the platform is critical infrastructure, prepare for oversight.
Action: Rebuild seller economics, separate genuine service charges from extraction, and model churn before relaunching the fee schedule.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Map the market structure and identify whether the firm is the seller, the buyer, or both.
2. Estimate demand elasticity, input supply elasticity, and the size of any price-cost wedge.
3. Compute MR, MC, Lerner Index, and any welfare loss before recommending a price or wage move.
4. Compare short-run gain against long-run risks such as entry, churn, litigation, or regulation.
5. Test whether the market power is real, temporary, or just a result of poor benchmarking.
6. Build a negotiation playbook for procurement or compensation that protects ecosystem quality.
7. Escalate to legal, policy, or antitrust review if the markup or markdown looks persistent.
