# Metrics and KPIs

## Overview

Metrics and KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) are the numbers that tell you whether your business model is actually working. Metrics are any measurable data points. KPIs are the subset of metrics that directly reflect the health of your strategy. Tracking the wrong numbers gives a false sense of progress. Tracking the right ones lets you spot problems early, double down on what works, and make decisions based on evidence instead of opinion.

---

## Why It Matters

Without clear KPIs tied to your business model, teams default to vanity metrics — impressive-sounding numbers that do not drive real value. A social media company celebrating page views while ignoring daily active users may be growing a ghost town. The right KPIs connect day-to-day operations to strategic outcomes like profitability, retention, and market share.

## Key Principles

- Choose KPIs that directly reflect the value drivers of your specific business model, not generic industry benchmarks
- Limit the number of KPIs per team to three to five so people can actually focus on moving them
- Make KPIs visible and review them regularly — dashboards that nobody looks at are decoration, not management tools
- Update KPIs when the business model changes; stale metrics from a previous strategy will mislead the organization

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **KPI (Key Performance Indicator)** | A measurable value that shows how effectively the company is achieving its most important business objectives |
| **Leading indicator** | A metric that predicts future performance (e.g., pipeline size predicts future revenue) |
| **Lagging indicator** | A metric that confirms what has already happened (e.g., quarterly revenue reports past performance) |
| **Vanity metric** | A number that looks good on paper but does not correlate with meaningful business outcomes |

## Use Case

A subscription box company tracks three KPIs: customer acquisition cost, monthly churn rate, and customer lifetime value. These three numbers tell the leadership team whether the business is acquiring customers profitably, keeping them long enough to recoup acquisition costs, and generating net positive value over time.

## Scenario

> An e-commerce startup proudly reported 500,000 app downloads in its first quarter. But when the board looked deeper, only 8% of those users made a purchase and the repeat purchase rate was 2%. Downloads were a vanity metric masking a retention crisis. The company shifted its primary KPIs to first-purchase conversion rate and 90-day repeat purchase rate. Within two quarters, focused improvements on onboarding and loyalty rewards pushed repeat purchases to 14%, tripling actual revenue.

## Examples

- Netflix tracks hours viewed per subscriber as a KPI because engagement predicts retention, which is the lifeblood of its subscription model
- Uber monitors driver utilization rate (percentage of time a driver has a passenger) because high utilization keeps drivers earning enough to stay on the platform

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Metrics are the numbers you can measure; KPIs are the few that actually tell you whether the business model is working. For an IT, AI, Product, or Consulting leader, the point is not to watch every number but to track the leading indicator that predicts the result and the lagging indicator that confirms it. The source is explicit: avoid vanity metrics, keep the KPI set small, and update the dashboard when the model changes.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### KPI Hierarchy
**Purpose:** Separate activity metrics from the KPIs that actually steer the business model.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Inputs -> Leading indicators -> KPI -> Lagging indicators
traffic -> activation -> retention -> revenue
effort  -> adoption    -> churn     -> profit
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Input metrics: effort and activity inside the team.
Leading indicators: early signals that forecast the result.
Core KPI: the small number that reflects the business model's health.
Lagging indicators: output after the strategy has already played out.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS product team tracks trial activation as the leading indicator, monthly churn as the KPI, and quarterly revenue as the lagging indicator. The team then uses those numbers to decide whether onboarding, pricing, or product value needs the next fix.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When everyone is reporting different numbers and nobody agrees on what actually matters.

## 4. Formulas
[verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: KPI Quality Score = Strategic Relevance + Predictive Power + Actionability - Vanity Risk - Reporting Noise

Variables:
Strategic Relevance = how tightly the metric ties to the business model
Predictive Power = how well the metric forecasts future performance
Actionability = whether a team can influence the number
Vanity Risk = the chance the metric looks good without changing outcomes
Reporting Noise = clutter, duplicates, or unstable definitions

Why this formula exists: It helps leadership decide whether a metric deserves KPI status or should stay in the background.

How to interpret the output:
Score < 0 -> discard or demote the metric
Score 0-2 -> track it, but not as a KPI
Score > 2 -> keep it in the core dashboard

Worked example with numbers: If strategic relevance is 5, predictive power is 4, actionability is 4, vanity risk is 1, and reporting noise is 1, the score is 11. That means the metric belongs on the executive dashboard.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Don't track page views when revenue and retention are the real strategy. | Do pick KPIs that reflect the value driver of the model. |
| Don't give every team ten numbers to chase. | Do keep the KPI set to three to five. |
| Don't rely on a dashboard nobody reviews. | Do make the dashboard part of the operating cadence. |
| Don't keep the same KPIs after the business model changes. | Do refresh the metric set when the strategy shifts. |
| Don't celebrate activity that does not change outcomes. | Do separate vanity metrics from decision metrics. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

Scenario 1: SaaS activation versus revenue
Situation: An IT SaaS company celebrates signups, but most trial users never reach the core workflow. The product team wants a better operating metric.
Applicable framework/metric: KPI Hierarchy; trial activation, retention, and revenue.
Analysis: 1,000 signups convert to 220 activated users and 74 paid accounts. Trial activation is the leading indicator, while monthly churn is the KPI that tells the team whether the model is healthy.
Decision rule: If activation is above 30% and churn is below 5%, scale acquisition. If activation is high but churn remains elevated, fix onboarding. If both are weak, rethink the offer.
Action: Replace signup volume on the dashboard with activation and churn, then redesign onboarding around the core workflow.

Scenario 2: AI product usage versus retention
Situation: An AI product team has strong demo interest but weak repeat usage after the first week. Leadership needs one metric that predicts whether the product will stick.
Applicable framework/metric: Leading indicator and lagging indicator; weekly active use and 30-day retention.
Analysis: Weekly active use is the leading indicator because it shows whether people keep returning to the workflow. 30-day retention is the lagging indicator that confirms the habit.
Decision rule: If weekly active use rises above 40% and retention improves for two consecutive cohorts, keep investing. If active use is flat, rework the product. If retention falls even with high initial use, the value proposition is not landing.
Action: Focus the roadmap on the one workflow that drives repeat use and remove low-value features.

Scenario 3: Consulting dashboard with client outcomes
Situation: A consulting team tracks hours billed and deck delivery, but the client cares about adoption of the recommended operating changes. The team wants a better KPI set.
Applicable framework/metric: KPI Quality Score; client adoption, cycle time, and margin.
Analysis: Hours billed are easy to count but can become a vanity metric. Client adoption of recommendations and realized margin are more strategic because they tie to outcomes and repeat business.
Decision rule: If recommendation adoption exceeds 60% and the account margin stays healthy, keep the engagement model. If adoption is low, simplify the plan. If margins collapse, reprice or re-scope.
Action: Replace effort-based reporting with client outcome reporting in the weekly review.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Write the business model in one sentence before selecting metrics.
2. List every candidate metric and classify it as input, leading indicator, KPI, or lagging indicator.
3. Remove vanity metrics that do not drive decisions.
4. Limit each team to three to five KPIs and assign an owner to each one.
5. Put the dashboard into a fixed review cadence so it gets used.
6. Rebuild the metric set whenever the business model changes.
7. Test whether each KPI changes behavior before keeping it.
