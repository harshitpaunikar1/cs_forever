# Adjacency Expansion

## Overview

Adjacency expansion means growing a business by moving into areas that are close to — but distinct from — the company's current core. This could be a new customer segment, a new geography, a new product category, or a new step in the value chain. The key is that the expansion leverages existing strengths rather than starting from zero. Amazon moved from books to electronics to cloud computing, each step adjacent to the last.

---

## Why It Matters

Core markets eventually saturate. Companies that rely on a single product or segment become vulnerable to disruption and cyclical downturns. Adjacency expansion provides new growth without the extreme risk of entering completely unrelated markets. Research shows that companies growing through repeated adjacency moves deliver higher shareholder returns than those that diversify randomly.

## Key Principles

- Map your current capabilities and assets; expand where those assets give you an unfair advantage
- Move one adjacency at a time — stacking multiple simultaneous jumps multiplies risk
- Test the adjacency with a small pilot before committing full resources
- Ensure the new market is large enough to justify the investment and management attention

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Adjacency** | A market, product, or capability that is one logical step away from the company's current core |
| **Core-to-adjacent ratio** | The balance between investment in the existing core business and investment in adjacent growth |
| **Repeatable formula** | A proven process for entering adjacent markets that a company can reuse across multiple expansions |
| **Capability leverage** | Using strengths developed in the core business to gain competitive advantage in the new space |

## Use Case

A regional bank with strong small-business lending expertise expands into small-business insurance. Its loan officers already have deep relationships and financial data on these clients, so cross-selling insurance requires minimal new infrastructure and almost no customer acquisition cost.

## Scenario

> A specialty running-shoe brand had saturated its niche. Instead of jumping into unrelated fashion, it expanded into running apparel — adjacent because it shared the same customer, retail channels, and brand credibility. The apparel line reached profitability in its second year because the company did not need to build distribution or brand awareness from scratch. It then used the same playbook to add running accessories, growing revenue 40% over three years.

## Examples

- Google expanded from search to email, maps, and cloud storage — each adjacent to its core capability in large-scale data infrastructure and user-facing web services
- Peloton moved from stationary bikes to treadmills and then to a digital fitness subscription, each step leveraging its existing connected-fitness platform and loyal community

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Core-to-Adjacent Map
**Purpose:** Find the next growth move that stays close enough to reuse existing strengths.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Core business -> Adjacent product -> Adjacent segment -> Adjacent geography -> Adjacent step in value chain
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Core business, which supplies the capability base.
Component 2: Adjacent product, where the same capability is repackaged.
Component 3: Adjacent segment, where the buyer changes but the underlying strength stays relevant.
Component 4: Adjacent geography or value chain move, where the company extends reach or control.

IT/Product worked example: A SaaS company with a strong project-tracking app moves into lightweight time tracking, then into billing automation, because the same workflow data and user base can support each step. The decision is not "what is exciting" but "what reuses the most capability with the least new risk?"
When to pull this out in a meeting: When growth is needed but the team wants to avoid a random new bet.

### Capability Leverage Matrix
**Purpose:** Rank which adjacent ideas deserve investment first.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
High leverage / Low novelty   |  High leverage / High novelty
-----------------------------|------------------------------
Easy adjacency               |  Strategic adjacency

Low leverage / Low novelty    |  Low leverage / High novelty
-----------------------------|------------------------------
Maintenance move              |  Speculative move
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Capability leverage, meaning how much the core advantage transfers.
Component 2: Novelty, meaning how far the new market or product is from current practice.
Component 3: Easy adjacency, where execution should be quick and low risk.
Component 4: Strategic adjacency, where transfer is strong but the market requires more learning.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI platform vendor can reuse its model hosting, observability, and billing stack to launch a new inference product for enterprise teams. That is high leverage because the core platform transfers, but high novelty if the buyer, workflow, or compliance expectations change.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When multiple adjacent ideas are on the table and the team needs to sequence them.

### Adjacency Test
**Purpose:** Decide whether a proposed move is close enough to the core.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
If it uses existing capability? -> yes/no
If it reuses customer trust or channels? -> yes/no
If it needs new infrastructure? -> yes/no
If it needs new brand meaning? -> yes/no
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Capability reuse checks operational fit.
Component 2: Channel and trust reuse checks go-to-market fit.
Component 3: Infrastructure and brand change check whether the move becomes too expensive or too far.

IT/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm with strong product analytics expertise considers launching an internal benchmarking dashboard. It passes the adjacency test if it reuses the same analysts, client relationships, and reporting stack, but it fails if it needs a new delivery model that changes the firm’s economics.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is debating whether a new idea is actually adjacent or just a distraction.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: `Core-to-adjacent ratio = adjacent investment / core investment`
Variables:
`adjacent investment` = money, attention, and talent dedicated to the new move
`core investment` = money, attention, and talent dedicated to the existing business
Why this formula exists: It answers whether growth is balanced or whether the core is being ignored.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> the company is underinvesting in growth -> increase adjacent bets
Value moderate -> the company is balancing core and growth -> keep the mix
Value high -> the company may be overextending -> slow down or sequence the bets
Worked example with numbers: A SaaS company spends $2M on core product stability and $500k on a new workflow module. The ratio is 0.25, which suggests a cautious adjacent bet rather than a full pivot.

Formula: `Pilot conversion rate = pilot users who adopt / pilot users exposed`
Variables:
`pilot users who adopt` = users who continue after the pilot
`pilot users exposed` = users invited into the pilot
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the adjacency is worth scaling.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> the move is not resonating -> stop or redesign
Value moderate -> the move has promise -> refine and retest
Value high -> the move is ready to scale -> commit more resources
Worked example with numbers: A product team invites 200 customers to test a new analytics add-on and 60 keep using it. The conversion rate is 30%, enough to justify a second pilot with better onboarding.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Jump into an unrelated market | Move one adjacency at a time |
| Fund several risky expansions at once | Use a repeatable formula and sequence the bets |
| Treat all adjacent ideas as equal | Rank them by capability leverage |
| Skip a small test | Validate with a pilot before full rollout |
| Ignore the core business | Track the core-to-adjacent ratio deliberately |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: SaaS feature expansion
Situation: A project-management tool wants to add billing automation. The team believes it can reuse workflow data, permissions, and the existing customer base.
Applicable framework/metric: Core-to-Adjacent Map.
Analysis: If the billing feature reuses 80% of the existing stack and mainly changes the output layer, it is a clean adjacency. If it needs a new finance system and a new sales motion, it is too far from the core.
Decision rule: If capability reuse is above 70%, move forward. If between 40% and 70%, run a pilot. If below 40%, stop and redesign.
Action: Build a narrow beta for current customers and measure adoption.

Scenario 2: AI platform expansion
Situation: An AI vendor has a model hosting platform and wants to launch a workflow product for a specific team, such as support or operations.
Applicable framework/metric: Capability Leverage Matrix.
Analysis: The hosting and billing stack provide leverage, but the workflow product may require new UX, compliance, and messaging. That makes it a strategic adjacency, not a trivial one.
Decision rule: If leverage is high and novelty is low, scale immediately. If leverage is high and novelty is high, pilot first. If leverage is low, avoid the move.
Action: Reuse infrastructure, then test a narrow use case with one customer segment.

Scenario 3: Consulting offer expansion
Situation: A consulting practice that specializes in operating-model redesign wants to launch a benchmarking portal for clients.
Applicable framework/metric: Adjacency Test.
Analysis: The offer is adjacent if it reuses the same analysts, client relationships, and insight-generation process. It is not adjacent if it requires a full product company with a new sales and support motion.
Decision rule: If three or more adjacency-test questions are yes, proceed. If two are yes, pilot. If one or fewer are yes, reject.
Action: Package one benchmark report, test it with a few clients, and measure repeat usage.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Map the current core capabilities, customers, channels, and assets.
2. List the next 10 possible adjacent moves across product, segment, geography, and value chain.
3. Score each move by capability leverage, novelty, and pilotability.
4. Run a small pilot for the top-ranked adjacency with existing customers.
5. Measure conversion, retention, and support burden during the pilot.
6. Compare adjacent investment against core investment using the core-to-adjacent ratio.
7. Scale only the moves that reuse the most capability and prove demand quickly.
