# Rapid Prototyping

## Overview

Rapid prototyping is the practice of quickly building a simplified version of a product, service, or business model to test assumptions with real users. The prototype does not need to be polished or complete — it needs to be good enough to generate honest feedback. Paper mockups, landing pages, concierge services, and clickable wireframes are all valid prototypes. Speed matters more than perfection because the goal is learning, not launching.

---

## Why It Matters

Building a full product before testing it with customers is one of the most expensive mistakes a company can make. Rapid prototyping compresses the feedback loop from months to days. It saves money, surfaces design flaws early, and ensures you are building something people actually want. Companies that prototype fast fail cheap and succeed sooner.

## Key Principles

- Build the prototype in days, not months — if it takes too long, you are over-engineering it
- Put the prototype in front of real users, not just internal stakeholders, because internal opinions are biased
- Focus on testing the riskiest assumption first; the prototype should answer your biggest open question
- Iterate based on feedback: prototype, test, learn, revise, repeat

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Prototype** | A preliminary model of a product or service built to test a concept before full development |
| **Concierge MVP** | Manually delivering a service to early customers to test demand before automating the process |
| **Wizard of Oz prototype** | A prototype that appears automated to the user but is actually operated by a human behind the scenes |
| **Iteration cycle** | One complete loop of building, testing, gathering feedback, and revising the prototype |

## Use Case

A fintech startup wants to test whether freelancers will pay for automated tax estimation. Instead of building an app, the founders create a Google Form where freelancers submit their income data. The team manually calculates estimates and emails them back within 24 hours. If freelancers find the service valuable and are willing to pay, the team builds the real app.

## Scenario

> Zappos founder Nick Swinmurn tested whether people would buy shoes online by photographing shoes at local stores and posting them on a simple website. When someone ordered, he bought the shoes at retail and shipped them. He lost money on each sale, but the prototype proved that online shoe buying had real demand. That evidence justified building inventory systems, warehouses, and the full e-commerce operation that Amazon later acquired for over $1 billion.

## Examples

- Airbnb's founders prototyped their concept by renting out air mattresses in their own apartment during a conference, validating demand for peer-to-peer lodging before building a platform
- Food delivery startup DoorDash started by creating a simple landing page with restaurant menus and a phone number; the founders personally delivered orders to test whether the market existed

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Prototype Learning Loop
**Purpose:** Decide what to prototype first and how to learn from it quickly.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Biggest assumption -> Fast prototype -> Real users -> Feedback -> Revise
       |                   |               |          |         |
     risk first         days, not weeks   evidence   learn     repeat
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Assumption risk: how likely the idea fails if this assumption is wrong.
Speed: how fast the team can create a testable version.
User realism: whether the test is close to the real buying or adoption context.
Iteration quality: whether the feedback meaningfully changes the next version.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team tests an AI scheduling assistant with a Wizard of Oz prototype, where humans handle complex requests behind the scenes. The test answers whether users value the workflow enough to justify building the automation layer.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is debating a full build before validating demand.

## 4. Formulas
[verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: Learning Velocity = Insight Quality / Prototype Cycle Time

Variables:
Insight Quality = how much the prototype clarifies the risky assumption
Prototype Cycle Time = how long it takes to build, test, and revise

Why this formula exists: It rewards fast learning, not flashy prototypes.

How to interpret the output:
Score < 1 -> the team is moving too slowly or learning too little
Score 1-3 -> acceptable learning pace
Score > 3 -> strong prototyping discipline

Worked example with numbers: If insight quality is 9 and the cycle time is 3 days, the score is 3. That means the team is learning fast enough to continue, but it should keep shortening the loop.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Don't spend months building before testing demand. | Do build in days and test with real users early. |
| Don't prototype the easy part first. | Do test the riskiest assumption first. |
| Don't rely on internal opinions alone. | Do put the prototype in front of actual users. |
| Don't automate a service before proving people value it. | Do use a concierge MVP to validate demand first. |
| Don't stop after one round of feedback. | Do iterate until the evidence is strong enough to build. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

Scenario 1: AI assistant demand test
Situation: An AI startup wants to know whether teams will adopt a scheduling assistant. Building the full automation stack would take months, so the team needs a quick test.
Applicable framework/metric: Wizard of Oz prototype; task completion and repeat use.
Analysis: In the prototype, humans route requests behind the scenes while users think the assistant is automated. If task completion is high and repeat use rises after the first week, the automation is worth building.
Decision rule: If completion exceeds 80% and repeat use exceeds 35%, build the product. If completion is high but repeat use is weak, change the workflow. If both are low, stop.
Action: Keep the human layer, document the manual steps, and map the automation backlog from the repeated requests.

Scenario 2: Product landing page before engineering
Situation: A product team wants to launch a new feature for premium customers, but the engineering estimate is six weeks. Leadership wants evidence before committing the build.
Applicable framework/metric: Landing page prototype; click-through and signup rate.
Analysis: The team publishes a landing page describing the feature and measures whether target users click and sign up for early access. That reveals demand without full development cost.
Decision rule: If click-through is above 8% and signups are above 4%, move to a build sprint. If clicks are high but signups are low, the value prop needs work. If both are weak, revise the offer.
Action: Run two headline variants, collect objections, and only then scope engineering.

Scenario 3: Consulting concierge pilot
Situation: A consulting team wants to test a new analytics service for one client segment. Instead of building a platform, the team manually prepares the reports and delivers them directly.
Applicable framework/metric: Concierge MVP; willingness to pay and delivery time.
Analysis: The team measures whether clients pay for the manual service and how long delivery takes. If the manual version is valuable, the productization case is real.
Decision rule: If willingness to pay is clear and delivery time stays manageable, productize the service. If service load is too high, narrow the scope. If clients do not pay, do not automate.
Action: Productize only the repeated tasks, keep the bespoke steps manual until they prove repeat demand.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Write the riskiest assumption in one sentence.
2. Choose the lightest prototype that can test that assumption.
3. Put the prototype in front of real users, not just internal reviewers.
4. Measure the one or two signals that matter most.
5. Capture objections, confusion, and repeat behavior immediately.
6. Revise the prototype quickly and rerun the test.
7. Scale only after the evidence is strong enough to justify the build.
