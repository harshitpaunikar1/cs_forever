# Development Conversations

## Overview

Development conversations are structured discussions between a manager and an employee focused on the employee's growth, career aspirations, and skill-building. Unlike performance reviews that look backward at results, development conversations look forward at possibilities. They answer the question: "Where do you want to go, and how can we help you get there?"

---

## Why It Matters

Employees who see no growth path leave. Replacing a mid-level employee costs roughly 50-75% of their annual salary. Regular development conversations signal that the company is invested in the person, not just the role. They also help managers spot and retain high-potential talent before a recruiter does, and they build a stronger internal bench for succession planning.

## Key Principles

- Separate development conversations from performance evaluations and compensation talks
- Let the employee lead by sharing their aspirations first
- Discuss skills to build, experiences to seek, and relationships to develop
- Create a written development plan with specific actions and timelines
- Follow up at least quarterly to track progress and adjust the plan

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Individual development plan (IDP)** | A written document outlining an employee's growth goals, actions, and timelines |
| **Career lattice** | A model allowing lateral and diagonal career moves, not just vertical promotions |
| **Stretch assignment** | A project or role that pushes an employee beyond their current skill level |
| **Mentorship** | A relationship where a more experienced person guides a less experienced one's professional growth |

## Use Case

A finance team has three senior analysts who all want to become managers, but only one management role is expected to open in the next year. Development conversations reveal that one analyst actually prefers a technical specialist track. The manager adjusts plans accordingly: one pursues management readiness, one explores a lateral move to strategy, and the third deepens technical expertise.

## Scenario

> An engineering manager noticed her top performer growing disengaged. In a development conversation, the engineer said he wanted to move into product management but did not know how. Together they built a plan: shadow the PM for two sprints, lead one customer discovery session, and take an online product course. Nine months later, he transferred to the product team and his engagement scores returned to top-quartile levels.

## Examples

- A manager asks an employee three questions each quarter: "What skills do you want to build? What experiences would help? What support do you need from me?"
- A law firm pairs junior associates with senior partners for monthly career conversations separate from case supervision, giving associates a safe space to discuss long-term goals

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Development conversations are structured manager-employee discussions about growth, aspirations, and the experiences needed to move someone forward.
For IT, AI, product, and consulting leaders, the point is not a feel-good chat. It is a capability design tool that helps the business retain talent, build future leaders, and avoid losing people because the company never asked what they wanted next.
The practical decision: what growth path, stretch experience, and support plan will turn an engaged employee into a stronger future fit for the business.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Development Conversation Ladder
**Purpose:** Turn a vague career chat into a concrete plan.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Aspiration -> Gap -> Experience -> Support -> Review
```

Axes / Components explained:
Component 1: aspiration, meaning what the employee wants next.
Component 2: gap, meaning what is missing for that next step.
Component 3: experience, meaning the assignments that build the missing capability.
Component 4: support, meaning coaching, mentoring, and access.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A software engineer wants to become a product manager. The ladder shows a gap in customer discovery and trade-off framing, so the development plan adds shadowing, discovery sessions, and a product mentor.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the conversation stays abstract and no one leaves with next steps.

### Career Lattice Matrix
**Purpose:** Show that growth can be vertical, lateral, or diagonal.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Vertical: bigger scope / more authority
Lateral: new domain / same level
Diagonal: new domain + more scope
```

Axes / Components explained:
Component 1: scope, meaning breadth of responsibility.
Component 2: domain, meaning the functional area or skill family.
Component 3: readiness, meaning how close the employee is to the move.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A finance analyst may not be ready for people management, but can move laterally into strategy analytics or diagonal into product operations.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When employees assume the only growth path is promotion.

### Stretch Assignment Portfolio
**Purpose:** Balance growth experiences instead of overloading one high performer with random work.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Low risk / low stretch -> maintenance
High risk / low stretch -> delegation
Low risk / high stretch -> development sweet spot
High risk / high stretch -> overload
```

Axes / Components explained:
Component 1: stretch, meaning how much the work expands capability.
Component 2: risk, meaning how likely it is to fail or disrupt delivery.
Component 3: support, meaning whether coaching or guardrails exist.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting manager gives a promising analyst ownership of one workstream in a client workshop, but not the entire engagement, so the analyst grows without creating delivery risk.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When managers keep saying "we'll give them stretch" without checking workload risk.

## 4. Formulas

The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are analytical enrichments [verified from model knowledge, not source].

Formula: `Development Progress Score = completed development actions / planned development actions`
Variables:
completed development actions = actions finished on the plan
planned development actions = total actions agreed
Why this formula exists: It tells whether growth planning is actually happening.
How to interpret the output:
Below 0.50 = stalled
0.50 to 0.80 = in progress
Above 0.80 = strong execution
Worked example with numbers: If 4 of 5 actions are done, the score is 0.8. Decision: keep the plan but add the next stretch step.

Formula: `Readiness Gap = required evidence - current evidence`
Variables:
required evidence = proof needed for the next role
current evidence = what the employee has already demonstrated
Why this formula exists: It clarifies what is missing before promotion or transfer.
How to interpret the output:
Positive gap = more development needed
Zero gap = ready
Negative gap = overprepared relative to the target
Worked example with numbers: If a PM role needs 5 evidence points and the employee has 3, the gap is 2. Decision: add two targeted experiences.

Formula: `Internal Mobility Potential = skill match score x exposure score`
Variables:
skill match score = how well current skills fit future roles
exposure score = how many relevant experiences the employee has had
Why this formula exists: It estimates whether the person can move quickly into the next opportunity.
How to interpret the output:
Low = narrow path
Medium = possible with support
High = strong internal candidate
Worked example with numbers: A 0.8 skill match and 0.7 exposure score yield 0.56. Decision: build more exposure before expecting a move.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Turn the meeting into a disguised performance review | Keep growth and evaluation separate when possible |
| Ask only what the manager wants | Start with the employee's aspirations and constraints |
| Promise promotion without evidence | Tie growth to concrete readiness criteria |
| Offer random stretch work | Pick assignments that build the missing skill |
| Treat development as a once-a-year event | Revisit the plan regularly and update it |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Engineer moving toward product
Situation: A top software engineer wants to move into product management.
Applicable framework/metric: Development Conversation Ladder and Readiness Gap.
Analysis: The employee already has strong technical judgment, but lacks customer exposure and prioritization experience. The manager should not promise a transfer without building evidence first.
Decision rule: If readiness gap is greater than zero, convert the gap into assignments rather than vague encouragement.
Action: Add shadowing, one customer interview, and ownership of a small roadmap decision.

### Scenario 2: Analyst with two possible paths
Situation: A finance team has a senior analyst who is unsure about management.
Applicable framework/metric: Career Lattice Matrix and Internal Mobility Potential.
Analysis: The person may be more valuable in a specialist or strategy path than in a people-management path. The company benefits when the path fits the talent, not when it forces a default promotion.
Decision rule: If aspiration and capability point sideways, build a lateral move.
Action: Create a career lattice discussion with one specialist track and one leadership track.

### Scenario 3: Consulting team succession risk
Situation: A consulting practice has a star manager with no obvious successor.
Applicable framework/metric: Stretch Assignment Portfolio and Development Progress Score.
Analysis: The firm needs bench strength, so development should be intentional and measured. One high-potential consultant can be given client-facing leadership with guardrails to test readiness.
Decision rule: If a key role has weak bench strength, assign growth work early, not after the vacancy appears.
Action: Use quarterly check-ins and track the progress score for the successor candidate.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Ask the employee what they want next before giving advice.
2. Translate the aspiration into a specific role, skill, or experience target.
3. Identify the gap between current evidence and next-role readiness.
4. Choose one or two stretch assignments that close the biggest gap.
5. Add a mentor, sponsor, or coach where the employee needs support.
6. Write the plan down with dates and measurable milestones.
7. Review progress quarterly and adjust based on new evidence.
