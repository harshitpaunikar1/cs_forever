# Continuous Feedback

## Overview

Continuous feedback is the practice of giving and receiving performance-related input on an ongoing basis rather than saving it for annual reviews. It can happen in one-on-ones, after meetings, through digital tools, or in casual hallway conversations. The goal is to close the gap between when something happens and when the employee hears about it, so behavior can be adjusted in real time.

---

## Why It Matters

Feedback that arrives months after an event is nearly useless. The employee has forgotten the context, the manager has forgotten the details, and the conversation becomes a vague generalization. Continuous feedback keeps corrections small and timely. It also reinforces good performance immediately, which is more motivating than a generic "great year" comment in December.

## Key Principles

- Make feedback specific: describe the behavior, its impact, and the desired change
- Balance positive and corrective feedback; do not save praise only for formal reviews
- Use a simple framework like SBI (Situation-Behavior-Impact) to keep feedback clear
- Create psychological safety so employees feel comfortable giving upward feedback
- Use lightweight tools or rituals to make feedback a habit, not a special event

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **SBI model** | A feedback framework: describe the Situation, the Behavior observed, and its Impact |
| **Psychological safety** | A team climate where people feel safe to speak up without fear of punishment |
| **Real-time feedback** | Input delivered within hours or days of the observed behavior |
| **Feedback loop** | A recurring cycle where information about performance flows back to the performer |

## Use Case

A design agency notices that project post-mortems always surface the same complaints about unclear briefs. The agency introduces a five-minute feedback round at the end of every client kickoff meeting. Within three months, brief-related rework drops by 40% because issues are caught on day one instead of day thirty.

## Scenario

> A call center had annual reviews where agents received a single satisfaction score. Managers switched to weekly 15-minute coaching huddles reviewing three recorded calls. After six months, first-call resolution rose from 68% to 81%, and agent satisfaction scores climbed because people felt their growth was taken seriously.

## Examples

- A product manager sends a two-sentence Slack message to a developer after a sprint demo: "Your live debugging during the demo saved us 20 minutes of confusion. That kind of transparency builds trust with stakeholders."
- A sales director holds a five-minute debrief after every lost deal, asking the rep what they would do differently, and sharing one tactical suggestion

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Feedback Loop Map
**Purpose:** Show how feedback moves from observation to behavior change.

```text
Observe -> Describe -> Discuss -> Decide -> Reinforce
```

Observe: capture the behavior while it is fresh.
Describe: state what happened without loading in assumptions.
Discuss: connect the behavior to impact and expectations.
Decide: agree on the next action.
Reinforce: check whether the change actually happened.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: In a product retro, a PM notices that design feedback is arriving too late to change sprint scope. The loop helps the team move from complaint to a concrete adjustment, such as earlier review checkpoints.

When to pull this out in a meeting: when people keep repeating the same issue and no one can explain why the loop is broken.

### SBI-to-Feedforward Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether the moment needs correction, reinforcement, or future guidance.

```text
                Past behavior focus     Future behavior focus
Positive            Reinforce               Feedforward
Corrective          SBI correction          Action plan
```

SBI correction: name the event, behavior, and impact.
Feedforward: describe what to do next time.
Reinforce: make the good behavior visible.
Action plan: agree on a next-step experiment or habit.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consultant handles a client escalation well during a tense steering call. The manager should reinforce the behavior and feedforward how to do it again in the next meeting rather than saving the praise for a quarterly review.

When to pull this out in a meeting: when a leader needs to choose between praise, correction, or development.

### Feedback Cadence Grid
**Purpose:** Match feedback frequency to the type of work.

```text
High uncertainty / High collaboration -> frequent feedback
High uncertainty / Low collaboration  -> scheduled check-ins
Low uncertainty / High collaboration  -> weekly coaching
Low uncertainty / Low collaboration   -> lighter cadence
```

High uncertainty: work where scope changes often.
High collaboration: work requiring lots of handoffs.
Scheduled check-ins: defined recurring sessions.
Weekly coaching: short, high-signal conversations.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI product team in model experimentation needs more frequent feedback than a stable operations team. The grid prevents managers from using one cadence for every function.

When to pull this out in a meeting: when a manager says “we already meet once a month” as if that fits every team.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below are practical business-use metrics [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Feedback Latency
Formula: `Feedback Latency = Time of feedback - Time of observed event`

Variables:
Time of feedback = when the conversation happens
Time of observed event = when the behavior was seen

Why this formula exists: it measures how quickly learning can happen.
How to interpret the output:
Short latency -> higher learning value
Long latency -> lower context and weaker change
Worked example with numbers: If feedback happens 2 days after a demo issue, latency is 2 days.

### Formula 2: Feedback-to-Action Rate
Formula: `Feedback-to-Action Rate = Actions completed / Feedback items agreed`

Variables:
Actions completed = feedback items that led to a visible change
Feedback items agreed = total items the manager and employee accepted

Why this formula exists: it checks whether feedback produces behavior change.
How to interpret the output:
Low rate -> talk is not translating into action
High rate -> feedback is operationally useful
Worked example with numbers: 8 completed actions out of 10 agreed items = 80%.

### Formula 3: Coaching Coverage
Formula: `Coaching Coverage = Employees receiving regular coaching / Total employees`

Variables:
Employees receiving regular coaching = people with a defined cadence
Total employees = people in the team

Why this formula exists: it shows whether feedback is systematized or random.
How to interpret the output:
Low coverage -> inconsistent management
High coverage -> stronger learning culture
Worked example with numbers: 24 coached employees out of 30 = 80% coverage.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Wait for annual reviews to correct repeated issues | Give feedback while the event is still fresh |
| Make feedback vague or personality-based | Describe the situation, behavior, and impact |
| Save praise for formal reviews only | Reinforce good behavior immediately |
| Treat feedback as a one-way manager activity | Encourage upward feedback and dialogue |
| Use feedback to punish instead of improve | Use it to shorten the learning loop |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Product demo issues keep repeating
Situation: A product team keeps missing key points in customer demos, but the manager only hears about it after the quarter ends.
Applicable framework/metric: Feedback Loop Map and Feedback Latency.
Analysis: The late review cycle means the team repeats the same mistake for weeks. A short post-demo debrief and a simple checklist cut the error pattern quickly.
Decision rule: If latency is measured in weeks, shorten it to days or hours.
Action: Add a 10-minute demo debrief and assign one concrete improvement per meeting.

### Scenario 2: Consulting team wants better meeting behavior
Situation: A consulting associate talks over peers in client workshops, creating friction.
Applicable framework/metric: SBI-to-Feedforward Matrix and Feedback-to-Action Rate.
Analysis: The manager should use a specific SBI conversation, then agree on a future behavior such as waiting two beats before responding.
Decision rule: If the behavior is observable, name it directly and tie it to client impact.
Action: Follow the next workshop with a quick check-in to verify the change.

### Scenario 3: Engineering manager needs a coaching habit
Situation: An engineering lead only gives feedback during escalation meetings.
Applicable framework/metric: Feedback Cadence Grid and Coaching Coverage.
Analysis: The team is getting judgment, not coaching. A recurring weekly cadence with each direct report creates a more reliable feedback loop.
Decision rule: If coaching coverage is below 70%, managers are probably too reactive.
Action: Schedule recurring 1:1s and log one development note per conversation.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Choose a short feedback format such as SBI or feedforward.
2. Tie feedback to specific observed behavior, not a general impression.
3. Deliver corrective input as soon as possible after the event.
4. Reinforce good behavior with equal discipline, not just corrections.
5. Build a regular cadence of one-on-ones, retros, or coaching huddles.
6. Ask for upward feedback so managers are not blind to their own impact.
7. Track whether feedback actually changed behavior over time.
