# Organizational Culture

## Overview

Organizational culture is the set of shared values, beliefs, norms, and behaviors that shape how people work inside a company. It is "how things are done here." Culture shows up in how decisions get made, how conflicts are handled, how meetings run, and what gets rewarded or punished. It is both visible (dress code, office layout) and invisible (unwritten rules, assumptions).

---

## Why It Matters

Culture drives behavior more powerfully than any policy manual. A toxic culture produces high turnover, low innovation, and ethical lapses no matter how good the strategy looks on paper. A strong, healthy culture attracts talent, accelerates execution, and creates resilience during tough times. Ignoring culture is like ignoring the current while trying to steer a boat.

## Key Principles

- Culture is set by what leaders tolerate and reward, not by what they say in town halls
- Measure culture through employee surveys, turnover patterns, and behavioral data, not just mission statements
- Subcultures will always form in large organizations; manage them, do not fight them
- Changing culture requires changing visible behaviors first, then values follow
- Hire and promote for cultural contribution, not just cultural fit

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Artifacts** | Visible elements of culture such as office design, dress code, and rituals |
| **Espoused values** | The values a company publicly claims to hold |
| **Basic assumptions** | Deep, unconscious beliefs that truly drive behavior |
| **Cultural fit** | The degree to which a person's values and work style align with the organization's culture |

## Use Case

A fast-growing startup realizes its "move fast and break things" culture is causing quality problems now that it has enterprise clients. Leadership deliberately shifts the culture by adding code-review requirements, celebrating bug-free releases publicly, and adjusting promotion criteria to include reliability. Within a year, critical bugs drop 60%.

## Scenario

> A 200-person manufacturing firm had a blame culture where mistakes led to public reprimands. A new plant manager introduced a "no-blame incident review" process and started sharing her own mistakes at weekly meetings. Within a year, near-miss reporting increased 300%, and the plant's safety incident rate fell to the lowest in the company.

## Examples

- A consulting firm reinforces its collaborative culture by tying 20% of partner bonuses to cross-practice project referrals
- A hospital posts its core values at every nurses' station and asks staff to nominate colleagues who demonstrate those values each month, with winners recognized at grand rounds

### Psychological Safety vs Accountability Grid
**Purpose:** Avoid the false choice between being nice and demanding results.

Component 2: accountability, meaning people are still expected to deliver.
Component 3: fear, meaning silence and risk hiding.

## 4. Formulas

The source does not provide explicit formulas; the calculations below are added for operational use [verified from model knowledge, not source].

Formula: `Culture Health Score = (Speak-Up Rate + Recognition Coverage + Values Alignment) / 3`
Variables:
Speak-Up Rate = normalized measure of issue raising and idea sharing
Recognition Coverage = percent of teams receiving reinforcement for desired behavior
Values Alignment = score showing how closely behavior matches stated values
Why this formula exists: It gives a simple view of whether the culture is functioning or merely described.
How to interpret the output:
Below 0.60 = culture risk
0.60 to 0.80 = mixed and needs attention
Above 0.80 = healthy operating culture
Worked example with numbers: If speak-up rate is 0.75, recognition coverage is 0.70, and values alignment is 0.85, the culture health score is 0.77. That is acceptable but not yet strong.

Formula: `Culture Drift Index = 1 - (Observed Behavior Score / Stated Values Score)`
Variables:
Observed Behavior Score = how often people actually act on the value
Stated Values Score = how strongly the organization claims the value matters
Why this formula exists: It shows the gap between the culture story and the lived culture.
How to interpret the output:
Close to 0 = little drift
0.10 to 0.25 = meaningful drift
Above 0.25 = serious culture mismatch
Worked example with numbers: If the company says customer obsession is 100 but observed behavior is 72, the drift index is 0.28. That indicates the value is more slogan than practice.

Formula: `Turnover Pressure in Critical Roles = Critical Role Attrition / Total Critical Roles`
Variables:
Critical Role Attrition = number of key people who left
Total Critical Roles = number of identified critical roles
Why this formula exists: It connects culture problems to business risk.
How to interpret the output:
Below 0.10 = manageable
0.10 to 0.20 = watch carefully
Above 0.20 = strategic risk
Worked example with numbers: If 3 of 12 critical product and engineering leaders leave, turnover pressure is 0.25. That is a direct signal that the culture is not retaining core talent.

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Assume values posters create culture | Change behaviors, rewards, and rituals |
| Tolerate a mismatch between words and incentives | Align promotions and recognition with values |
| Treat culture as one company-wide blob | Map subcultures by team or site |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Product team speed versus quality
Situation: A product organization is shipping fast but defect rates are rising.
Applicable framework/metric: Culture Signal Ladder, Culture Drift Index.
Analysis: The team celebrates launch velocity, but the norms punish delay and ignore post-release quality work. The stated value of customer obsession is being undercut by the actual reward system.
Decision rule: If drift index exceeds 0.20, revise rituals and incentives immediately.
Action: Add quality reviews, post-launch retros, and rewards for defect reduction.

### Scenario 2: AI incident reporting
Situation: An AI operations team is seeing repeated model failures but few incident reports.
Applicable framework/metric: Psychological Safety vs Accountability Grid, Speak-Up Rate.
Analysis: The low report volume suggests people may be hiding problems. If safety is low, engineers will silence issues rather than surface them early.
Decision rule: If speak-up rate is below target for two cycles, run a blame-free review and leader walk-through.
Action: Separate learning reviews from disciplinary action and publish fixes visibly.

### Scenario 3: Consulting firm values and promotion
Situation: A consulting firm says it values collaboration but partner promotions are based mainly on individual revenue.
Applicable framework/metric: Values-Behavior Matrix, Turnover Pressure in Critical Roles.
Analysis: Collaboration is an espoused value, but the promotion logic reinforces solo performance. That mismatch can push team players out and reduce cross-sell.
Decision rule: If high-performing collaborators are leaving while individual rainmakers are promoted, the matrix is in hypocrisy risk.
Action: Add cross-practice referrals, team delivery, and client development contributions to promotion criteria.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Identify the behaviors the business actually needs.
2. Compare those behaviors with what leaders reward today.
3. Map visible artifacts, rituals, and norms that reinforce or block the desired culture.
4. Measure speak-up, turnover, recognition, and values alignment.
5. Redesign incentives, meetings, and promotion criteria to match the culture target.
6. Use retrospectives and incident reviews to convert mistakes into learning.
7. Watch subcultures separately rather than assuming one culture score fits all.
8. Recheck the culture after any major strategy, product, or org change.
