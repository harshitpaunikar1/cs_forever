# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and concepts covering people resources management (Q1 – Q70).

## Q1. What is strategic human resource management?

**Level:** Basic

Strategic human resource management (SHRM) is the practice of aligning HR policies and practices with the long-term goals of the business. Rather than treating HR as a support function, SHRM positions workforce decisions as core strategic choices that directly affect competitive advantage.

**Real-life applications:**
- Aligning hiring plans with a company's three-year growth targets
- Linking training investments to specific revenue or efficiency goals
- Ensuring compensation structures reward behaviors the business strategy requires
- Including the HR leader in executive strategic planning sessions
- Redesigning roles when the company pivots to a new market segment

**Key concepts:** `strategic alignment`, `human capital`, `competitive advantage`, `workforce strategy`, `vertical alignment`

---

## Q2. What is the difference between vertical and horizontal alignment in HR?

**Level:** Basic

Vertical alignment is the fit between HR practices and the overall business strategy. Horizontal alignment is the internal consistency among different HR practices themselves. Both must exist for SHRM to work. If hiring targets growth talent but compensation rewards stability, the HR system works against itself.

**Real-life applications:**
- Checking that recruitment criteria match the skills needed for the company's strategic goals
- Ensuring performance metrics reinforce the same behaviors that training programs teach
- Auditing whether benefits and rewards attract the same type of talent the business strategy demands
- Reviewing onboarding content to confirm it reflects current strategic priorities
- Aligning promotion criteria with the leadership competencies the strategy requires

**Key concepts:** `vertical alignment`, `horizontal alignment`, `HR system coherence`, `strategic fit`, `internal consistency`

---

## Q3. What is an HR scorecard?

**Level:** Basic

An HR scorecard is a measurement tool that links HR activities to business performance metrics. It tracks indicators like time-to-fill, employee engagement, turnover cost, and training ROI alongside financial outcomes. It helps HR leaders demonstrate their function's contribution to the bottom line in concrete numbers.

**Real-life applications:**
- Presenting quarterly HR metrics to the board alongside financial results
- Tracking the correlation between engagement scores and customer satisfaction
- Measuring cost-per-hire against quality-of-hire to optimize recruiting spend
- Comparing training investment per employee to productivity improvements
- Identifying departments where high turnover correlates with missed revenue targets

**Key concepts:** `HR scorecard`, `HR analytics`, `business metrics`, `workforce measurement`, `ROI`

---

## Q4. What is human capital?

**Level:** Basic

Human capital is the economic value of employees' skills, knowledge, experience, and health. It is an intangible asset that grows when companies invest in training, education, and well-being. Unlike machines that depreciate, human capital can appreciate over time if properly developed and retained.

**Real-life applications:**
- Calculating the cost of losing a senior engineer with ten years of institutional knowledge
- Justifying tuition reimbursement programs as investments in human capital
- Valuing a company higher during acquisition because of its skilled workforce
- Measuring the return on leadership development programs
- Comparing human capital investment levels across competitors in the same industry

**Key concepts:** `human capital`, `intangible assets`, `knowledge economy`, `talent investment`, `workforce value`

---

## Q5. What is workforce planning?

**Level:** Basic

Workforce planning is the process of determining how many people you need, with what skills, in which locations, and by when. It matches labor supply to labor demand so the business is never understaffed or overstaffed. Effective workforce planning typically looks 12 to 36 months ahead and adjusts as conditions change.

**Real-life applications:**
- Forecasting seasonal hiring needs for a retail chain before the holiday rush
- Mapping retirement timelines to identify roles that will need successors within two years
- Planning headcount increases tied to a new product launch timeline
- Modeling the impact of automation on warehouse staffing levels
- Budgeting for contingent workers during demand spikes

**Key concepts:** `workforce planning`, `headcount`, `labor supply`, `labor demand`, `capacity planning`

---

## Q6. What is a skills inventory?

**Level:** Basic

A skills inventory is a database or catalog of the competencies, certifications, training, and experience held by current employees. It gives leaders a clear picture of what capabilities already exist in the organization. This makes it easier to identify gaps, redeploy talent, and make informed hiring decisions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Quickly finding employees with project management certifications when a new initiative launches
- Identifying which departments lack critical technical skills before budget season
- Matching internal candidates to open roles based on verified competencies
- Tracking the decay of certifications that require periodic renewal
- Planning training programs based on the most common skill gaps across the organization

**Key concepts:** `skills inventory`, `competency mapping`, `talent database`, `capability assessment`, `skills gap`

---

## Q7. What is gap analysis in workforce planning?

**Level:** Basic

Gap analysis compares the workforce you have today against the workforce you will need in the future. It identifies shortfalls in headcount, skills, experience, or location. Once gaps are mapped, HR can decide whether to fill them through hiring, training, redeployment, or automation.

**Real-life applications:**
- Discovering the company needs 15 data scientists but only has 4 on staff
- Identifying that half the sales team lacks negotiation training needed for enterprise deals
- Finding that a new office in another country requires bilingual managers not currently in the workforce
- Spotting that upcoming retirements will leave three critical engineering roles unfilled
- Recognizing that growth plans require more middle managers than the current pipeline can produce

**Key concepts:** `gap analysis`, `talent shortfall`, `future-state workforce`, `supply-demand mismatch`, `workforce diagnostics`

---

## Q8. What is a contingent workforce?

**Level:** Basic

A contingent workforce consists of temporary, contract, freelance, or gig workers hired to meet specific needs without permanent employment commitments. Companies use contingent workers to handle demand spikes, access specialized skills, or test roles before converting them to full-time. Managing this workforce well means balancing cost savings with quality and compliance risks.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hiring contract developers to build a mobile app without adding permanent headcount
- Using temp workers in warehouses during peak shipping season
- Engaging a freelance graphic designer for a product rebrand project
- Staffing a call center with seasonal agents during tax filing season
- Bringing in a contract CFO while searching for a permanent hire

**Key concepts:** `contingent workforce`, `temporary staffing`, `gig economy`, `flexible labor`, `workforce agility`

---

## Q9. What is talent acquisition?

**Level:** Basic

Talent acquisition is the full process of finding, attracting, evaluating, and hiring the right people for an organization. It goes beyond posting a job ad and reviewing resumes. A strong talent acquisition function builds candidate pipelines proactively, creates compelling hiring experiences, and ensures new hires match both the role and the culture.

**Real-life applications:**
- Building relationships with university career centers before graduation season
- Maintaining a database of past applicants who were strong but not selected for prior roles
- Designing a structured interview process that reduces hiring bias
- Creating referral programs that incentivize employees to recommend qualified candidates
- Attending industry conferences to network with passive candidates

**Key concepts:** `talent acquisition`, `recruiting`, `candidate pipeline`, `hiring process`, `talent sourcing`

---

## Q10. What is a talent pipeline?

**Level:** Basic

A talent pipeline is a pool of pre-qualified candidates who have been identified and nurtured before a role opens. It ensures the company does not start from scratch every time it needs to hire. Maintaining a pipeline shortens time-to-fill, improves candidate quality, and reduces dependency on expensive job postings or agencies.

**Real-life applications:**
- Keeping in touch with strong candidates who declined a previous offer
- Running quarterly networking events to meet potential future hires
- Building relationships with bootcamp graduates six months before graduation
- Tracking intern performance to identify potential full-time offers
- Maintaining a CRM with candidates segmented by skill set and seniority level

**Key concepts:** `talent pipeline`, `candidate relationship management`, `proactive recruiting`, `pipeline nurturing`, `time-to-fill`

---

## Q11. What is a passive candidate?

**Level:** Basic

A passive candidate is someone currently employed who is not actively looking for a new job but may be open to the right opportunity. Most high-performing professionals fall into this category. Reaching them requires different tactics than job boards: networking, referrals, social media outreach, and compelling employer branding.

**Real-life applications:**
- A recruiter reaching out to a software engineer on LinkedIn who has not updated their profile in years
- An executive search firm approaching a VP at a competitor for a C-suite role
- An employee referring a former colleague who is happy at their current job but intrigued by the opportunity
- A company publishing thought-leadership content that attracts senior professionals
- A hiring manager connecting with industry experts at conferences

**Key concepts:** `passive candidate`, `talent sourcing`, `headhunting`, `candidate engagement`, `employer branding`

---

## Q12. What is a structured interview?

**Level:** Basic

A structured interview is an interview format where every candidate is asked the same predetermined questions and evaluated using the same scoring rubric. It reduces interviewer bias, improves prediction of job performance, and makes hiring decisions more defensible. Research consistently shows structured interviews are far more reliable than unstructured ones.

**Real-life applications:**
- Creating a standard question set for all sales associate interviews across store locations
- Training interviewers to use a 1-5 rating scale for each competency
- Reducing legal risk by ensuring all candidates receive the same evaluation process
- Comparing candidates objectively because they all answered the same questions
- Improving inter-rater reliability by calibrating interviewers on sample responses

**Key concepts:** `structured interview`, `interview rubric`, `hiring bias`, `predictive validity`, `standardized evaluation`

---

## Q13. What is quality of hire?

**Level:** Basic

Quality of hire is a metric that measures how well new hires perform after joining and how long they stay. It is often calculated by combining performance ratings, time to full productivity, hiring manager satisfaction, and retention at the 12-month mark. It tells the company whether its recruiting process is bringing in the right people, not just filling seats.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking which recruiting sources produce employees with the highest first-year performance ratings
- Comparing quality-of-hire scores across different recruiters to identify best practices
- Identifying patterns where certain interview questions predict strong or weak new-hire performance
- Justifying investment in a longer but more thorough interview process
- Evaluating whether a referral program yields better hires than job board postings

**Key concepts:** `quality of hire`, `new-hire performance`, `retention rate`, `recruiting effectiveness`, `hiring metrics`

---

## Q14. What is employer branding?

**Level:** Basic

Employer branding is the reputation a company builds as a place to work. It covers everything job seekers see and hear: careers pages, employee reviews on Glassdoor, social media presence, and word of mouth from current employees. A strong employer brand attracts more and better applicants, reduces recruiting costs, and makes it easier to close offers.

**Real-life applications:**
- Publishing employee testimonial videos on the company careers page
- Responding publicly to negative Glassdoor reviews with action plans
- Showcasing company culture on Instagram and LinkedIn
- Highlighting benefits and growth opportunities in job descriptions
- Running an employee ambassador program where staff share their experiences

**Key concepts:** `employer branding`, `employee value proposition`, `talent attraction`, `reputation management`, `candidate perception`

---

## Q15. What is an employee value proposition?

**Level:** Basic

An employee value proposition (EVP) is the unique set of benefits, experiences, and rewards a company offers employees in exchange for their skills and contributions. It answers the question: "Why should someone work here instead of somewhere else?" A compelling EVP differentiates the employer in the talent market and drives both attraction and retention.

**Real-life applications:**
- Articulating why a startup with lower base pay offers faster career growth and equity upside
- Surveying current employees to discover which benefits they value most
- Tailoring EVP messaging for different candidate segments like engineers versus marketers
- Including the EVP in job postings, career pages, and recruiter outreach scripts
- Benchmarking the EVP against competitors to identify unique selling points

**Key concepts:** `employee value proposition`, `EVP`, `total rewards`, `employer differentiation`, `talent attraction`

---

## Q16. What is employer Net Promoter Score?

**Level:** Basic

Employer Net Promoter Score (eNPS) is a survey metric that measures how likely employees are to recommend the company as a place to work. Employees answer on a 0-10 scale. Scores of 9-10 are promoters, 7-8 are passives, and 0-6 are detractors. The eNPS is calculated as the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running a quarterly eNPS pulse survey to track trends in employee sentiment
- Comparing eNPS across departments to identify pockets of dissatisfaction
- Using eNPS trends to evaluate the impact of new policies or leadership changes
- Benchmarking against industry averages to understand competitive positioning
- Following up with detractors to understand their concerns and take action

**Key concepts:** `eNPS`, `employee satisfaction`, `engagement survey`, `promoter`, `detractor`

---

## Q17. What is a performance appraisal system?

**Level:** Basic

A performance appraisal system is the structured process a company uses to evaluate employee performance. It typically includes setting expectations, gathering evidence, rating performance against criteria, and holding a formal review conversation. The system provides a basis for compensation decisions, promotions, development plans, and performance improvement actions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Conducting annual reviews using a standardized rating scale across all departments
- Using self-assessments alongside manager evaluations for a more complete picture
- Feeding appraisal data into compensation models to determine merit increases
- Identifying underperformers who need coaching or performance improvement plans
- Recognizing top performers for bonuses, promotions, or special projects

**Key concepts:** `performance appraisal`, `evaluation process`, `rating scale`, `review cycle`, `performance criteria`

---

## Q18. What is 360-degree feedback?

**Level:** Basic

360-degree feedback is a review process that collects performance input from an employee's manager, peers, direct reports, and sometimes clients or external partners. It provides a more complete picture than manager-only reviews because it captures how the employee behaves across different relationships. It is especially useful for assessing leadership behaviors and interpersonal skills.

**Real-life applications:**
- Giving a manager feedback from their direct reports on communication and delegation skills
- Identifying blind spots where an employee's self-assessment differs sharply from peer perceptions
- Using 360 data to design individualized leadership development plans
- Helping senior leaders understand how their style is experienced across the organization
- Collecting client feedback for consultants to improve service delivery

**Key concepts:** `360-degree feedback`, `multi-rater assessment`, `peer feedback`, `upward feedback`, `self-awareness`

---

## Q19. What is a calibration session?

**Level:** Basic

A calibration session is a meeting where managers come together to compare and discuss performance ratings across their teams. The purpose is to ensure consistency and fairness. Without calibration, one manager might rate everyone as "exceeds expectations" while another holds a tighter standard, creating inequity in pay and promotion decisions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Holding a department-wide calibration meeting before finalizing annual ratings
- Presenting examples of employee work to justify ratings to peer managers
- Identifying and correcting grade inflation in teams with lenient managers
- Ensuring diverse employees are rated fairly by checking for patterns of bias
- Aligning definitions of each rating level across the organization

**Key concepts:** `calibration`, `rating consistency`, `performance standards`, `manager alignment`, `fairness`

---

## Q20. What is recency bias in performance reviews?

**Level:** Basic

Recency bias is the tendency to base a performance review on only the most recent weeks or months of work rather than the full review period. A strong finish can mask a weak year, and a recent mistake can overshadow months of strong results. Combating recency bias requires managers to keep notes throughout the year, not just before review time.

**Real-life applications:**
- A manager remembering only the last quarter when writing a review covering twelve months
- An employee who had a great first half but a poor recent project receiving an unfairly low rating
- Implementing quarterly check-ins so notes exist for the entire review period
- Using a performance journal where managers log key observations monthly
- Designing review forms that prompt managers to comment on each quarter separately

**Key concepts:** `recency bias`, `cognitive bias`, `performance documentation`, `fair evaluation`, `review period`

---

## Q21. What are SMART goals?

**Level:** Basic

SMART goals are goals that are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. This framework forces clarity by requiring that every goal answers what will be done, how success will be measured, whether it is realistic, why it matters, and when it must be completed. SMART goals are among the most widely used goal-setting tools in business.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting a sales target: "Close 15 new enterprise accounts worth at least $50K each by December 31"
- Setting a recruiting goal: "Reduce time-to-fill for engineering roles from 60 to 40 days by Q3"
- Setting a training goal: "Complete AWS certification by June 30 to support the cloud migration project"
- Setting a customer service goal: "Achieve a 90% first-call resolution rate by end of quarter"
- Replacing vague goals like "improve communication" with measurable actions

**Key concepts:** `SMART goals`, `goal clarity`, `measurable objectives`, `time-bound targets`, `achievability`

---

## Q22. What are OKRs?

**Level:** Basic

OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) are a goal-setting framework that pairs a qualitative objective with two to five measurable key results. The objective describes what you want to achieve in plain language. The key results define how you will know you achieved it. OKRs are typically set quarterly and reviewed regularly to maintain focus and adaptability.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting a company objective like "Become the market leader in customer satisfaction" with key results such as NPS above 70 and support response time under 2 hours
- Cascading OKRs from executive level to team level to ensure alignment
- Reviewing OKRs at monthly all-hands meetings to track progress publicly
- Using OKRs to prioritize projects by asking which ones contribute most to key results
- Scoring OKRs at the end of the quarter to learn what worked and what did not

**Key concepts:** `OKR`, `objectives`, `key results`, `goal alignment`, `quarterly planning`

---

## Q23. What is a KPI?

**Level:** Basic

A Key Performance Indicator (KPI) is a metric used to evaluate how effectively a person, team, or organization is achieving important objectives. KPIs are quantifiable and trackable. Good KPIs are directly tied to outcomes that matter, not just activities that are easy to count. They tell you whether you are on track or falling behind.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking monthly recurring revenue as a KPI for a subscription business
- Using employee turnover rate as a KPI for the HR department
- Monitoring defect rate as a KPI for a manufacturing quality team
- Reviewing average deal size as a KPI for a sales team
- Displaying KPI dashboards in team meetings to drive accountability

**Key concepts:** `KPI`, `performance metric`, `measurement`, `tracking`, `business outcome`

---

## Q24. What is cascading goals?

**Level:** Basic

Cascading goals is the process of breaking company-level goals into department, team, and individual goals so that every person's work connects back to the organization's priorities. It creates a clear line of sight from an individual contributor's daily tasks to the CEO's strategic objectives. Without cascading, departmental goals may drift out of alignment.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company goal of 20% revenue growth becoming a sales team goal of adding 50 new clients and a marketing team goal of generating 500 qualified leads
- Breaking a company-wide customer satisfaction goal into specific targets for product, support, and delivery teams
- Ensuring each individual's quarterly goals map to at least one team-level objective
- Using cascading to resolve conflicts when two teams set goals that work against each other
- Reviewing cascaded goals at the start of each quarter to check alignment

**Key concepts:** `cascading goals`, `goal alignment`, `line of sight`, `strategic execution`, `organizational coherence`

---

## Q25. What is continuous feedback?

**Level:** Basic

Continuous feedback is the practice of giving and receiving performance input on an ongoing basis rather than only during formal annual reviews. It includes comments after meetings, quick messages, one-on-one check-ins, and peer recognition. The goal is to close the time gap between a behavior and the response to it, enabling real-time course correction and reinforcement.

**Real-life applications:**
- A manager sending a quick Slack message to praise an employee's presentation right after the meeting
- Holding weekly one-on-ones that include a brief feedback exchange
- Using an app like Lattice or 15Five for asynchronous feedback sharing
- Conducting brief post-project retrospectives where team members share what went well and what to improve
- Asking for upward feedback at the end of each sprint

**Key concepts:** `continuous feedback`, `real-time input`, `timely recognition`, `ongoing coaching`, `feedback culture`

---

## Q26. What is the SBI feedback model?

**Level:** Basic

The SBI model is a feedback framework that stands for Situation, Behavior, Impact. You describe the specific situation where the behavior occurred, the observable behavior itself, and the impact that behavior had. SBI keeps feedback concrete and non-judgmental, making it easier for the recipient to hear and act on.

**Real-life applications:**
- "In yesterday's client call (Situation), you interrupted the client twice (Behavior), which made them visibly frustrated and we lost momentum (Impact)"
- Training new managers on SBI so they avoid vague feedback like "you need to be more professional"
- Using SBI for positive feedback: "In the team meeting (S), you summarized the data clearly in two minutes (B), which helped the team make a quick decision (I)"
- Pairing SBI with a forward-looking request for change
- Practicing SBI in role-play exercises during management training

**Key concepts:** `SBI model`, `structured feedback`, `behavioral observation`, `impact statement`, `constructive feedback`

---

## Q27. What is psychological safety?

**Level:** Basic

Psychological safety is a team climate where people feel safe to speak up, ask questions, admit mistakes, and offer ideas without fear of embarrassment or punishment. It does not mean being nice all the time. It means creating conditions where honesty and risk-taking are welcomed. Research by Google's Project Aristotle found psychological safety to be the number-one predictor of team effectiveness.

**Real-life applications:**
- A manager openly admitting they made a wrong call, modeling that mistakes are safe to acknowledge
- A team where junior members feel comfortable challenging a senior person's idea in meetings
- Running anonymous suggestion boxes to surface concerns people are afraid to raise publicly
- Celebrating failed experiments in innovation teams to encourage future risk-taking
- Including "what did we learn?" as a standing agenda item in post-mortems

**Key concepts:** `psychological safety`, `trust`, `team climate`, `open communication`, `vulnerability`

---

## Q28. What is a development conversation?

**Level:** Basic

A development conversation is a structured discussion between a manager and an employee focused on the employee's career growth and skill development. Unlike a performance review that looks backward, a development conversation looks forward. It explores aspirations, identifies skills to build, and creates a plan for how the employee can progress.

**Real-life applications:**
- Asking an employee what role they see themselves in three years from now
- Co-creating a plan that includes a stretch assignment and an online course
- Helping an employee decide between a management track and a technical specialist track
- Scheduling development conversations quarterly, separate from performance reviews
- Documenting agreed-upon development actions and revisiting them at the next meeting

**Key concepts:** `development conversation`, `career growth`, `skill building`, `manager coaching`, `forward planning`

---

## Q29. What is an individual development plan?

**Level:** Basic

An individual development plan (IDP) is a written document that outlines an employee's growth goals, the specific actions they will take, and the timeline for completion. It typically covers skills to learn, experiences to pursue, and resources or support needed. The IDP is co-created by the employee and their manager and reviewed regularly.

**Real-life applications:**
- An analyst creating an IDP to learn SQL and Tableau within six months to qualify for a data analyst promotion
- A manager writing an IDP focused on improving delegation skills through coaching sessions and a stretch project
- Using IDP templates in an HR system so progress can be tracked across the organization
- Reviewing IDPs during quarterly one-on-ones to celebrate progress and adjust plans
- Linking IDP completion to eligibility for promotions or lateral moves

**Key concepts:** `individual development plan`, `IDP`, `growth goals`, `action plan`, `career development`

---

## Q30. What is a career lattice?

**Level:** Basic

A career lattice is a model that allows employees to move laterally and diagonally across the organization, not just vertically up a hierarchy. It recognizes that career growth can happen by gaining breadth of experience, switching functions, or exploring new specialties. This gives employees more pathways and gives the company more flexibility in deploying talent.

**Real-life applications:**
- An HR generalist moving laterally into a talent analytics role to build data skills
- A sales representative shifting to customer success to deepen client relationship expertise
- Visualizing career paths as a lattice diagram on the company intranet
- Using lateral moves as a retention tool when vertical promotion slots are limited
- Encouraging cross-functional projects as stepping stones to lattice moves

**Key concepts:** `career lattice`, `lateral move`, `career flexibility`, `non-linear career`, `talent mobility`

---

## Q31. What is a stretch assignment?

**Level:** Basic

A stretch assignment is a project or responsibility that pushes an employee beyond their current skill level. It is designed to accelerate growth by putting someone in a situation where they must learn new skills, deal with ambiguity, or lead at a higher level. Stretch assignments are one of the most effective development tools when paired with coaching and feedback.

**Real-life applications:**
- Asking a mid-level engineer to lead a cross-functional product launch for the first time
- Having a marketing coordinator present the campaign results directly to the executive team
- Assigning a junior analyst to manage a client relationship solo for one quarter
- Sending a domestic operations manager to oversee the opening of an international office
- Giving a team lead responsibility for the department budget during their manager's leave

**Key concepts:** `stretch assignment`, `growth opportunity`, `learning by doing`, `skill acceleration`, `challenge`

---

## Q32. What is mentorship?

**Level:** Basic

Mentorship is a professional relationship where a more experienced person guides a less experienced person's career development. The mentor shares knowledge, provides advice, opens doors to networks, and acts as a sounding board. Unlike coaching, which is often short-term and goal-specific, mentorship tends to be a longer relationship covering broader career guidance.

**Real-life applications:**
- A senior partner at a law firm meeting monthly with a junior associate to discuss career strategy
- A formal mentorship program pairing new hires with employees who have been at the company for over five years
- A mentor helping a mentee prepare for their first board presentation
- Reverse mentoring where junior employees teach senior leaders about technology trends
- A cross-functional mentoring program that helps employees understand other departments

**Key concepts:** `mentorship`, `career guidance`, `knowledge transfer`, `professional relationship`, `mentor-mentee`

---

## Q33. What is organizational culture?

**Level:** Basic

Organizational culture is the set of shared values, beliefs, norms, and behaviors that define how things are done inside a company. It includes both visible elements like office design and dress code, and invisible elements like unwritten rules and assumptions about what matters. Culture shapes every decision, interaction, and priority in the workplace.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company where decisions are made by consensus versus one where the highest-ranking person decides
- An organization that celebrates risk-taking versus one that punishes failure
- The way new employees learn "how we do things here" during their first weeks
- Whether meetings start on time or routinely run late, signaling the cultural value placed on punctuality
- How quickly the company responds to employee complaints, reflecting its values around respect

**Key concepts:** `organizational culture`, `shared values`, `workplace norms`, `cultural artifacts`, `behavioral patterns`

---

## Q34. What are cultural artifacts?

**Level:** Basic

Cultural artifacts are the visible, tangible elements of an organization's culture. They include things like office layout, dress code, company rituals, logos, stories told about founders, and how physical space is used. Artifacts are the easiest layer of culture to observe but the hardest to interpret without understanding the deeper values they represent.

**Real-life applications:**
- Open-plan offices signaling a culture of collaboration and transparency
- A startup's ping-pong table representing a value of work-life integration
- Company-wide celebrations for product launches reflecting a culture of achievement
- The CEO sitting in the same open-plan area as everyone else, signaling flat hierarchy
- Wall displays of the company mission statement and core values

**Key concepts:** `cultural artifacts`, `visible culture`, `symbols`, `rituals`, `organizational identity`

---

## Q35. What are espoused values versus enacted values?

**Level:** Basic

Espoused values are the values a company publicly claims to hold, such as those printed on posters or listed on the website. Enacted values are the values actually demonstrated through daily behavior and decisions. When these two diverge, employees become cynical and trust erodes. Healthy cultures have close alignment between what is said and what is done.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company that espouses "work-life balance" but promotes people who work 70-hour weeks
- An organization that claims "innovation" as a value but punishes employees who take risks and fail
- Testing alignment by surveying employees on whether they see stated values practiced daily
- Leaders who model espoused values through their own behavior, closing the gap
- Removing values from the wall that the company is not willing to actually live by

**Key concepts:** `espoused values`, `enacted values`, `value alignment`, `cultural integrity`, `trust`

---

## Q36. What is change management?

**Level:** Basic

Change management is the structured approach to moving people, teams, and organizations from a current state to a desired future state. It focuses on the human side of change: communication, training, resistance handling, and reinforcement. Most organizational changes fail not because of bad plans but because people are not prepared or willing to adopt them.

**Real-life applications:**
- Preparing employees for a new software system with training sessions and a help desk
- Communicating the reasons behind a company restructuring before rumors fill the gap
- Appointing change champions in each department to support their peers during a transition
- Tracking adoption metrics after a process change to see if people are actually using the new approach
- Holding post-implementation reviews to capture lessons for the next change initiative

**Key concepts:** `change management`, `adoption`, `communication plan`, `resistance`, `transition`

---

## Q37. What is a change sponsor?

**Level:** Basic

A change sponsor is a senior leader who authorizes, funds, and visibly supports a change initiative. The sponsor's role is to communicate why the change matters, remove obstacles, and hold people accountable for adoption. Without an active sponsor, change efforts lose credibility and momentum because employees assume leadership is not truly committed.

**Real-life applications:**
- The CFO sponsoring a new expense-reporting system by using it personally and referencing it in meetings
- A CEO recording a video message explaining why the company is restructuring and what it means for employees
- A division president attending change workshops to signal support and answer questions
- The sponsor escalating when a department head quietly resists adopting a new process
- An executive sponsor chairing a steering committee that reviews change progress monthly

**Key concepts:** `change sponsor`, `executive support`, `leadership commitment`, `accountability`, `visible sponsorship`

---

## Q38. What is resistance to change?

**Level:** Basic

Resistance to change is the pushback that occurs when individuals or groups oppose a new way of doing things. It can be active (vocal complaints, refusal to comply) or passive (ignoring the change, doing the minimum). Resistance often stems from fear of the unknown, loss of control, lack of trust, or past negative experiences with change. It is a normal human reaction, not a sign of bad employees.

**Real-life applications:**
- Long-tenured employees resisting a new technology because they fear their skills will become obsolete
- Middle managers resisting a reorganization that removes their authority
- Frontline workers ignoring a new process because no one explained why it was changing
- Survey results showing low adoption of a new tool because training was inadequate
- Addressing resistance by listening to concerns, involving resisters in planning, and demonstrating quick wins

**Key concepts:** `resistance`, `change adoption`, `fear of unknown`, `employee concerns`, `change readiness`

## Q39. What is leadership development?

**Level:** Basic

Leadership development is the intentional process of building the skills, behaviors, and mindsets people need to lead others effectively. It combines formal training with real-world experiences like stretch assignments, coaching, and mentoring. The goal is to prepare individuals to inspire, guide, and enable their teams to achieve results.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running a six-month leadership academy for newly promoted managers
- Pairing a high-potential employee with an executive coach for quarterly sessions
- Assigning emerging leaders to cross-functional projects that test their influence skills
- Using 360-degree feedback to help leaders see how their behavior affects their teams
- Measuring the impact of leadership programs through team engagement scores

**Key concepts:** `leadership development`, `management training`, `coaching`, `skill building`, `leadership pipeline`

## Q40. What is a leadership competency model?

**Level:** Basic

A leadership competency model is a framework that defines the specific skills, behaviors, and attributes expected of leaders at each level of the organization. It provides a common language for hiring, developing, and evaluating leaders. A good model is tailored to the company's strategy, not a generic list copied from a textbook.

**Real-life applications:**
- Defining that all directors must demonstrate strategic thinking, people development, and cross-functional collaboration
- Using competency models to design interview questions for leadership roles
- Aligning leadership training programs to the competencies the model specifies
- Rating leaders on competency dimensions during performance reviews
- Updating the competency model when the company's strategy shifts

**Key concepts:** `competency model`, `leadership behaviors`, `skill framework`, `performance standards`, `leadership criteria`

## Q41. What is executive coaching?

**Level:** Basic

Executive coaching is a one-on-one professional development relationship between a trained coach and a leader. The coach helps the leader improve self-awareness, develop new behaviors, navigate complex situations, and achieve specific goals. It is confidential, personalized, and typically runs for three to twelve months. It is one of the most effective tools for senior-level development.

**Real-life applications:**
- A newly promoted VP working with a coach to improve their executive presence
- A CEO using a coach to develop better listening skills after receiving critical 360 feedback
- A high-potential director receiving coaching to prepare for a C-suite role within two years
- An executive working with a coach during a major organizational restructuring
- A leader recovering from a setback getting coaching to rebuild confidence and stakeholder trust

**Key concepts:** `executive coaching`, `one-on-one development`, `self-awareness`, `leadership growth`, `behavioral change`

## Q42. What is action learning?

**Level:** Basic

Action learning is a leadership development method where small groups work together on real business problems. Participants learn by doing: they research the issue, propose solutions, present recommendations, and often implement them. It combines learning with value creation because the company gets practical solutions while the participants build leadership skills.

**Real-life applications:**
- Assigning a group of high-potential managers to solve a real customer-retention problem as their development project
- Using action learning as the capstone of a leadership program, replacing traditional exams
- Having cross-functional teams tackle operational inefficiencies and present solutions to the executive committee
- Creating learning teams that rotate through different business challenges each quarter
- Measuring both the business impact of solutions and the skill growth of participants

**Key concepts:** `action learning`, `experiential learning`, `problem-based learning`, `team development`, `practical application`

## Q43. What is a high-potential employee?

**Level:** Basic

A high-potential (HiPo) employee is someone identified as having the ability, aspiration, and engagement to move into a significantly larger leadership role. Being high-performing is not the same as being high-potential. Performance measures past results; potential measures capacity for future growth. HiPo identification drives succession planning and targeted development investments.

**Real-life applications:**
- Using a 9-box grid to plot employees on performance and potential axes
- Creating accelerated development tracks for HiPo employees including rotations and coaching
- Communicating HiPo status transparently to the employee to increase retention
- Investing more development resources in HiPos while still offering growth to all employees
- Monitoring HiPo attrition as a key talent risk metric

**Key concepts:** `high-potential`, `HiPo`, `talent identification`, `leadership pipeline`, `future readiness`

## Q44. What is succession planning?

**Level:** Basic

Succession planning is the process of identifying and developing internal employees who can fill critical roles when current holders depart. It is not just writing down replacement names. It is an active development pipeline that prepares people for bigger responsibilities before the need arises. Good succession planning protects the organization from leadership vacuums.

**Real-life applications:**
- Identifying two to three potential successors for every VP-level role
- Creating development plans for successor candidates that close their readiness gaps
- Conducting annual talent review meetings where leaders discuss the bench for key roles
- Tracking successor readiness as "ready now," "ready in 1-2 years," or "ready in 3+ years"
- Using succession planning to inform which leadership development programs to offer

**Key concepts:** `succession planning`, `leadership continuity`, `bench strength`, `talent review`, `readiness assessment`

---

## Q45. What is a critical role?

**Level:** Basic

A critical role is a position whose vacancy would significantly disrupt business operations, strategy execution, or customer delivery. Not every senior role is critical, and some non-senior roles are. Identifying critical roles helps focus succession planning and development resources where the impact of an unplanned vacancy would be greatest.

**Real-life applications:**
- Labeling the Chief Technology Officer role as critical in a software company because all product decisions flow through it
- Identifying a specialized plant manager as critical because only two people in the company can run that production line
- Using criteria like revenue impact, knowledge concentration, and replacement difficulty to score criticality
- Reviewing the critical role list annually as the business strategy evolves
- Prioritizing succession planning efforts on the roles with the highest criticality scores

**Key concepts:** `critical role`, `key position`, `business impact`, `succession priority`, `role criticality`

---

## Q46. What is bench strength?

**Level:** Basic

Bench strength is the depth and quality of internal candidates available to fill key roles when they become vacant. Strong bench strength means the organization has several ready or nearly ready successors for its most important positions. Weak bench strength creates vulnerability: one resignation or retirement can cause a crisis.

**Real-life applications:**
- Reporting to the board that 80% of director-level roles have at least one successor ready within a year
- Comparing bench strength across divisions to identify where succession risk is highest
- Using bench strength metrics to justify investment in leadership development programs
- Tracking bench strength trends over time to see if development efforts are working
- Flagging any critical role with zero identified successors as an urgent talent risk

**Key concepts:** `bench strength`, `successor depth`, `talent readiness`, `succession risk`, `internal pipeline`

---

## Q47. What is a talent review?

**Level:** Basic

A talent review is a periodic meeting where leaders discuss the performance, potential, and readiness of their team members. It typically uses tools like the 9-box grid to categorize employees. Talent reviews inform decisions about promotions, development investments, succession slotting, and retention interventions. They bring objectivity and transparency to talent decisions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Holding quarterly talent reviews where each director presents their team's 9-box placement
- Using talent reviews to identify employees at risk of leaving and developing retention plans
- Discussing which high-potentials are ready for stretch assignments or promotions
- Surfacing hidden talent that a particular manager may not be advocating for
- Linking talent review outcomes to budget allocation for training and development

**Key concepts:** `talent review`, `9-box grid`, `performance-potential matrix`, `leadership discussion`, `talent decisions`

## Q48. What is the 9-box grid?

**Level:** Basic

The 9-box grid is a talent management tool that plots employees on two axes: performance (low, moderate, high) and potential (low, moderate, high). This creates nine categories, from "low performance, low potential" to "high performance, high potential." It helps leaders make more consistent and transparent decisions about development, succession, and resource allocation.

**Real-life applications:**
- Placing all managers in a division on the 9-box during an annual talent review
- Using the top-right box (high performance, high potential) to identify succession candidates
- Creating tailored action plans for each box, such as coaching for high performers with moderate potential
- Triggering retention interventions for high performers who may be flight risks
- Comparing 9-box placements year over year to track employee growth

**Key concepts:** `9-box grid`, `performance-potential matrix`, `talent categorization`, `development planning`, `talent assessment`

## Q49. What is onboarding?

**Level:** Basic

Onboarding is the process of integrating a new hire into the organization, covering everything from paperwork and tool setup to cultural orientation and role-specific training. Good onboarding accelerates time-to-productivity, improves retention, and sets the tone for the employee's experience. Poor onboarding leaves new hires confused, disconnected, and more likely to quit early.

**Real-life applications:**
- Providing a structured 90-day onboarding plan with milestones for each month
- Assigning a buddy or peer mentor to help the new hire navigate the company
- Holding a welcome session with the team and a meeting with the skip-level manager in the first week
- Sending pre-boarding materials before the start date so day one is not all paperwork
- Surveying new hires at 30, 60, and 90 days to improve the onboarding experience

**Key concepts:** `onboarding`, `new hire integration`, `time to productivity`, `orientation`, `first impressions`

## Q50. What is employee engagement?

**Level:** Basic

Employee engagement is the level of emotional commitment, motivation, and connection an employee feels toward their work and their organization. Engaged employees go beyond the minimum. They care about outcomes, contribute ideas, and stay longer. Engagement is not the same as happiness or satisfaction; it is about investment in the work and the mission.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running annual engagement surveys and acting on the results visibly
- Tracking engagement by department to identify where leadership or culture needs attention
- Correlating engagement scores with business outcomes like revenue, safety incidents, and customer satisfaction
- Using pulse surveys to measure engagement more frequently than once a year
- Recognizing and celebrating the behaviors that indicate high engagement

**Key concepts:** `employee engagement`, `emotional commitment`, `discretionary effort`, `motivation`, `organizational connection`

## Q51. What is employee retention?

**Level:** Basic

Employee retention is the ability of an organization to keep its employees over time. High retention means fewer people leave voluntarily. Retention matters because turnover is expensive: recruiting, hiring, onboarding, and lost productivity can cost 50-200% of an employee's annual salary depending on the role. Retention strategies focus on pay, growth, culture, and management quality.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking annual voluntary turnover rates by department and tenure
- Conducting stay interviews to learn what keeps current employees engaged
- Offering retention bonuses for employees in critical roles during times of organizational change
- Analyzing exit interview data to identify the top three reasons people leave
- Benchmarking retention rates against industry averages to assess competitiveness

**Key concepts:** `employee retention`, `voluntary turnover`, `stay interview`, `retention strategy`, `turnover cost`

## Q52. What is an exit interview?

**Level:** Basic

An exit interview is a conversation held with an employee who is voluntarily leaving the organization. Its purpose is to learn why the person is leaving, what their experience was like, and what the company could do better. When data from many exit interviews is analyzed together, patterns emerge that can inform retention strategies and culture improvements.

**Real-life applications:**
- Asking departing employees to rate their manager, growth opportunities, and compensation satisfaction
- Identifying that a particular department has a pattern of employees leaving due to the same manager
- Comparing exit interview themes over time to see if recent improvements are making a difference
- Offering exit interviews in writing for employees who are uncomfortable speaking in person
- Sharing anonymized exit interview trends with leadership quarterly

**Key concepts:** `exit interview`, `turnover analysis`, `departing employee`, `feedback collection`, `retention insight`

---

## Q53. What is a stay interview?

**Level:** Basic

A stay interview is a proactive conversation with a current employee to understand what keeps them at the company and what might cause them to leave. Unlike exit interviews that happen too late, stay interviews happen while the employee is still engaged. They give managers early warning of retention risks and actionable ideas for improving the employee experience.

**Real-life applications:**
- Asking top performers: "What is the number one thing that could make you consider leaving?"
- Conducting stay interviews after major organizational changes to check in on sentiment
- Using stay interview insights to adjust a specific employee's role, schedule, or development plan
- Training managers to hold stay interviews as part of their regular one-on-one cadence
- Aggregating stay interview themes to inform company-wide retention strategies

**Key concepts:** `stay interview`, `proactive retention`, `employee voice`, `risk identification`, `engagement check`

## Q54. What is total rewards?

**Level:** Basic

Total rewards is the complete package of compensation and benefits an employer provides, including base salary, bonuses, equity, health insurance, retirement contributions, paid time off, flexibility, recognition programs, and career development opportunities. Thinking in terms of total rewards helps companies compete for talent even when base salary alone is not the highest in the market.

**Real-life applications:**
- Presenting a job offer that breaks down salary, equity, benefits, and growth opportunities as a total package
- Surveying employees to learn which elements of total rewards they value most
- Adjusting the rewards mix for different employee segments, such as offering more flexibility to working parents
- Benchmarking total rewards against competitors rather than just comparing base salaries
- Using total rewards statements to help employees understand the full value of their compensation

**Key concepts:** `total rewards`, `compensation`, `benefits`, `employee value`, `reward strategy`

## Q55. What is compensation benchmarking?

**Level:** Basic

Compensation benchmarking is the process of comparing an organization's pay levels against the market to ensure competitiveness. It involves collecting salary data from surveys, industry reports, and public databases, then positioning the company's pay relative to the market median, 25th percentile, or 75th percentile. Benchmarking prevents both underpaying (which loses talent) and overpaying (which wastes money).

**Real-life applications:**
- Using salary survey data to determine the market rate for a software engineer in a specific city
- Deciding to pay at the 75th percentile for critical roles to attract top talent
- Adjusting salaries after benchmarking reveals the company is 15% below market for data scientists
- Reviewing benchmarks annually to keep pace with market movement
- Using benchmarking data to design pay bands for each job level

**Key concepts:** `compensation benchmarking`, `market rate`, `salary survey`, `pay competitiveness`, `pay bands`

## Q56. What is pay equity?

**Level:** Basic

Pay equity means ensuring that employees doing the same or substantially similar work receive comparable pay, regardless of gender, race, or other protected characteristics. It is both a legal requirement and an ethical imperative. Pay equity audits identify gaps, and adjustments correct them. Ignoring pay equity creates legal risk, harms morale, and damages the employer brand.

**Real-life applications:**
- Conducting an annual pay equity audit comparing salaries across gender and ethnicity within each job level
- Allocating budget to close identified pay gaps through targeted salary adjustments
- Training hiring managers to make offers based on the role's pay band, not the candidate's salary history
- Publishing pay equity statistics in the company's annual report to build trust
- Banning salary history questions in interviews to prevent perpetuating past inequities

**Key concepts:** `pay equity`, `equal pay`, `compensation fairness`, `pay gap analysis`, `legal compliance`

## Q57. What is employee recognition?

**Level:** Basic

Employee recognition is the practice of acknowledging and rewarding employees for their contributions, achievements, and behaviors that support organizational goals. It can be formal (awards, bonuses) or informal (a public thank-you, a handwritten note). Effective recognition is timely, specific, and tied to the values or outcomes the company wants to reinforce.

**Real-life applications:**

- A peer recognition platform where employees send digital kudos visible to the whole team
- Quarterly awards for employees who exemplify company values
- Spot bonuses for exceptional contributions outside normal job duties
- Anniversary recognition for milestone tenure like 5, 10, or 20 years

**Key concepts:** `employee recognition`, `reward`, `appreciation`, `motivation`, `positive reinforcement`

## Q58. What is employee well-being?

**Level:** Basic

Employee well-being is the overall physical, mental, financial, and social health of employees. Companies that invest in well-being see lower absenteeism, higher productivity, and better retention. Well-being programs go beyond gym memberships to include mental health support, financial wellness education, flexible work arrangements, and workload management.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q59. What is diversity, equity, and inclusion?

Diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) is a framework for creating workplaces where people of all backgrounds are represented (diversity), have fair access to opportunities (equity), and feel valued and respected (inclusion). DEI is not just a moral stance; diverse teams make better decisions, and inclusive cultures retain more talent. It requires intentional action, not just good intentions.

**Real-life applications:**

- Training managers on inclusive leadership behaviors like equitable meeting facilitation

## Q60. What is an employee resource group?

An employee resource group (ERG) is a voluntary, employee-led group organized around a shared identity, interest, or experience. Common ERGs include groups for women, people of color, LGBTQ+ employees, veterans, and parents. ERGs provide community, support, and a voice for underrepresented groups. They also serve as talent pipelines and advisory bodies for leadership.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q61. What is labor law compliance?

Labor law compliance means following the legal rules that govern the employer-employee relationship. These laws cover minimum wage, overtime, anti-discrimination, workplace safety, family leave, and more. Non-compliance can result in lawsuits, fines, reputational damage, and even criminal penalties. HR must stay current on laws that vary by country, state, and municipality.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q62. What is HR analytics?

**Level:** Basic

HR analytics is the practice of using data to understand and improve workforce decisions. It involves collecting, measuring, and analyzing HR data like turnover rates, engagement scores, time-to-fill, and training effectiveness. HR analytics turns gut feelings into evidence-based decisions. It answers questions like "Why are people leaving?" and "Which recruiting channel produces the best hires?"

**Real-life applications:**

- Analyzing which interview questions best predict on-the-job performance
- Identifying flight risk by combining engagement survey data with market salary data

## Q63. What is the difference between HR efficiency and HR effectiveness?

**Level:** Basic

HR efficiency measures how quickly and cheaply HR delivers its services, like time-to-fill or cost-per-hire. HR effectiveness measures whether those services produce the desired outcomes, like quality-of-hire or employee engagement improvements. An HR function can be efficient but ineffective if it fills roles fast but with the wrong people. Both matter, but effectiveness should drive strategy.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking cost-per-hire as an efficiency metric and quality-of-hire as an effectiveness metric
- Measuring how fast onboarding is completed versus how well-prepared new hires feel after onboarding
- Automating payroll processing for efficiency while improving accuracy for effectiveness
- Reducing time spent on benefits administration while increasing employee satisfaction with benefit options
- Balancing self-service HR tools that improve efficiency with personalized support that improves effectiveness

**Key concepts:** `HR efficiency`, `HR effectiveness`, `operational metrics`, `outcome metrics`, `service delivery`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### People Decision Ladder
**Purpose:** Translate a people issue into a decision path.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Business need
   |
   v
People capability required
   |
   v
Current workforce reality
   |
   v
Gap analysis
   |
   v
Action: hire / train / redeploy / retain
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: business need, meaning the strategic problem the company faces.
Component 2: capability required, meaning the people skill or role needed to solve it.

Formula: `Capability Gap = Required Capacity - Available Capacity`
Variables:
Required Capacity = the amount of skill, headcount, or output needed
Available Capacity = what the current workforce can supply
Why this formula exists: It makes the talent shortfall visible.
How to interpret the output:
Positive value = shortage
Zero = sufficient coverage
Negative value = excess capacity or redeployment opportunity
Worked example with numbers: If a product org needs 8 data translators and has 5, the gap is 3. That should trigger hiring, training, or a temporary borrow decision.

Why this formula exists: It combines selection quality and retention into one check.
