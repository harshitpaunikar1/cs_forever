# Search Engine Marketing (SEM)

## Overview

Search Engine Marketing is the practice of placing paid advertisements on search engine results pages. When someone searches for a keyword you've bid on, your ad can appear above or alongside organic results. The most common model is pay-per-click (PPC), where you pay only when a user clicks your ad. Google Ads is the dominant platform, followed by Microsoft Advertising.

---

## Why It Matters

SEM puts your brand in front of people at the exact moment they are searching for what you sell. Unlike SEO, which takes months, a well-built SEM campaign can drive qualified traffic within hours of launch. It's also highly measurable — you know exactly how much each click and conversion costs.

## Key Principles

- Keyword selection determines who sees your ad; match types control how broadly your ad triggers
- Quality Score rewards relevant ads with lower costs and better positions
- Negative keywords prevent your ad from showing on irrelevant searches, saving budget
- Continuous A/B testing of ad copy and landing pages is essential for improving return on ad spend

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **PPC (Pay-Per-Click)** | An advertising model where you pay each time someone clicks your ad |
| **Quality Score** | Google's rating of the relevance and quality of your keywords, ads, and landing pages |
| **CPC (Cost Per Click)** | The actual amount you pay for each click on your ad |
| **Ad Rank** | The value that determines your ad's position, calculated from bid, Quality Score, and ad extensions |

## Use Case

A new meal-kit delivery service needs customers fast. It launches Google Ads campaigns targeting keywords like "meal kit delivery" and "healthy meal subscription." By pausing low-performing keywords weekly and raising bids on high-converting ones, the company achieves a 5:1 return on ad spend within the first month.

## Scenario

> A regional law firm spent $3,000 per month on broad-match keywords and saw a 1.2% conversion rate. After adding 150 negative keywords, switching high-intent terms to exact match, and rewriting ad copy to include specific practice areas, conversion rate rose to 4.8% and cost per lead dropped from $120 to $45.

## Examples

- A hotel chain bids on competitor brand names so that travelers searching for a rival hotel also see its own offer with a price-match guarantee
- A SaaS company uses responsive search ads with multiple headline and description variations, letting Google's algorithm serve the best-performing combination

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Keyword-Intent Matrix
**Purpose:** Align keyword choice with user readiness and business value.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
High intent / High value   -> bid aggressively
High intent / Low value    -> bid selectively
Low intent / High value    -> educate or remarket
Low intent / Low value     -> exclude or de-prioritize
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: user intent - how close the query is to purchase.
Component 2: business value - the margin or lead quality of the query.
Component 3: keyword type - branded, generic, competitor, or problem-aware.
Component 4: budget action - bid, cap, test, or exclude.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A meal-kit company bids hard on "healthy meal subscription" because intent and value are both high, but it down-bids broad recipe terms because they attract research traffic with weak conversion.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the account is spending on keywords that feel busy but do not convert.

### Ad-Rank Ladder
**Purpose:** Show why position is a function of both bid and relevance.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Bid -> Quality Score -> Ad Extensions -> Ad Rank -> Position
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: bid strength - how much the advertiser is willing to pay.
Component 2: quality signal - relevance of keyword, ad copy, and landing page.
Component 3: extension value - extra information that improves ad utility.
Component 4: auction outcome - the visible position on the results page.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A legal firm improves Quality Score by making the ad and landing page match the exact practice area, which lifts position without requiring a higher bid.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team thinks position only comes from spending more.

### Search Optimization Loop
**Purpose:** Turn search performance into a repeatable test-and-learn cycle.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
query -> click -> landing -> conversion -> learn -> adjust keywords/ad/landing
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: query quality - whether the right search terms were targeted.
Component 2: ad relevance - whether the message matches the query.
Component 3: landing relevance - whether the page fulfills the promise.
Component 4: learning feedback - what gets changed after the data comes in.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company tests three headline variants, sees one practice-area keyword set convert better, and then adjusts the landing page copy to match the highest-intent query.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team treats SEM as a one-time setup instead of an ongoing optimization loop.

## 4. Formulas
The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are practical search-advertising heuristics [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: CPC [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `CPC = total spend / total clicks`
Variables:
total spend = money spent on the campaign
total clicks = clicks received from the campaign
Why this formula exists: It measures the price of traffic.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> traffic is cheap
Value medium -> acceptable depending on conversion
Value high -> check keywords, Quality Score, and intent
Worked example with numbers: If a campaign spends $3,000 for 200 clicks, CPC is $15. Decision: keep only if the downstream conversion rate supports that cost.

### Formula 2: ROAS [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `ROAS = attributed revenue / ad spend`
Variables:
attributed revenue = revenue credited to the campaign
ad spend = spend on the campaign
Why this formula exists: It shows whether search traffic is paying back.
How to interpret the output:
Value below 1 -> losing money
Value 1-3 -> borderline
Value above 3 -> often attractive, subject to margin
Worked example with numbers: If a meal-kit campaign spends $10,000 and drives $50,000 in revenue, ROAS is 5.0. Decision: scale carefully and protect margin.

### Formula 3: Conversion Rate [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Conversion Rate = conversions / clicks`
Variables:
conversions = leads, sales, deposits, or sign-ups
clicks = visits from paid search
Why this formula exists: It tells you whether the traffic quality and landing page are working together.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> mismatch between query and offer
Value medium -> okay, but improvable
Value high -> strong intent and landing-page fit
Worked example with numbers: If 96 of 2,000 clicks convert, conversion rate is 4.8%. Decision: keep the high-intent terms and improve the landing page for the rest.

### Formula 4: Negative Keyword Savings [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Negative Keyword Savings = avoided irrelevant clicks * average CPC`
Variables:
avoided irrelevant clicks = clicks prevented by exclusions
average CPC = average cost per click
Why this formula exists: It quantifies the value of excluding waste.
How to interpret the output:
Value positive and meaningful -> negatives are worth maintaining
Value small -> the exclusions may be too narrow to matter
Worked example with numbers: If 200 irrelevant clicks are avoided at $12 CPC, savings are $2,400. Decision: keep refining the negative list weekly.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Bid on everything and hope the algorithm sorts it out. | Prioritize high-intent, high-value keywords first. |
| Treat search ads as isolated from landing pages. | Align keyword, ad copy, and landing page as one system. |
| Ignore negative keywords. | Block irrelevant queries aggressively. |
| Assume position is only about bid size. | Improve Quality Score and ad relevance too. |
| Optimize for clicks without tracking conversions. | Tie every keyword set to revenue or lead quality. |
| Let the campaign run unchanged for months. | Test copy, bids, and landing pages continuously. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Law Firm Lead Generation
Situation: A regional law firm spends $3,000 a month on broad-match keywords and gets weak conversion.
Applicable framework/metric: Keyword-Intent Matrix and Conversion Rate.
Analysis: After adding 150 negative keywords, switching important terms to exact match, and rewriting ads to name practice areas, conversion rises from 1.2% to 4.8% and cost per lead falls from $120 to $45.
Decision rule: If broad traffic underperforms, tighten intent before raising budget. If conversion improves, keep pruning low-quality terms.
Action: Maintain exact-match high-intent terms and review negative keywords weekly.

### Scenario 2: Meal-Kit Launch
Situation: A new meal-kit service needs customers fast and uses search ads to capture demand.
Applicable framework/metric: Search Optimization Loop and ROAS.
Analysis: Bids on "meal kit delivery" and "healthy meal subscription" create qualified traffic, and the campaign reaches 5:1 ROAS in the first month. The team pauses poor keywords each week and reallocates budget to terms with the best conversion.
Decision rule: If ROAS is below target, cut or rewrite the weakest keywords. If ROAS is strong, scale only where margin allows.
Action: Expand the high-intent term set and test new landing-page offers.

### Scenario 3: Hotel Brand Defense
Situation: A hotel chain bids on competitor brand names to intercept travelers near booking time.
Applicable framework/metric: Ad-Rank Ladder and Negative Keyword Savings.
Analysis: The chain uses competitor terms only when landing pages and price-match offers are strong enough to convert. It also adds negatives to prevent irrelevant clicks from generic travel research queries.
Decision rule: If search intent is close to booking, competitor bidding can work. If the query is too broad, exclude it.
Action: Keep competitor terms tightly controlled and measure incremental bookings, not just clicks.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Classify every keyword by intent and value.
2. Build the campaign structure around themes, not just around volume.
3. Add negative keywords before scaling spend.
4. Match each ad group to a landing page that fulfills the search promise.
5. Track CPC, conversion rate, and ROAS for every meaningful keyword cluster.
6. Revisit ad copy and landing pages weekly.
7. Move budget from broad intent to high-intent terms when quality is proven.
8. Use search data to inform product messaging and content strategy, not just media buying.
