# Global Capital Flows and Trade

## Overview

Capital flows are the money that moves across borders as foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, or loans. Trade in goods is only half the story of globalization — capital flows are the other half. A multinational that builds a factory abroad is exporting capital; an investor buying foreign stocks is doing portfolio investment.

---

## Why It Matters

Exchange rates, factory location decisions, funding costs, and even tariff policy are all shaped by how capital moves. Managers planning expansions, hedging currency, or choosing where to list shares work in a world where trade and capital flows are tightly linked. A surge or sudden stop in capital flows can upend a business plan overnight.

## Key Principles

- Current account (trade) and capital account typically mirror each other.
- FDI is sticky; portfolio flows are quick and volatile.
- Strong capital inflows can push up the currency and hurt exports.
- Financial crises often start with a sudden stop in capital flows.
- Capital controls exist but come with tradeoffs in cost and credibility.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Current Account** | Net trade in goods and services plus income and transfers. |
| **Capital Account** | Net investment flows across borders. |
| **FDI** | Foreign Direct Investment — long-term ownership of assets abroad. |
| **Hot Money** | Short-term portfolio flows that can reverse quickly. |

## Use Case

A consumer-goods firm expanding into a new market chooses between exporting from home (trade flow) and building a local plant (capital flow) by comparing tariffs, wage costs, currency risk, and investment incentives.

## Scenario

> A Southeast Asian country saw a flood of foreign portfolio money during a global rate-cut period. The currency rose, exports fell 8%, and factories shed jobs. When global rates rose again, the capital reversed and the currency crashed. The government later introduced moderate, targeted capital-flow rules to soften future swings.

## Examples

- A German automaker opening a plant in Mexico to serve North America, combining trade and FDI strategies.
- A sovereign wealth fund shifting billions across markets, nudging exchange rates and export competitiveness.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Trade vs Capital Decision Lens
**Purpose:** Choose between exporting and investing locally.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                Lower local investment cost
                          ^
                          |
      Export from home     |     Build local presence
   (trade-heavy strategy)  |   (capital-flow-heavy strategy)
                          |
                          +------------------------------> Lower trade friction
                           Higher trade friction
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Trade friction - tariffs, logistics, and cross-border delay.
Component 2: Local investment cost - plant, staffing, compliance, and setup time.
Component 3: Currency risk - how much FX movement changes the economics.
Component 4: Control and permanence - whether you need durable local presence.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm selling analytics advisory into a new market can either fly experts in from the home office or open a local delivery hub. If tariffs are irrelevant but client data rules and currency risk are high, the local hub can still win because it reduces delivery friction and stabilizes billing.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is deciding between cross-border delivery and local buildout.

### Capital Flow Stability Matrix
**Purpose:** Separate stable capital from volatile capital.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    More stable
                         ^
                         |
           FDI           |          Long-term loans
                         |
-------------------------+------------------------> Faster exit
                         |
      Portfolio inflows   |           Hot money
                         |
                    More volatile
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Holding horizon - how long the money is likely to stay.
Component 2: Reversal risk - how fast the money can leave.
Component 3: Strategic attachment - whether the flow is tied to real assets.
Component 4: Macro impact - how much the flow can move exchange rates.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI startup with foreign strategic investors is closer to FDI when the investors want board rights and product scaling support. A foreign hedge fund buying the startup’s listed parent shares is closer to portfolio investment and can reverse quickly if risk appetite changes.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When treasury or leadership needs to classify a cross-border funding source.

### Balance-of-Payments Linkage
**Purpose:** Show why trade flows and capital flows move together.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Trade surplus
   -> foreign currency earned
   -> capital outflow or reserve buildup
Trade deficit
   -> foreign currency needed
   -> capital inflow or reserve drawdown
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Current account direction - net export or net import position.
Component 2: Capital account direction - net inflow or outflow of investment money.
Component 3: FX pressure - whether currency strengthens or weakens.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company billing mostly in dollars but paying a large share of staff costs in a weaker local currency can see inflows strengthen the local currency, making exports of services more expensive in overseas markets. That changes pricing and hiring plans.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When FX moves are changing both pricing and financing choices.

## 4. Formulas
Formula: Current Account + Capital Account ≈ 0 [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Variables:
Current Account = net trade, income, and transfers
Capital Account = net cross-border investment flows

Why this formula exists: It is a quick shorthand for the idea that trade and capital flows tend to offset each other in the external accounts.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0: external deficit pressure -> expect dependence on foreign capital or reserve use
Value = 0: balanced external position -> no immediate pressure
Value > 0: external surplus pressure -> expect capital outflow or reserve accumulation
Worked example with numbers: A consulting group earns $120M from overseas clients and pays $100M in foreign delivery costs, so the current-account-style surplus is $20M. If foreign investors put $20M into the group’s local subsidiary, the combined picture is roughly balanced.

Formula: Net capital inflow = FDI + portfolio investment + loans - outflows [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Variables:
FDI = long-term ownership flows
Portfolio investment = securities buying and selling
Loans = cross-border borrowing and lending
Outflows = money leaving the country or company

Why this formula exists: It helps finance teams separate sticky strategic money from reversible money.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0: net outflow -> tighter liquidity and likely FX pressure
Value = 0: neutral -> stable but not expansionary
Value > 0: net inflow -> easier funding and possible currency appreciation
Worked example with numbers: An AI platform raises $40M of FDI, attracts $15M of foreign equity purchases, and repays $5M of external debt. Net inflow is $50M, which can support hiring and cloud spend but may also push up the local currency.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat every foreign inflow as equally stable. | Separate FDI from hot money before making strategy calls. |
| Assume a stronger currency automatically helps exporters. | Test export competitiveness after each major inflow. |
| Ignore funding costs when choosing a market-entry model. | Compare home-office export, local subsidiary, and hybrid financing. |
| Use tariffs as the only market-entry variable. | Include currency risk, capital controls, and operating control. |
| Wait until a reversal hits before planning. | Build a sudden-stop scenario into treasury and board reviews. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: SaaS expansion versus local office
Situation: A B2B SaaS firm wants to enter a new market and can either sell remotely from its home country or set up a local office. The market has low tariffs but high currency volatility and strict billing preferences.
Applicable framework/metric: Trade vs Capital Decision Lens.
Analysis: Remote delivery keeps setup cost near $0.5M, while the local office costs $2.5M upfront. If currency swings add 8% to expected revenue volatility and local presence cuts churn by 12%, the local office wins when lifetime value lift exceeds setup cost.
Decision rule: If incremental annual gross margin gain is above $1M, do the local build. If it is between $0.4M and $1M, pilot with a hybrid setup. If below $0.4M, stay remote.
Action: Model landed delivery cost, churn, and FX sensitivity in one board slide before approving the market-entry plan.

Scenario 2: AI fundraising and exchange-rate impact
Situation: An AI startup raises foreign capital to fund model training and international hiring. The funding arrives as mostly long-term strategic investment, but there is also a fast-moving secondary share purchase.
Applicable framework/metric: Capital Flow Stability Matrix.
Analysis: $30M in strategic FDI and $10M in portfolio-style secondary demand create a $40M inflow. If $10M of the flow can reverse in one quarter, only $30M should be treated as stable runway.
Decision rule: If reversible share is above 25%, keep 6 months of cash buffer. If it is between 10% and 25%, keep 3 months. If below 10%, normal buffer is enough.
Action: Tag each dollar of incoming capital as strategic, reversible, or debt-like before using it in hiring plans.

Scenario 3: Consulting delivery hub and FX shock
Situation: A consulting firm serves APAC clients from a home base and is considering a local delivery hub after the home currency appreciates.
Applicable framework/metric: Balance-of-Payments Linkage.
Analysis: If the currency appreciates 9%, the effective price of the consulting package rises by the same order unless the firm resets rates or localizes delivery. A local hub reduces client price shock but introduces new compliance cost.
Decision rule: If FX pass-through to client pricing exceeds 5%, localize delivery. If it is between 2% and 5%, hedge and localize selectively. If below 2%, keep the existing structure.
Action: Add FX pass-through, local payroll, and client retention to the renewal model.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Map every market into a trade-first, capital-first, or hybrid entry option.
2. Classify each funding source as FDI, portfolio investment, or loans.
3. Build an FX dashboard that tracks spot rates, volatility, and net exposure.
4. Add a sudden-stop scenario to the quarterly treasury review.
5. Review whether tariffs, compliance rules, or capital controls change the market-entry math.
6. Separate stable runway from reversible capital in every board forecast.
7. Recheck export competitiveness after any major currency move.
