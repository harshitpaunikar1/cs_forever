# GATT 1994 Basic Principles

## Overview

GATT 1994 is the core agreement on trade in goods inside the WTO system. It carries over the rules of the original 1947 GATT and adds updates. Its main job is to keep tariffs predictable and stop governments from using sneaky non-tariff tricks to block imports.

---

## Why It Matters

GATT principles set the default rules your export or import plan runs on. Whenever you see a "bound tariff," "MFN rate," or a challenge to an import quota, GATT 1994 is the document behind it. Managers reading tariff schedules or customs rulings are reading GATT's rules in action.

## Key Principles

- Non-discrimination: treat trading partners and imports fairly and consistently.
- Bind tariffs: members promise not to raise tariffs above agreed ceilings.
- Ban most quantitative restrictions — use tariffs, not quotas.
- Transparency: publish trade rules so traders can comply.
- Allow exceptions for health, safety, national security, and balance-of-payments crises.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Bound Tariff** | The maximum tariff a country has legally committed not to exceed. |
| **Applied Tariff** | The tariff actually charged, which may be lower than the bound rate. |
| **Quantitative Restriction** | A quota or ban on how much can be imported. |
| **Schedule of Concessions** | A country's official list of bound tariffs by product. |

## Use Case

An importer plans a five-year sourcing deal and checks the destination country's bound tariff rate for insurance against sudden tariff hikes, because the bound rate is the legal maximum.

## Scenario

> A processed-food exporter was hit by a sudden import quota from a foreign buyer-country. The exporter's government argued at the WTO that the quota violated GATT's ban on quantitative restrictions. The quota was lifted and replaced with a tariff, which was more predictable and easier to plan around.

## Examples

- A manufacturer using bound-rate data to model worst-case tariff costs in a long-term contract.
- A government challenging an import licensing scheme as a disguised quota.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Tariff Predictability Test
**Purpose:** Decide whether a sourcing or market-entry plan is stable enough to sign.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Bound tariff high?  -> yes -> tariff risk is capped
Applied tariff low?  -> yes -> current cost is manageable
Quota or ban present? -> yes -> predictability drops sharply
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Bound tariff, the legal ceiling.
Component 2: Applied tariff, the current charge.
Component 3: Quantitative restriction risk, whether a quota or ban could override price-based planning.
Component 4: Predictability, the core business value GATT is trying to protect.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A SaaS vendor sells AI-enabled devices into a foreign market and needs to sign a three-year distribution deal. If the bound tariff is 8 percent and the applied tariff is 3 percent, the vendor can model landed cost with some confidence; if a quota appears, the plan becomes fragile.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When procurement or commercial teams ask whether to lock a long-term contract.

### Non-Discrimination Check
**Purpose:** Verify that trade treatment is consistent across partners and products.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Partner A ---- same treatment ----> domestic/imported goods
Partner B ---- same treatment ----> domestic/imported goods
If treatment differs without a legal exception, risk rises
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Partner treatment, whether the country applies a consistent rule.
Component 2: Product treatment, whether imported goods face hidden discrimination.
Component 3: Legal exception, whether health, safety, national security, or crisis rules apply.
Component 4: Compliance risk, the chance of a dispute or forced change.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A consulting firm runs procurement across multiple countries. If one location gets a hidden import license delay while another gets normal clearance, the legal and delivery risk rises even if the tariff rate is unchanged.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When two markets look "similar" but behave differently in practice.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: [verified from model knowledge, not source] Tariff headroom = Bound tariff - Applied tariff
Variables:
Bound tariff = maximum legally committed tariff
Applied tariff = tariff currently charged
Why this formula exists: It answers how much room a government has before it breaches its commitment.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> a compliance breach or data error -> escalate immediately
Value 0-5 -> limited policy room -> manage contracts conservatively
Value > 5 -> meaningful tariff buffer -> still monitor for quota-style changes
Worked example with numbers: If the bound tariff is 12 percent and the applied tariff is 4 percent, tariff headroom = 8 percentage points. A sourcing team can absorb some volatility, but it should still watch for legal or political change.

Formula: Compliance risk score = Non-discrimination gap + Quantitative restriction pressure + Transparency gap
Variables:
Non-discrimination gap = how much treatment differs across partners or goods
Quantitative restriction pressure = likelihood of quota-like controls
Transparency gap = how unclear or unpublished the rule is
Why this formula exists: It answers whether a trade rule is likely to trigger a GATT dispute or operational disruption.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 3 -> low risk -> proceed with normal monitoring
Value 3-5 -> moderate risk -> review contract clauses and legal fallback options
Value > 5 -> high risk -> delay commitment and escalate
Worked example with numbers: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A market has a 2-point non-discrimination gap, a 3-point quota risk, and a 1-point transparency gap. Risk score = 6, so the team should not treat the market as stable.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| Don't | Do |
|---------|-------|
| Sign a long-term sourcing deal without checking the bound tariff. | Model landed cost against the legal ceiling and the applied tariff. |
| Treat quotas as the same as tariffs. | Flag quantitative restrictions as a separate risk because they are harder to plan around. |
| Assume equal tariff treatment across partners. | Run a non-discrimination check before launching in multiple markets. |
| Ignore legal exceptions until a dispute starts. | Test health, safety, national security, and balance-of-payments exceptions upfront. |
| Rely on informal customs behavior. | Demand transparent, published rules before scaling a trade-dependent operation. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: Hardware sourcing for an AI product
Situation: A product company sourcing imported sensors for an AI device has a three-year launch plan. The CFO wants to know whether the current tariff environment is safe enough to lock a supply contract. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Tariff Predictability Test.
Analysis: Bound tariff is 10 percent, applied tariff is 4 percent, and no quota is in place, so the plan has 6 points of tariff headroom. That supports a long-term contract if the legal team confirms there is no hidden restriction.
Decision rule: If bound-applied headroom is above 5 and no quota exists, sign. If headroom is 2-5, add flexibility clauses. If a quota appears, pause and renegotiate.
Action: Add tariff escalators, exit clauses, and customs monitoring to the vendor agreement.

Scenario 2: Consulting delivery into two countries
Situation: A consulting firm delivers the same digital transformation offer in two markets, but one market adds extra licensing friction to imported equipment while the other does not. Revenue teams want to know whether the difference is legal or just operational noise. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Non-Discrimination Check.
Analysis: If identical goods or partners face different treatment without an exception, the compliance risk score rises above 5 and the market becomes unstable. That means the issue needs legal review, not just operations cleanup.
Decision rule: If treatment is equal, scale. If treatment differs slightly with a clear exception, monitor. If treatment differs materially without an exception, stop and investigate.
Action: Build a market-by-market customs and legal control tower.

Scenario 3: Global SaaS expansion with customs exposure
Situation: A SaaS company shipping edge devices to enterprise customers wants to forecast landed cost after tariff updates. Operations prefers a simple rate card, while finance wants to know the worst-case exposure.
Applicable framework/metric: Tariff headroom.
Analysis: Bound tariff 15 percent minus applied tariff 6 percent gives 9 points of room. That is enough to protect the launch if the team keeps a reserve for policy change and avoids assuming the current rate is permanent.
Decision rule: If headroom is high, launch with monitoring. If it is moderate, stage the rollout. If it is near zero, delay and build contingency stock.
Action: Put tariff headroom into the launch dashboard next to margin and service level.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Pull the tariff schedule and identify the bound tariff for each product line you import or export.
2. Compare applied tariffs with bound tariffs to see where the policy buffer is thin.
3. Screen each market for quotas, bans, or other quantitative restrictions that create planning risk.
4. Verify non-discrimination across trading partners before expanding distribution or procurement.
5. Check whether any health, safety, national security, or balance-of-payments exception is in play.
6. Add contractual flexibility for customs change, especially in multi-year sourcing and delivery deals.
7. Build a monthly compliance dashboard with tariff, quota, and publication-status updates.
