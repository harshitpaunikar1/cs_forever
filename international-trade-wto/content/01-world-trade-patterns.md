# World Trade Patterns

## Overview

World trade patterns describe who trades what with whom, in what volumes, and how those flows shift over time. Trade moves goods, services, money, and ideas across borders, creating a web of dependencies between countries. The shape of this web tells you which economies are growing, which are specializing, and where the next shocks are likely to come from.

---

## Why It Matters

Managers who ignore global trade flows get blindsided by supplier shocks, currency swings, and sudden tariff changes. Watching patterns helps you forecast input prices, pick new markets to enter, and understand why a competitor suddenly has a cost advantage. Trade flows also signal political risk — a country that depends heavily on one export is fragile.

## Key Principles

- Trade volumes grow faster than world GDP in most decades, so exposure only increases over time.
- Developed economies dominate services trade; emerging economies dominate manufactured goods trade.
- Regional trade (within Asia, within Europe) often exceeds interregional trade.
- Commodity exporters face wild price swings; diversified exporters ride them out better.
- A few shipping lanes and chokepoints (Suez, Panama, Strait of Hormuz) carry a huge share of global trade.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Trade Volume** | Total value of goods and services crossing borders in a period. |
| **Trade Balance** | Exports minus imports for a country. |
| **Merchandise Trade** | Physical goods crossing borders, as opposed to services. |
| **Trade Intensity** | How much two countries trade with each other relative to their size. |

## Use Case

A consumer electronics brand reviewing its sourcing mix will map annual trade volumes between its factory locations and its main sales markets to decide where to add a second plant. Shifts in bilateral trade flows often reveal new low-cost hubs before wage data catches up.

## Scenario

> A mid-size auto-parts firm sourced 80% of its steel from one country. When a port dispute closed shipping lanes for three weeks, production stopped. After mapping trade-flow data, the firm split sourcing across three regions, cutting single-point risk and saving 4% on landed cost within a year.

## Examples

- Tracking how semiconductor trade flows shift between Taiwan, South Korea, and the US to plan component orders.
- Spotting the long-run rise of India–Africa trade to decide where to open a regional sales office.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Trade-Flow Concentration Map
**Purpose:** Show where concentration creates risk and where diversification creates resilience.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Low concentration ------------------------------> High concentration
  diversified suppliers                           single-country dependence
  multiple routes                                 one route / one port
  multiple markets                                one market / one customer
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Supplier concentration, meaning how much of the input base comes from one source.
Component 2: Route concentration, meaning how dependent the business is on one shipping lane or chokepoint.
Component 3: Market concentration, meaning how dependent revenue is on one country or region.
Component 4: Diversification, meaning the spread of supply and demand across multiple nodes.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI device manufacturer maps chip supply, assembly locations, and export markets; once it sees that one country and one port dominate both inputs and outbound sales, it splits sourcing and sales exposure across regions.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a team has a cheap supply chain but no resilience.

### Trade Pattern Signal Matrix
**Purpose:** Turn flow changes into decisions about sourcing and expansion.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                Improving signal
                       ^
                       |
     Export growth     |     Balanced growth
     in one region     |     trade growth + spread
                       |
-----------------------+------------------------->
                       |
     Volatile signal   |     Fragile signal
     price swings      |     concentrated dependency
                       |
                Worsening signal
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Improving signal, meaning trade flows are supporting resilience or market entry.
Component 2: Balanced growth, meaning rising trade with broad diversification.
Component 3: Volatile signal, meaning price swings or tariff changes are distorting planning.
Component 4: Fragile signal, meaning a narrow export or sourcing base can be disrupted easily.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting team advising a consumer-tech client sees rising India-Africa trade and uses it as a signal to open a regional sales office rather than doubling down on a stagnant corridor.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When trade data is being reviewed for expansion or risk, not just for trivia.

## 4. Formulas
Formula: Trade balance = exports - imports
Variables:
Exports = goods and services sold abroad.
Imports = goods and services purchased from abroad.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether a country is a net seller or net buyer in world trade.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> trade deficit -> dependence on external supply or capital inflow
Value 0- > break-even -> balanced trade -> less external imbalance pressure
Value > 0 -> trade surplus -> stronger export position
Worked example with numbers: If a country exports 120 and imports 150, the trade balance is -30. That signals a deficit and may point to imported-input dependence. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: Trade intensity = bilateral trade value / combined economic size
Variables:
Bilateral trade value = trade between two countries.
Combined economic size = GDP of the two countries or another agreed denominator.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether two economies are trading more than you would expect from size alone.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 1 -> low intensity -> room to deepen trade ties
Value 1-2 -> typical intensity -> normal commercial linkage
Value > 2 -> very high intensity -> strong bilateral dependency
Worked example with numbers: Two countries trade 40 billion against a combined GDP base of 20 billion in the chosen denominator, giving an intensity of 2.0. That says the corridor is highly strategic and worth hedging against disruption. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: Single-point risk ratio = volume from dominant node / total volume
Variables:
Volume from dominant node = supply, demand, or transport volume controlled by one source, route, or market.
Total volume = total volume across all nodes.
Why this formula exists: It answers how exposed the business is to one failure point.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.30 -> diversified -> resilience is relatively strong
Value 0.30-0.60 -> concentrated -> add backup nodes
Value > 0.60 -> fragile -> urgent diversification needed
Worked example with numbers: An auto-parts firm sources 80 percent of steel from one country, so the ratio is 0.80. That is a clear single-point risk and justifies split sourcing. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat low cost as the same thing as low risk. | Compare landed cost and concentration risk together. |
| Rely on one country for a critical input. | Diversify sourcing across regions or suppliers. |
| Ignore shipping chokepoints. | Map Suez, Panama, Hormuz, and other route bottlenecks. |
| Look only at domestic data when planning exports. | Track bilateral trade flows and regional trade patterns. |
| Assume services trade is irrelevant to trade strategy. | Include software, consulting, and finance services in the trade picture. |
| Wait for a shock before redesigning the network. | Use trade-pattern data to pre-empt supplier shocks and tariff changes. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: Consumer electronics company planning a second plant
Situation: A consumer electronics brand sells into Europe and North America but assembles most products in one Asian location. It is reviewing trade patterns to decide where a second plant should sit.
Applicable framework/metric: Trade-Flow Concentration Map; single-point risk ratio.
Analysis: If 70 percent of inputs and 65 percent of outbound sales run through one corridor, the network is too concentrated. The company should diversify both supply and demand nodes rather than chase the lowest current freight rate.
Decision rule: If single-point risk > 0.60, diversify quickly; if 0.30-0.60, build backup capacity; if below 0.30, keep monitoring.
Action: Compare two alternate manufacturing corridors and score them on landed cost, tariff exposure, and route resilience.

Scenario 2: Product team evaluating regional expansion
Situation: A B2B product company sees strong growth in India-Africa trade and wants to know whether the signal is durable enough to justify opening a regional sales office.
Applicable framework/metric: Trade Pattern Signal Matrix; trade intensity.
Analysis: If bilateral trade intensity keeps rising and is supported by broader regional trade, it is a strong signal. If the growth is concentrated in one commodity or one port, the team should be more cautious.
Decision rule: If intensity > 2 and the flow is diversified, expand; if intensity is high but fragile, pilot first; if intensity is low, stay put.
Action: Build a country shortlist, test distributor availability, and run landed-cost scenarios before committing headcount.

Scenario 3: Consulting team advising an AI hardware company
Situation: A consulting team is helping an AI hardware company reduce production disruption after a port closure interrupted chip deliveries. The goal is to redesign the sourcing network without blowing up cost.
Applicable framework/metric: Trade-Flow Concentration Map; landed cost.
Analysis: The client finds that the cheapest option also carries the highest chokepoint risk. A modest landed-cost increase is justified if it materially reduces the chance of a plant shutdown.
Decision rule: If the resilience gain is large and landed cost rises less than the disruption cost, diversify; if the opposite is true, keep the current route; if both are poor, redesign the network.
Action: Add a second supplier region, pre-negotiate alternate freight lanes, and update the business continuity playbook.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Map every critical input, route, and sales market to a source country.
2. Calculate concentration ratios for supply, logistics, and demand.
3. Overlay chokepoints and tariff exposure on the trade map.
4. Compare landed cost under at least two sourcing scenarios.
5. Separate commodity risk from services trade risk in planning.
6. Build a backup sourcing plan for any node above the concentration threshold.
7. Track bilateral trade flow trends for markets you may enter next.
8. Review the map quarterly so it moves with trade and policy changes.
