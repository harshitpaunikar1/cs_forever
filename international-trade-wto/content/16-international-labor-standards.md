# International Labor Standards

## Overview

International labor standards are the basic rules on worker rights — freedom of association, no forced or child labor, no discrimination, safe conditions. They are set mainly by the International Labor Organization (ILO), not the WTO. But they show up in trade deals, import rules, and buyer codes of conduct more and more each year.

---

## Why It Matters

Buyers in rich markets face pressure from regulators and consumers to clean up their supply chains. A single forced-labor scandal can block shipments and wreck a brand. Managers sourcing globally must track both the legal rules (import bans, due-diligence laws) and the buyer expectations shaping everyday contracts.

## Key Principles

- Core ILO conventions cover freedom of association, forced labor, child labor, and discrimination.
- Trade agreements increasingly include labor chapters with commitments.
- Import bans on forced-labor goods are expanding in major markets.
- Due diligence laws require buyers to check supplier labor practices.
- Certification and audits help but do not fully substitute for on-the-ground verification.

## Use Case

A garment buyer builds a tiered audit system for its suppliers — announced audits, surprise audits, worker interviews — to comply with new due-diligence laws in its main sales markets.

## Scenario

> An apparel importer got hit with a forced-labor ban on shipments from one foreign region. Containers were seized at port. The importer mapped its deep supply chain, cut off the flagged tier-3 supplier, and rebuilt sourcing through audited factories in two other countries. Recovery took nine months and cost millions, but sales resumed.

- A trade deal's labor chapter forcing a partner country to reform union-registration rules.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Supplier Labor-Risk Matrix
**Purpose:** Separate low-risk suppliers from suppliers that need deep review, remediation, or exit.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                 High traceability
              +-----------------------+
High legal    |  Monitor and improve   |  Scale with confidence
exposure      |  with controls         |  if controls stay strong
              +-----------------------+
              |  Remediate or exit     |  Accept only with heavy
Low legal     |  quickly               |  due diligence
exposure      +-----------------------+
                 Low traceability
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Legal exposure: how likely the supplier is to trigger import bans, regulatory scrutiny, or contract breach.
Traceability: how well you can see sub-tier suppliers, labor practices, and evidence of compliance.
High legal exposure with low traceability is the worst case because the risk is hard to prove away and hard to manage.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI device company buys battery packs from a contract manufacturer that cannot document tier-2 labor practices. The supplier sits in the "remediate or exit" quadrant until the team gets sub-tier mapping, worker interviews, and an audit trail.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team wants to scale a supplier but the evidence on labor practices is incomplete.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: Supplier labor-risk score = tier depth x country risk x audit gap [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
tier depth = how far down the supply chain the risk sits
country risk = baseline labor-rights risk in the source market
audit gap = how much of the supplier chain remains unverified
Why this formula exists: It estimates how likely a hidden labor issue is to surface later as a legal or shipment problem.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.25 -> low risk -> keep monitoring
Value 0.25-0.50 -> medium risk -> tighten controls and re-audit
Value > 0.50 -> high risk -> remediate, pause, or replace the supplier
Worked example with numbers: A product company has tier depth 0.8, country risk 0.7, and audit gap 0.6. The score is 0.336. That is medium risk, so the buyer should keep the supplier only with stronger evidence and more frequent checks.

Formula: Compliance coverage ratio = verified suppliers / total suppliers [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
verified suppliers = suppliers with current audit or certification evidence
total suppliers = all active suppliers in scope
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the buyer can actually defend its due-diligence claim.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.60 -> weak coverage -> the program is not defensible
Value 0.60-0.85 -> workable coverage -> keep improving and target blind spots
Value > 0.85 -> strong coverage -> maintain and spot-check
Worked example with numbers: A consulting-led procurement team has 34 verified suppliers out of 40 in scope. Coverage = 0.85. That is just on the strong edge, so the next move is to audit the remaining six, especially the highest-risk geographies.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat labor standards as an HR-only issue. | Manage them as a sourcing, legal, and brand-risk issue. |
| Assume a certificate proves the chain is clean. | Pair certification with on-the-ground verification and worker interviews. |
| Wait for a seizure or scandal before acting. | Screen suppliers before volume scales and before contracts renew. |
| Stop at your direct supplier. | Map tier-2 and tier-3 suppliers where forced-labor risk often hides. |
| Keep a risky supplier because switching is inconvenient. | Use remediation timelines and exit criteria tied to business impact. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

Scenario 1: AI hardware buyer facing forced-labor risk
Situation: An AI device company imports cable assemblies from a contract manufacturer with incomplete sub-tier records. Customs scrutiny rises, and the sales team wants no shipment delays.
Applicable framework/metric: Supplier labor-risk score and compliance coverage ratio.
Analysis: The supplier scores 0.54 on labor risk because tier depth is deep, country risk is elevated, and the audit gap is large. Coverage for the category is 0.62 because only 31 of 50 suppliers are verified.
Decision rule: If risk is >0.50 or coverage is <0.60, pause scale-up. If risk is 0.25-0.50, remediate. If risk is <0.25 and coverage is >0.85, keep shipping.
Action: Freeze new volume until the supplier maps sub-tiers, then add a second source in a lower-risk location.

Scenario 2: Consulting team designing a due-diligence program
Situation: A consulting team is helping a retail client build a global supplier-risk program after a forced-labor import scare. The board wants a process that is defensible, not just PR-friendly.
Applicable framework/metric: Compliance coverage ratio.
Analysis: The client has 120 active suppliers and 99 verified through audits or certifications, so coverage is 0.825. That is workable, but the unverified 21 suppliers are concentrated in high-risk categories.
Decision rule: If coverage is below 0.60, build the program from scratch. If it is 0.60-0.85, focus on high-risk gaps. If above 0.85, move to monitoring and spot checks.
Action: Prioritize the unverified suppliers in the highest-risk geographies, then require contract clauses and annual revalidation.

Scenario 3: Product leader handling a supplier scandal
Situation: A consumer-tech product team discovers that a lower-tier supplier may be using forced labor. The brand team wants a public response, and the operations team wants the fastest path back to shipping.
Applicable framework/metric: Supplier labor-risk score plus remediation timeline.
Analysis: The chain is high risk because traceability is poor and the issue sits at tier-3. The company can recover only if it can isolate the supplier, prove remediation, and restore compliance evidence.
Decision rule: If the risk cannot be traced within two weeks, exit the supplier. If traceability returns but evidence remains incomplete, keep the line on hold. If evidence is clean, restart with monitored volume.
Action: Cut off the flagged tier, rebuild sourcing through audited factories, and lock a public escalation process for future incidents.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build a supplier map that includes direct and sub-tier vendors.
2. Add forced-labor, child-labor, discrimination, and safety checks to the sourcing scorecard.
3. Require contract language for due diligence, audits, and remediation deadlines.
4. Set a verification calendar with announced audits, surprise audits, and worker interviews.
5. Define escalation rules for import bans, legal notices, and customer complaints.
6. Track certification, audit findings, and corrective actions in one dashboard.
7. Review high-risk suppliers before each volume increase or renewal cycle.
