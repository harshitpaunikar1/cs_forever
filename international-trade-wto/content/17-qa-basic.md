# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and concepts across international trade and WTO. Questions Q1 through Q70.

## Q1. What is international trade?

**Level:** Basic

International trade is the buying and selling of goods and services across national borders. It includes both imports (what a country buys from abroad) and exports (what it sells abroad). Countries trade because different places have different resources, costs, and skills. Trade is measured in value, volume, and balance terms.

**Real-life applications:**
- A car maker exporting finished vehicles to another continent.
- A coffee chain importing beans from multiple growing countries.
- A consulting firm selling services to clients in ten countries.
- A retailer sourcing packaging from the cheapest international supplier.

**Key concepts:** `exports`, `imports`, `trade volume`, `globalization`, `cross-border commerce`

---

## Q2. What is meant by world trade patterns?

**Level:** Basic

World trade patterns describe who trades what with whom and how those flows change over time. They show the direction, volume, and composition of global trade. Patterns include which countries export which products, which regions trade most with each other, and how shares shift as economies grow or shrink.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking the shift of electronics manufacturing between Asian hubs.
- Watching African countries increase trade with Asia over Europe.
- Mapping which countries dominate rare-earth mineral exports.
- Forecasting port capacity needs along a major shipping lane.

**Key concepts:** `trade flows`, `trade composition`, `export share`, `regional trade`, `trade direction`

---

## Q3. What is the theory of comparative advantage?

**Level:** Basic

Comparative advantage is the idea that countries should produce and export what they can make at the lowest opportunity cost, and import the rest. Even if a country is worse at making everything, it still gains by specializing in what it is least bad at. Both sides get richer through trade.

**Real-life applications:**
- A high-wage country importing low-skilled textiles and exporting software.
- An outsourcing firm locating call centers in lower-wage English-speaking countries.
- A winemaker specializing in premium wine and importing bulk wine.
- A farmer growing high-value spices instead of basic grains for export.

**Key concepts:** `opportunity cost`, `specialization`, `gains from trade`, `factor endowment`, `Ricardo`

---

## Q4. What is the difference between absolute and comparative advantage?

**Level:** Basic

Absolute advantage means producing more with the same inputs than another country. Comparative advantage means producing at a lower opportunity cost, even if you're absolutely worse at everything. Comparative, not absolute, advantage is what drives beneficial trade between countries.

**Real-life applications:**
- A superstar doctor who hires a nurse to do intake, freeing time for surgery.
- A tech firm offshoring testing to focus scarce senior talent on architecture.
- A developed country importing basic goods despite being able to make them.
- A skilled lawyer hiring a paralegal even if the lawyer could do both jobs better.

**Key concepts:** `absolute advantage`, `comparative advantage`, `opportunity cost`, `specialization`, `trade gains`

---

## Q5. What is a tariff?

**Level:** Basic

A tariff is a tax a country charges on imported goods. It makes imports more expensive so domestic producers can compete. Tariffs raise government revenue and also influence trade patterns by discouraging imports.

**Real-life applications:**
- A 10% tariff on imported cars raising their retail price.
- A country using steel tariffs to protect domestic mills.
- A government cutting tariffs on medical equipment to lower healthcare costs.
- An exporter routing shipments through an FTA partner to avoid a tariff.

**Key concepts:** `import tax`, `protectionism`, `bound tariff`, `applied tariff`, `tariff revenue`

---

## Q6. What are non-tariff barriers?

**Level:** Basic

Non-tariff barriers (NTBs) are trade restrictions other than tariffs — things like quotas, licensing rules, technical standards, and customs delays. They often block imports more effectively than tariffs, and they are harder to measure and challenge.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country requiring unique labeling that only domestic firms can meet easily.
- Complex import licensing that delays foreign shipments by weeks.
- Safety inspections applied more strictly to imports than domestic goods.
- Local content rules that force foreign firms to source parts domestically.

**Key concepts:** `NTB`, `quota`, `licensing`, `technical regulation`, `administrative barrier`

---

## Q7. What is the World Trade Organization (WTO)?

**Level:** Basic

The WTO is the international body that sets and enforces rules for trade between countries. It was founded in 1995 and has over 160 member countries. Its main jobs are to run trade agreements, host negotiations, settle disputes, and monitor trade policies.

**Real-life applications:**
- A government filing a case at the WTO against unfair tariffs.
- Countries negotiating lower tariffs under WTO rounds.
- A business checking WTO-bound tariff rates before signing contracts.
- The WTO reviewing a member country's trade-policy changes.

**Key concepts:** `WTO`, `trade body`, `member states`, `multilateral system`, `rules-based trade`

---

## Q8. What is GATT?

**Level:** Basic

GATT stands for the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. The original version was signed in 1947 to cut tariffs after World War II. GATT 1994 is the updated version that lives inside the WTO and covers trade in goods.

**Real-life applications:**
- A customs office applying GATT rules to determine tariff treatment.
- A country publishing its GATT schedule of bound tariffs.
- An exporter citing GATT articles when challenging a foreign quota.
- A panel ruling based on GATT 1994 principles in a WTO case.

**Key concepts:** `GATT`, `trade in goods`, `bound tariff`, `GATT 1994`, `multilateral agreement`

---

## Q9. What is Most Favored Nation (MFN) treatment?

**Level:** Basic

MFN means that if you give any trade advantage to one WTO member, you must give the same advantage to all WTO members. It stops countries from playing favorites and keeps trade rules broad. MFN is one of the pillars of the WTO.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country cutting tariffs on autos extending the same rate to all WTO members.
- An exporter in a small country getting the same tariff as a big exporter.
- A government revoking MFN as a political sanction against another country.
- A business comparing MFN and preferential rates before shipping.

**Key concepts:** `MFN`, `non-discrimination`, `equal treatment`, `tariff`, `WTO principle`

---

## Q10. What is National Treatment?

**Level:** Basic

National Treatment means that once a foreign product enters a country, it must be treated the same as a domestic product. No higher taxes, no tougher rules, no extra inspections just because it is imported. This principle prevents internal discrimination against imports.

**Real-life applications:**
- A foreign beverage taxed the same as a similar domestic one.
- Imported cars meeting the same emissions tests as domestic cars.
- A WTO case challenging higher VAT on imported goods.
- A country equalizing labeling rules for foreign and local products.

**Key concepts:** `national treatment`, `like products`, `internal tax`, `non-discrimination`, `WTO principle`

---

## Q11. What are the main functions of the WTO?

**Level:** Basic

The WTO administers trade agreements, provides a forum for trade negotiations, settles disputes between members, monitors national trade policies, and offers technical help to developing countries. Together these functions make trade more predictable and rules-based.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running the Doha Round of trade negotiations.
- Hearing a dispute between two members over subsidies.
- Publishing trade policy reviews on every member country.
- Helping a least-developed country build its customs capacity.

**Key concepts:** `administration`, `negotiation`, `dispute settlement`, `policy review`, `technical assistance`

---

## Q12. What is the difference between exports and imports?

**Level:** Basic

Exports are goods and services a country sells to another country. Imports are what it buys from abroad. When exports exceed imports, a country has a trade surplus; when imports exceed exports, a trade deficit.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country reporting a monthly trade surplus in manufactured goods.
- A firm filing export paperwork before shipping abroad.
- A government tracking imports to forecast foreign-exchange needs.
- A business calculating value added based on imported inputs.

**Key concepts:** `exports`, `imports`, `trade balance`, `surplus`, `deficit`

---

## Q13. What is a trade surplus?

**Level:** Basic

A trade surplus happens when a country exports more than it imports in value terms. It means foreigners are paying in more than the country is paying out for traded goods and services. Sustained surpluses often build up foreign reserves.

**Real-life applications:**
- An oil-exporting country running big surpluses when prices rise.
- A manufacturing hub like Germany sustaining long-term surpluses.
- A country using surplus savings to buy foreign bonds.
- A currency strengthening as foreigners buy the exporter's money.

**Key concepts:** `trade surplus`, `exports`, `imports`, `balance of trade`, `foreign reserves`

---

## Q14. What is a trade deficit?

**Level:** Basic

A trade deficit happens when imports exceed exports. A country buys more from abroad than it sells. Deficits must be financed, usually by selling assets or borrowing from abroad, which can be healthy or risky depending on scale and duration.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country borrowing to finance persistent deficits.
- A growing economy importing capital equipment to industrialize.
- A currency weakening when deficits grow too fast.
- A government cutting imports to stabilize the trade balance.

**Key concepts:** `trade deficit`, `current account`, `foreign borrowing`, `imports`, `balance of payments`

---

## Q15. What is a customs union?

**Level:** Basic

A customs union is a group of countries that remove tariffs between themselves and share a common external tariff against non-members. It goes deeper than a free-trade agreement because members set the same tariff on outsiders, speaking with one voice in trade deals.

**Real-life applications:**
- The European Union applying one tariff to goods from outside the bloc.
- MERCOSUR members negotiating trade deals as a group.
- A firm facing the same tariff in any EU country once cleared at one port.
- Joint customs procedures cutting compliance paperwork.

**Key concepts:** `customs union`, `common external tariff`, `FTA`, `regional bloc`, `integration`

---

## Q16. What is a free trade area?

**Level:** Basic

A free trade area is a group of countries that remove tariffs on goods traded among themselves but keep their own external tariffs. Rules of origin decide which products qualify as "made" in the area and get tariff-free treatment.

**Real-life applications:**
- USMCA members shipping goods duty-free between each other.
- A Mexican car qualifying under North American content rules.
- An ASEAN electronics firm sourcing parts regionally to hit rules of origin.
- A Korean firm using an FTA to avoid tariffs in a partner country.

**Key concepts:** `FTA`, `rules of origin`, `regional trade`, `preferential tariff`, `trade creation`

---

## Q17. What are rules of origin?

**Level:** Basic

Rules of origin are criteria used to decide where a product is "from" for trade purposes. They determine whether a product qualifies for preferential tariff treatment under a trade agreement. They usually look at where value was added or where materials came from.

**Real-life applications:**
- A laptop qualifying as "North American" only if 60% of value is from member countries.
- Firms restructuring sourcing to meet threshold content rules.
- Customs officers verifying origin certificates at the border.
- Trade negotiators arguing over which processes count as "substantial transformation."

**Key concepts:** `rules of origin`, `substantial transformation`, `value added`, `certificate of origin`, `preferential tariff`

---

## Q18. What is dumping in international trade?

**Level:** Basic

Dumping is exporting a product at a price below its home-market price or below production cost. It is considered unfair trade because it can wipe out domestic producers in the importing country. WTO rules allow affected countries to respond with anti-dumping duties.

**Real-life applications:**
- A steel exporter selling abroad below its home price.
- A domestic industry filing an anti-dumping petition.
- A country imposing extra duties on specific exporting firms.
- A trade panel reviewing anti-dumping investigations.

**Key concepts:** `dumping`, `anti-dumping duty`, `fair value`, `injury`, `unfair trade`

---

## Q19. What is an anti-dumping duty?

**Level:** Basic

An anti-dumping duty is an extra tariff imposed on imports that have been found to be dumped and causing injury to the domestic industry. It offsets the unfair pricing. The duty must be based on a formal investigation following WTO rules.

**Real-life applications:**
- A 40% anti-dumping duty on imported tires from one country.
- A producer petitioning for investigation after price drops hurt sales.
- A firm facing margin calculations during an anti-dumping case.
- Countries negotiating settlements to avoid long-term anti-dumping duties.

**Key concepts:** `anti-dumping duty`, `dumping margin`, `injury test`, `trade remedy`, `WTO`

---

## Q20. What is a countervailing duty?

**Level:** Basic

A countervailing duty is a tariff imposed to offset an unfair subsidy granted by a foreign government to its exporters. If the subsidy hurts domestic industry in the importing country, a countervailing duty levels the field.

**Real-life applications:**
- A duty on subsidized solar panels to protect domestic makers.
- A government providing cheap loans to exporters triggering a case.
- A steel industry petitioning after foreign mills get state-backed electricity.
- A WTO panel reviewing whether a subsidy is specific to export.

**Key concepts:** `countervailing duty`, `subsidy`, `unfair trade`, `trade remedy`, `export support`

---

## Q21. What is a safeguard measure?

**Level:** Basic

A safeguard is a temporary import restriction imposed when a sudden surge of fairly traded imports causes serious injury to a domestic industry. Unlike anti-dumping, safeguards do not require proof of unfair practices, just a surge and injury.

**Real-life applications:**
- A tire industry winning a temporary tariff after imports doubled in a year.
- A country imposing a quota on steel during a global glut.
- A safeguard lasting four years while domestic firms modernize.
- A firm using the safeguard period to invest in new machinery.

**Key concepts:** `safeguard`, `import surge`, `serious injury`, `temporary protection`, `adjustment`

---

## Q22. What is the Dispute Settlement Understanding (DSU)?

**Level:** Basic

The DSU is the WTO's legal process for handling disputes between member countries. It has fixed time limits, a panel system, an appellate body, and enforcement through authorized retaliation. It replaces trade wars with legal rulings.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country filing a complaint over illegal tariffs at the WTO.
- A three-person panel hearing arguments from both sides.
- An appellate body ruling on legal issues in a panel report.
- Authorized retaliation if the losing side refuses to comply.

**Key concepts:** `DSU`, `panel`, `appellate body`, `consultation`, `retaliation`

---

## Q23. What is a regional trade agreement (RTA)?

**Level:** Basic

A regional trade agreement is a treaty among a limited group of countries to reduce trade barriers among themselves. Examples include USMCA, the EU, ASEAN, and MERCOSUR. The WTO allows RTAs as exceptions to MFN, provided they cover substantially all trade.

**Real-life applications:**
- ASEAN members trading autos at 0% tariff among themselves.
- A firm choosing a plant location to benefit from an RTA.
- A trade deal opening services markets not covered by the WTO.
- Rules of origin inside an RTA deciding qualifying products.

**Key concepts:** `RTA`, `FTA`, `preferential trade`, `regional integration`, `MFN exception`

---

## Q24. Who gains and who loses from trade?

**Level:** Basic

Consumers generally gain from lower prices and more variety. Producers in competitive export sectors gain. Workers and firms in import-competing sectors often lose. Overall gains usually exceed losses but are unevenly distributed.

**Real-life applications:**
- Shoppers buying cheaper electronics after tariff cuts.
- Export factory workers earning higher wages in booming sectors.
- Manufacturing towns losing jobs to lower-cost imports.
- Governments funding retraining for displaced workers.

**Key concepts:** `gains from trade`, `consumer surplus`, `losers`, `adjustment`, `distribution`

---

## Q25. What is foreign direct investment (FDI)?

**Level:** Basic

Foreign Direct Investment is when a firm or individual from one country builds, buys, or expands business assets in another country with the aim of long-term control. It's different from buying stocks (portfolio investment) because FDI involves management and stays put longer.

**Real-life applications:**
- A German carmaker building a plant in Mexico.
- A foreign investor acquiring a majority stake in a local firm.
- A tech company opening a research center abroad.
- A government offering tax incentives to attract FDI.

**Key concepts:** `FDI`, `greenfield investment`, `acquisition`, `multinational firm`, `capital flow`

---

## Q26. What is the current account?

**Level:** Basic

The current account tracks a country's trade in goods and services, income earned on investments, and transfers (like remittances). It is one half of the balance of payments. A current account deficit is financed by capital inflows.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country reporting monthly current account data.
- Economists forecasting currency trends from current account shifts.
- A remittance-dependent country counting transfers in its current account.
- A central bank watching the current account for stability signals.

**Key concepts:** `current account`, `balance of payments`, `trade balance`, `income`, `transfers`

---

## Q27. What is the capital account?

**Level:** Basic

The capital account (often called the financial account) records cross-border investment flows — FDI, portfolio flows, loans, and official reserves. It mirrors the current account: a current account deficit is funded by capital inflows.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country receiving billions in foreign bond purchases.
- A multinational's FDI showing up as a capital inflow.
- Central bank reserve changes entering capital-account data.
- A sudden stop in capital flows triggering a currency crisis.

**Key concepts:** `capital account`, `financial account`, `FDI`, `portfolio flow`, `reserves`

---

## Q28. What is protectionism?

**Level:** Basic

Protectionism is the policy of shielding domestic industries from foreign competition using tariffs, quotas, subsidies, or regulations. Governments use it to save jobs or nurture young industries, but it often raises consumer prices and hurts export industries.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country raising tariffs on imported textiles to protect local mills.
- Subsidies to keep a struggling auto industry alive.
- Local-content rules in public procurement.
- Political campaigns promising "buy local" policies.

**Key concepts:** `protectionism`, `tariff`, `quota`, `subsidy`, `infant industry`

---

## Q29. What is free trade?

**Level:** Basic

Free trade is a policy of letting goods and services move across borders with little or no government-imposed restriction. It relies on tariffs being low or zero and non-tariff barriers being minimal. Free trade aims to maximize efficiency and consumer welfare.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two countries signing an FTA to eliminate tariffs.
- A consumer paying lower prices because imports face no tariff.
- A firm sourcing inputs from wherever they are cheapest.
- A government joining a trade bloc to expand free-trade access.

**Key concepts:** `free trade`, `tariff reduction`, `open market`, `trade liberalization`, `efficiency`

---

## Q30. What is globalization?

**Level:** Basic

Globalization is the long-term process of countries and economies becoming more integrated through trade, investment, migration, technology, and information. It accelerates when transport and communication costs fall and trade rules open.

**Real-life applications:**
- A smartphone designed in one country, assembled in another, sold worldwide.
- A global retailer sourcing from 50 countries.
- A consultant working remotely for clients across time zones.
- Global supply chains cutting production times in half.

**Key concepts:** `globalization`, `integration`, `supply chain`, `trade`, `technology`

---

## Q31. What is a quota?

**Level:** Basic

A quota is a quantitative limit on how much of a product can be imported in a given period. Unlike a tariff, it caps volume rather than raising price. WTO rules generally prohibit quotas because they distort trade more than tariffs.

**Real-life applications:**
- An import limit of 100,000 tons of sugar per year.
- A voluntary export restraint agreed between two countries.
- A WTO case challenging a quota as illegal.
- A quota auction raising revenue for the importing government.

**Key concepts:** `quota`, `quantitative restriction`, `import limit`, `GATT`, `trade barrier`

---

## Q32. What is a subsidy in trade?

**Level:** Basic

A subsidy is a government financial benefit given to producers — a grant, tax break, or cheap loan. If it lowers export prices unfairly or harms foreign competitors, it can trigger countervailing duties under WTO rules.

**Real-life applications:**
- A government giving tax breaks to solar-panel makers.
- Cheap state loans to an export-focused firm.
- A WTO case over subsidies to a national aircraft maker.
- A country removing an illegal subsidy after a panel ruling.

**Key concepts:** `subsidy`, `countervailing duty`, `state aid`, `export support`, `WTO rules`

---

## Q33. What are the core WTO principles?

**Level:** Basic

The core WTO principles are non-discrimination (MFN and National Treatment), predictability (binding tariffs), transparency, promoting fair competition, encouraging development, and allowing some exceptions for health, safety, and security.

**Real-life applications:**
- A tariff schedule locked in at the WTO.
- Equal tax treatment for imports and domestic products.
- Publication of trade laws for foreign exporters to see.
- Longer transition times for developing economies.

**Key concepts:** `non-discrimination`, `predictability`, `transparency`, `fair competition`, `development`

---

## Q34. What is meant by binding tariffs at the WTO?

**Level:** Basic

Binding a tariff means a country commits to not raise its tariff above a stated ceiling. The "bound rate" is the legal maximum. The "applied rate" is what is actually charged and is often lower. Bindings make trade predictable.

**Real-life applications:**
- A manufacturer planning five-year exports using bound rates.
- A country negotiating lower bound rates in trade talks.
- A government raising the applied tariff up to the bound ceiling.
- A firm using bound rates to forecast worst-case tariff costs.

**Key concepts:** `bound tariff`, `applied tariff`, `tariff ceiling`, `schedule of concessions`, `predictability`

---

## Q35. What is trade liberalization?

**Level:** Basic

Trade liberalization is the process of removing or lowering trade barriers — tariffs, quotas, and regulations. It can be unilateral, regional, or multilateral. Its goal is to increase trade and the gains from it.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country cutting average tariffs from 25% to 10%.
- A trade deal eliminating duties across a partner bloc.
- A unilateral cut to lower consumer prices.
- The WTO overseeing multilateral tariff-reduction rounds.

**Key concepts:** `liberalization`, `tariff cut`, `open market`, `WTO round`, `multilateral`

---

## Q36. What is a trade bloc?

**Level:** Basic

A trade bloc is a group of countries with a shared trade agreement — an FTA, customs union, or common market. Members gain preferential access among themselves and often negotiate as a group with outsiders.

**Real-life applications:**
- The EU acting as one trade negotiator for 27 countries.
- ASEAN members offering tariff-free trade to each other.
- MERCOSUR coordinating trade policy across South American members.
- A firm relocating to benefit from a trade bloc's rules.

**Key concepts:** `trade bloc`, `regional integration`, `FTA`, `customs union`, `common market`

---

## Q37. What are the main arguments for free trade?

**Level:** Basic

Free trade lowers prices for consumers, lets firms specialize in what they do best, expands markets, encourages competition and innovation, and raises overall economic output. The gains exceed the losses on average.

**Real-life applications:**
- Shoppers buying cheaper imported electronics.
- Firms expanding exports into new markets.
- Companies competing harder to match global prices.
- Industries specializing where they have a cost edge.

**Key concepts:** `efficiency`, `consumer surplus`, `competition`, `specialization`, `innovation`

---

## Q38. What are the main arguments against free trade?

**Level:** Basic

Critics say free trade destroys jobs in import-competing sectors, widens inequality, allows race-to-the-bottom standards, and exposes countries to supply shocks. Infant industries may need protection to grow. National security sectors may need shielding.

**Real-life applications:**
- Manufacturing towns losing jobs to cheap imports.
- Rising income inequality between skilled and unskilled workers.
- Firms moving to countries with weak labor rules.
- Pandemic supply-chain crises highlighting import dependence.

**Key concepts:** `job loss`, `inequality`, `race to the bottom`, `infant industry`, `national security`

---

## Q39. What is the infant industry argument?

**Level:** Basic

The infant industry argument says a new domestic industry needs temporary protection from imports to grow to competitive scale. Once mature, protection is removed. In practice, protection often becomes permanent and breeds inefficiency.

**Real-life applications:**
- A developing country tariff on auto imports to nurture domestic assembly.
- Solar manufacturing getting subsidies and tariffs to scale.
- A protected industry lobbying against ever losing its shield.
- Economists debating whether specific infant policies worked.

**Key concepts:** `infant industry`, `temporary protection`, `scale economies`, `industrial policy`, `protectionism`

---

## Q40. What is export promotion?

**Level:** Basic

Export promotion is a policy strategy of boosting exports through subsidies, tax breaks, trade missions, export credit, and special zones. Many East Asian economies used export-led growth to industrialize rapidly in the 20th century.

**Real-life applications:**
- An export credit agency insuring overseas shipments.
- Special Economic Zones with tax breaks for exporters.
- Government trade missions helping firms find buyers abroad.
- Refundable VAT on exported goods.

**Key concepts:** `export promotion`, `export subsidy`, `SEZ`, `export credit`, `industrial policy`

---

## Q41. What is import substitution?

**Level:** Basic

Import substitution is a strategy of replacing imports with domestically produced goods, usually through tariffs and subsidies. Popular in Latin America mid-20th century, it often led to inefficient industries and fell out of favor compared to export-led growth.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country raising tariffs on cars to build domestic assembly.
- Local-content rules in electronics.
- Subsidized domestic steel to replace imports.
- Reform programs dismantling long-protected industries.

**Key concepts:** `import substitution`, `industrialization`, `tariff`, `local content`, `industrial policy`

---

## Q42. What is the difference between trade in goods and trade in services?

**Level:** Basic

Trade in goods involves physical products crossing borders. Trade in services involves intangible activities — banking, consulting, tourism, IT — which may not cross physically but are delivered across borders, through presence, or to consumers abroad.

**Real-life applications:**
- A call center in one country serving customers in another.
- A tourist traveling abroad counts as services trade.
- Software exports delivered electronically.
- A foreign bank branch providing services locally.

**Key concepts:** `goods trade`, `services trade`, `GATS`, `modes of supply`, `intangible`

---

## Q43. What is GATS?

**Level:** Basic

GATS is the General Agreement on Trade in Services, part of the WTO. It covers four modes of service delivery: cross-border, consumption abroad, commercial presence, and presence of natural persons. Members schedule sectoral commitments.

**Real-life applications:**
- A law firm setting up an office in a foreign market under GATS.
- A consultant traveling abroad to deliver services under mode 4.
- A country opening telecoms under a GATS commitment.
- A WTO case about a services-trade restriction.

**Key concepts:** `GATS`, `modes of supply`, `services trade`, `WTO`, `commitments`

---

## Q44. What is TRIPS?

**Level:** Basic

TRIPS stands for Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights. It is a WTO agreement setting minimum standards for patents, copyrights, trademarks, and industrial designs. It ties IP protection to trade rules.

**Real-life applications:**
- A pharmaceutical firm enforcing a patent globally under TRIPS.
- Countries adjusting copyright terms to meet TRIPS minimums.
- A compulsory licensing dispute over essential medicines.
- A firm registering a trademark in multiple WTO member countries.

**Key concepts:** `TRIPS`, `intellectual property`, `patents`, `copyright`, `WTO`

---

## Q45. What is the balance of trade?

**Level:** Basic

The balance of trade is the difference between the value of a country's exports and imports of goods (and sometimes services). A surplus means exports exceed imports; a deficit means the opposite.

**Real-life applications:**
- A ministry reporting a monthly trade balance figure.
- An economist analyzing long-term surplus economies.
- A currency weakening on a bigger-than-expected deficit.
- A firm noting how trade balance changes affect demand.

**Key concepts:** `trade balance`, `exports`, `imports`, `surplus`, `deficit`

---

## Q46. What is reciprocity in trade?

**Level:** Basic

Reciprocity means countries exchange equivalent concessions in trade deals — if you cut tariffs on my exports, I'll cut mine on yours. It's a core principle of negotiating balanced agreements at the WTO and bilaterally.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two countries exchanging matching tariff cuts in an FTA.
- Negotiators linking one sector's concession to another.
- A bilateral agreement built on reciprocal market access.
- Developing countries sometimes exempt from strict reciprocity.

**Key concepts:** `reciprocity`, `negotiation`, `concession`, `market access`, `balance`

---

## Q47. What is meant by market access?

**Level:** Basic

Market access is the ability of a firm to sell into a foreign market, shaped by tariffs, quotas, regulations, and other barriers. Improving market access is a top goal in trade negotiations.

**Real-life applications:**
- An exporter winning reduced tariffs under a new trade deal.
- A services firm getting a license to operate in a foreign market.
- A quota being replaced by a lower tariff.
- Customs delays being cut, improving practical market access.

**Key concepts:** `market access`, `tariffs`, `NTBs`, `negotiation`, `export`

---

## Q48. What is economic integration?

**Level:** Basic

Economic integration is the process of countries combining their economies through trade liberalization, shared regulations, and sometimes common currencies or labor markets. It runs from simple FTAs to full economic unions.

**Real-life applications:**
- EU single market allowing free movement of goods and labor.
- ASEAN harmonizing customs procedures.
- A common currency between a group of countries.
- Shared regulatory approval processes cutting paperwork.

**Key concepts:** `integration`, `FTA`, `customs union`, `single market`, `economic union`

---

## Q49. What is the Uruguay Round?

**Level:** Basic

The Uruguay Round (1986–1994) was a major set of multilateral trade negotiations that expanded GATT and created the WTO. It added services (GATS), intellectual property (TRIPS), and strengthened dispute settlement.

**Real-life applications:**
- The creation of the WTO in 1995 after the round concluded.
- Inclusion of services trade under GATS.
- Strengthened dispute settlement through the DSU.
- Lower tariffs on agricultural and industrial products.

**Key concepts:** `Uruguay Round`, `WTO creation`, `GATS`, `TRIPS`, `DSU`

---

## Q50. What is the Doha Round?

**Level:** Basic

The Doha Round, started in 2001, was meant to focus on development-friendly trade reforms — agriculture, services, and market access for poor countries. It stalled over disagreements between developed and developing countries.

**Real-life applications:**
- A partial deal on trade facilitation signed in Bali in 2013.
- Developing countries pushing for agricultural reforms.
- Stalled talks shifting negotiations to RTAs and bilateral deals.
- Fragmented outcomes instead of a single "Doha package."

**Key concepts:** `Doha Round`, `development`, `agriculture`, `WTO negotiation`, `trade facilitation`

---

## Q51. What is trade facilitation?

**Level:** Basic

Trade facilitation means simplifying and speeding up cross-border procedures — customs paperwork, inspections, clearance. It lowers transaction costs for exporters and importers. The WTO's Trade Facilitation Agreement entered force in 2017.

**Real-life applications:**
- A single electronic customs window cutting paperwork time.
- Pre-arrival processing so trucks clear the border in minutes.
- Risk-based inspection instead of 100% checking.
- Published customs rules so firms can comply easily.

**Key concepts:** `trade facilitation`, `customs`, `TFA`, `single window`, `transaction cost`

---

## Q52. What is a tariff schedule?

**Level:** Basic

A tariff schedule is a country's official list of tariffs by product code (usually the Harmonized System). It shows the duty charged on each imported item and is submitted to the WTO as the country's commitment.

**Real-life applications:**
- A customs officer looking up the HS code to apply tariffs.
- An exporter checking the tariff schedule before pricing a shipment.
- A country updating its schedule after tariff negotiations.
- A firm reclassifying products to seek a lower tariff code.

**Key concepts:** `tariff schedule`, `HS code`, `bound tariff`, `customs duty`, `classification`

---

## Q53. What is the Harmonized System (HS)?

**Level:** Basic

The Harmonized System is the global product classification code used for customs tariffs and trade statistics. Countries share the first six digits; deeper digits vary. Every traded good has an HS code.

**Real-life applications:**
- A shipment labeled with an HS code for tariff assessment.
- Trade data reported at HS-6-digit level globally.
- Firms arguing over classification to get a better tariff.
- A new HS revision updating codes every few years.

**Key concepts:** `HS code`, `classification`, `customs`, `WCO`, `tariff line`

---

## Q54. What is the balance of payments?

**Level:** Basic

The balance of payments is the full record of a country's economic transactions with the rest of the world, split into the current account and the capital (financial) account. The two must balance over time, with reserves adjusting.

**Real-life applications:**
- A central bank publishing quarterly BoP data.
- An economist analyzing BoP pressures before a currency move.
- A country using IMF support when BoP crises hit.
- A government tightening imports to protect reserves.

**Key concepts:** `balance of payments`, `current account`, `capital account`, `reserves`, `IMF`

---

## Q55. What is a multinational corporation (MNC)?

**Level:** Basic

A multinational corporation is a firm that operates in multiple countries, usually headquartered in one but with subsidiaries, factories, or offices abroad. MNCs are the main driver of FDI and global supply chains.

**Real-life applications:**
- A consumer-goods firm with plants in 30 countries.
- A tech firm routing IP through subsidiaries in low-tax locations.
- An automaker shifting production between countries based on costs.
- A retail chain opening stores in new markets.

**Key concepts:** `MNC`, `FDI`, `subsidiary`, `global value chain`, `headquarters`

---

## Q56. What is a global value chain (GVC)?

**Level:** Basic

A global value chain is the sequence of activities needed to produce and sell a product, spread across multiple countries. Design, components, assembly, logistics, and sales often happen in different places based on cost and capability.

**Real-life applications:**
- A smartphone designed in the US, assembled in China, sold globally.
- Automotive parts crossing borders several times before final assembly.
- Apparel cut in one country, sewn in another, finished in a third.
- A firm mapping its GVC to find disruption risks.

**Key concepts:** `GVC`, `supply chain`, `offshoring`, `value added`, `fragmentation`

---

## Q57. What is the Ministerial Conference at the WTO?

**Level:** Basic

The Ministerial Conference is the WTO's top decision-making body, meeting at least every two years. Trade ministers from member countries set the direction of the organization and approve major decisions and negotiating outcomes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Ministers adopting a Ministerial Declaration every two years.
- New members formally admitted at a Ministerial Conference.
- Key negotiating mandates launched at ministerials.
- Civil society protests often coinciding with ministerial meetings.

**Key concepts:** `Ministerial Conference`, `WTO`, `governance`, `trade ministers`, `declaration`

---

## Q58. What is a Trade Policy Review?

**Level:** Basic

A Trade Policy Review is a periodic WTO assessment of a member country's trade policies and practices. It promotes transparency and peer review. Major economies are reviewed every two to three years; smaller ones less often.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country submitting detailed data on tariffs and trade rules.
- WTO secretariat preparing an analytical report.
- Member countries questioning the reviewed country on policies.
- A published review used by businesses and economists for analysis.

**Key concepts:** `TPR`, `transparency`, `peer review`, `trade policy`, `WTO`

---

## Q59. What is an export subsidy?

**Level:** Basic

An export subsidy is a government payment or tax break tied specifically to exporting a product. Most export subsidies are banned under WTO rules because they distort trade. Exceptions exist for developing countries and agriculture historically.

**Real-life applications:**
- A direct payment to exporters per unit shipped abroad.
- A tax refund only applied to exported goods.
- A WTO case against an illegal export subsidy.
- Developing countries phasing out export subsidies as they grow.

**Key concepts:** `export subsidy`, `SCM agreement`, `WTO`, `unfair trade`, `distortion`

---

## Q60. What is the SCM Agreement?

**Level:** Basic

The Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures (SCM) sets WTO rules on what subsidies are prohibited, which are actionable if they harm trade, and how importing countries can apply countervailing duties.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country challenging an export subsidy as prohibited.
- A trade remedy case using SCM rules.
- A country proving a subsidy caused trade injury to win a case.
- Developing countries getting longer transition periods.

**Key concepts:** `SCM`, `subsidy`, `prohibited`, `actionable`, `countervailing duty`

---

## Q61. What is the WTO Secretariat?

**Level:** Basic

The WTO Secretariat, based in Geneva, supports the organization's daily work — research, legal advice, negotiations support, dispute-settlement administration, and trade policy reviews. It does not make policy but serves the members.

**Real-life applications:**
- Secretariat staff preparing a trade policy review report.
- Legal officers advising dispute panels.
- Economists producing world trade statistics.
- Staff translating documents into multiple languages.

**Key concepts:** `WTO Secretariat`, `Geneva`, `Director-General`, `staff`, `research`

---

## Q62. What is the Director-General of the WTO?

**Level:** Basic

The Director-General is the head of the WTO Secretariat. The role provides leadership, represents the WTO publicly, and helps broker deals between members. Members choose the DG by consensus every four years.

**Real-life applications:**
- A DG hosting ministerial meetings to push negotiations.
- Public speeches advocating trade reforms.
- Brokering compromises between developed and developing members.
- Representing the WTO at major summits like the G20.

**Key concepts:** `Director-General`, `WTO`, `leadership`, `governance`, `appointment`

---

## Q63. What are WTO observer countries?

**Level:** Basic

Observer countries are those not yet full WTO members but allowed to attend meetings as they prepare for accession. They must begin negotiations within five years to join.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country applying for observer status before full membership.
- Observers participating in committee meetings without voting rights.
- Reform efforts to align domestic laws with WTO rules.
- Transition to full membership after a multi-year accession process.

**Key concepts:** `observer`, `WTO accession`, `membership`, `reforms`, `transition`

---

## Q64. What is an export?

**Level:** Basic

An export is a good or service produced in one country and sold to a buyer in another country. Exports bring foreign currency into a country and support domestic jobs linked to foreign demand.

**Real-life applications:**
- A farmer shipping grain to a buyer overseas.
- A software firm selling licenses to clients in another country.
- A carmaker delivering finished vehicles to a foreign dealer.
- An engineer providing consulting services to a foreign client.

**Key concepts:** `export`, `foreign sales`, `revenue`, `trade surplus`, `foreign exchange`

---

## Q65. What is an import?

**Level:** Basic

An import is a good or service bought from a foreign producer. Imports provide inputs, consumer products, or services a country does not produce efficiently at home. They expand consumer choice but may compete with domestic producers.

**Real-life applications:**
- A factory importing specialized machinery.
- A retailer stocking imported clothing.
- A hospital buying imported medical equipment.
- A family buying an imported car.

**Key concepts:** `import`, `foreign purchase`, `consumer choice`, `trade deficit`, `input`

---

## Q66. What is an open economy?

**Level:** Basic

An open economy allows significant trade and capital flows with the rest of the world. High trade-to-GDP ratios, low tariffs, and liberal investment rules mark an open economy. Most modern economies are open to varying degrees.

**Real-life applications:**
- Singapore's economy with trade exceeding 200% of GDP.
- A country with low tariffs and free capital flows.
- Multinationals easily investing and repatriating profits.
- Consumer prices closely linked to global commodity trends.

**Key concepts:** `open economy`, `trade intensity`, `capital mobility`, `liberalization`, `globalization`

---

## Q67. What is a closed economy?

**Level:** Basic

A closed economy has little or no trade or investment with the rest of the world. Extreme examples are rare today, but some countries still restrict trade heavily through tariffs, sanctions, or policy choices.

**Real-life applications:**
- A sanctioned country with restricted imports and exports.
- Heavy autarky policies limiting foreign dependence.
- A country with capital controls blocking most flows.
- Historical examples like inter-war protectionism.

**Key concepts:** `closed economy`, `autarky`, `sanctions`, `capital controls`, `self-sufficiency`

---

## Q68. What is the ILO?

**Level:** Basic

The International Labor Organization sets international labor standards — on forced labor, child labor, discrimination, and freedom of association. It is a UN agency predating the WTO and influences trade policy indirectly through labor chapters in trade deals.

**Real-life applications:**
- ILO core conventions ratified by most countries.
- A trade deal's labor chapter referencing ILO standards.
- An ILO inspection report on forced-labor claims.
- A buyer company auditing suppliers against ILO benchmarks.

**Key concepts:** `ILO`, `labor standards`, `core conventions`, `UN agency`, `worker rights`

---

## Q69. What is a trade war?

**Level:** Basic

A trade war is an escalating cycle of tariffs and trade restrictions between countries. Each side retaliates, damaging trade flows and raising prices. Trade wars often hurt both economies more than either originally intended.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two countries raising tariffs on each other's exports.
- Firms seeing sudden cost spikes from new tariffs.
- Supply chains scrambling to reroute around high-tariff countries.
- Consumers paying higher prices during prolonged trade wars.

**Key concepts:** `trade war`, `retaliation`, `escalation`, `tariff`, `trade conflict`

---

## Q70. What is the difference between tariffs and subsidies?

**Level:** Basic

A tariff is a tax on imports to make them more expensive. A subsidy is a payment or tax break to domestic producers to lower their costs. Both can protect domestic industry but through different mechanisms — one raises import prices, the other lowers domestic costs.

**Real-life applications:**
- A country using a tariff to limit imports.
- A country using subsidies to boost exports of domestic goods.
- An industry lobbying for tariffs versus subsidies depending on impact.
- WTO rules treating subsidies and tariffs differently under separate agreements.

**Key concepts:** `tariff`, `subsidy`, `protection`, `trade remedy`, `domestic support`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Trade Policy Stack
**Purpose:** Separate the layers that shape whether a cross-border sale is actually workable.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Trade rule -> Tariff / quota / NTB -> Customs / documentation -> Agreement / dispute remedy -> Delivered market access
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Border layer - tariff, quota, tariff schedule, HS code, customs duty.
Component 2: Friction layer - licensing, technical regulation, customs delays, trade facilitation.
Component 3: Legal layer - WTO, GATT, MFN, national treatment, DSU.
Component 4: Commercial layer - price, margin, market access, export decision.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company entering Japan may face no physical tariff, but it still has to pass a licensing rule, set up local invoicing, and fit a GATS-style services commitment structure. The decision is not just "can we sell?" but "what frictions sit between the contract and the revenue?"
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a market-entry plan is stalled by a mix of tariff, customs, and legal issues.

### Trade Remedy Ladder
**Purpose:** Identify which policy tool is being used to defend an industry.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Normal trade -> Dumping? -> Anti-dumping duty
Normal trade -> Subsidy? -> Countervailing duty
Sudden surge -> Safeguard
Policy shield -> Tariff / quota / subsidy / local content
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Trigger type - unfair pricing, subsidy, or import surge.
Component 2: Proof burden - injury test, dumping margin, subsidy evidence.
Component 3: Duration - temporary safeguard versus longer remedy.
Component 4: Strategic effect - price increase, volume reduction, or negotiation leverage.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A hardware AI vendor ships a device to Europe and faces an anti-dumping petition from local rivals. The team needs to know whether the issue is dumping, a foreign subsidy, or a general surge because each route changes pricing, evidence collection, and legal response.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When procurement or sales is hit by a new import challenge.

### Trade Structure Ladder
**Purpose:** Show how trade relationships deepen from simple access to deep integration.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Tariff cuts -> FTA -> Customs union -> Common market -> Economic union
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Barrier removal - tariff reduction and market access.
Component 2: Rule alignment - rules of origin, common external tariff, shared regulation.
Component 3: Factor mobility - goods, services, capital, labor.
Component 4: Institutional depth - negotiation, governance, review, dispute settlement.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting network starts with simple tariff-free service delivery, then sets up local commercial presence, then uses a common market style operating model to share staff and contracts across countries. Each step adds more integration and more governance overhead.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When evaluating whether an FTA is enough or a deeper regional structure is required.

## 4. Formulas
Formula: Trade balance = exports - imports

Variables:
Exports = value sold abroad
Imports = value bought from abroad

Why this formula exists: It answers whether a country or business is net selling or net buying across borders.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0: deficit -> financing or cost pressure
Value = 0: balanced -> neutral trade position
Value > 0: surplus -> external earnings and reserve buildup
Worked example with numbers: A consulting group sells $18M in overseas services and buys $12M of cloud and vendor services from abroad. Trade balance = $6M surplus, which supports foreign exchange inflow and expansion.

Formula: Balance of payments pressure = current account + capital account [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Variables:
Current account = trade, income, and transfers
Capital account = cross-border investment flows

Why this formula exists: It summarizes the external financing identity behind trade and capital flows.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0: external financing gap -> reserves or inflows needed
Value = 0: balanced external position -> stable external accounts
Value > 0: surplus -> reserve accumulation or outward investment
Worked example with numbers: An AI firm earns a $10M services surplus but receives $10M in foreign equity inflows. The combined external position is roughly balanced, so cash planning can focus on operations rather than FX stress.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat WTO rules as only about tariffs. | Check MFN, national treatment, NTBs, services, and remedies together. |
| Assume one trade tool explains every border problem. | Separate tariff, quota, subsidy, and safeguard logic. |
| Read a trade agreement without rules of origin. | Verify origin, certification, and preferential tariff conditions. |
| Confuse FDI with short-term portfolio money. | Distinguish subsidiary control and long-term capital from reversible flows. |
| Assume services are outside trade policy. | Check GATS mode of supply, commitments, and market access. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: SaaS market access in an RTA
Situation: A SaaS company wants to expand into a partner country inside an FTA. The tariff is irrelevant, but the firm still faces data-hosting rules, a local license, and a requirement to show service commitments.
Applicable framework/metric: Trade Policy Stack.
Analysis: If market access delay is 90 days and the local office would cost $300k more per year, the firm should compare delay cost against fixed-cost overhead. If the delay cost exceeds $300k, local presence wins.
Decision rule: If annual delay cost is above $300k, build local presence. If it is between $100k and $300k, use a hybrid model. If below $100k, stay cross-border.
Action: Build a country-by-country checklist for GATS commitments, licensing, and customs-style administrative friction.

Scenario 2: AI hardware and trade remedy risk
Situation: An AI hardware vendor exports compute appliances that local rivals claim are dumped. The vendor is also sourcing key parts from a subsidized foreign supplier.
Applicable framework/metric: Trade Remedy Ladder.
Analysis: If the claimed dumping margin is 12% and the subsidy estimate is 8%, the vendor needs two separate response tracks. A 12% margin can justify a price defense, while an 8% subsidy risk can trigger a supplier change.
Decision rule: If exposure is above 10% on either line, legal review is mandatory. If exposure is 5% to 10%, tighten documentation. If below 5%, monitor quarterly.
Action: Segregate pricing files, supplier subsidies, and customs paperwork before the complaint matures.

Scenario 3: Consulting delivery in a deep integration region
Situation: A consulting firm serving multiple countries is deciding whether to keep each office separate or move to a more integrated regional model.
Applicable framework/metric: Trade Structure Ladder.
Analysis: If cross-border staff sharing would reduce delivery cost by 18% and the common-market-style coordination overhead is 6%, the net gain is 12%. That supports deeper integration.
Decision rule: If net savings exceed 10%, integrate the regional operating model. If savings are 3% to 10%, integrate selectively. If below 3%, keep offices mostly separate.
Action: Standardize contracts, billing, and compliance rules before expanding shared delivery across the region.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Map every target market to its tariff, NTB, and services-access profile.
2. Tag each product or service as goods trade, services trade, or a hybrid offer.
3. Classify your exposure to MFN, national treatment, and rules-of-origin risk.
4. Build a trade-remedy register for dumping, subsidies, and safeguard triggers.
5. Track current account, capital inflows, and reserve impact for priority countries.
6. Separate FDI, portfolio flow, and loan assumptions in expansion plans.
7. Refresh market-access assumptions whenever a WTO round, RTA, or policy review changes.
