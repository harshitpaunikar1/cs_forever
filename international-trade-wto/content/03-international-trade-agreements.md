# International Trade Agreements

## Overview

Trade agreements are contracts between countries that set rules for how goods, services, and investments move across their borders. They cut tariffs, harmonize standards, and commit governments to not surprise each other with new trade barriers. Some agreements are global (WTO), many are regional (EU, USMCA), and others are bilateral between just two countries.

---

## Why It Matters

Agreements decide your tariff bill, your paperwork, and which markets you can actually access. A single free-trade deal can cut a 10% tariff to zero overnight and open a country that was previously too expensive to sell into. Managers who read the fine print of agreements catch opportunities and avoid costly surprises.

## Key Principles

- Agreements reduce trade barriers in exchange for reciprocal concessions.
- "Rules of origin" decide whether a product actually qualifies for the low tariff rate.
- Most agreements include dispute mechanisms, not just tariff schedules.
- Deeper agreements cover services, investment, IP, and labor, not just goods.
- Agreements do not override national security, health, or safety rules.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **FTA (Free Trade Agreement)** | A deal removing tariffs between member countries. |
| **Customs Union** | FTA plus a shared external tariff with non-members. |
| **Rules of Origin** | Criteria that decide which country a product "comes from" for tariff purposes. |
| **Bilateral vs Multilateral** | Two parties vs many parties to the agreement. |

## Use Case

An electronics exporter uses rules of origin in a regional agreement to show that at least 60% of a laptop's value is added inside the member bloc, unlocking a 0% tariff into partner countries.

## Scenario

> A Canadian furniture maker exported to the US under USMCA. By re-sourcing its wood from a member country instead of a non-member supplier, it passed the rules-of-origin test, avoided a 6% tariff, and won a large retail contract on price.

## Examples

- A UK food exporter shifting paperwork to comply with EU–UK trade terms after Brexit.
- An Indian IT firm using a bilateral services agreement to open a subsidiary in a partner country.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Trade agreements are country-level rules that change the economics of cross-border selling, sourcing, and investment.
For IT, AI, product, and consulting leaders, they matter because they can change tariffs, qualification rules, paperwork, and market access overnight.
The decision they support is simple: should you enter, source, or price a market assuming the agreement will lower friction, or assuming it will not?

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Trade Agreement Type Matrix
**Purpose:** Compare the practical reach of an agreement before committing to a market-entry or sourcing plan.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    Scope of Coverage
                 Goods only -> Goods + services + investment + IP + labor
Structure
Global / multilateral      Broad rules, many members
Regional bloc              Regional access, deeper rules
Bilateral                  Narrower membership, faster targeting
Customs union               FTA + shared external tariff
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Structure: whether the deal is global, regional, bilateral, or a customs union.
Scope of Coverage: whether the deal covers only goods or also services, investment, IP, and labor.
For an IT services firm, the useful question is not only “is there an FTA?” but “does the agreement also support services delivery and IP protection?”
When to pull this out in a meeting: When deciding whether a country deserves a go/no-go expansion recommendation.

### Rules-of-Origin Gate
**Purpose:** Decide whether a shipment qualifies for the preferential tariff rate.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Source inputs -> local value added? -> origin threshold met? -> preferential tariff?
                 yes / no               yes / no              yes / no
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Input source mix: whether components or work come from member or non-member countries.
Local value added: whether enough value is added inside the member bloc.
Qualification result: whether the product gets the low tariff rate.
For a product team shipping hardware with embedded AI chips, the gate determines whether the landed cost model is real or fantasy.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When procurement wants to switch suppliers or a new market entry depends on tariff treatment.

Variables:
Import value = declared customs value of the shipment
Tariff rate = percentage duty in the schedule
Why this formula exists: It translates agreement terms into landed-cost impact.
How to interpret the output:
Value = 0 → tariff-free entry → prioritize growth and pricing agility
Value low → manageable duty → test margin sensitivity
Value high → expensive market access → reconsider sourcing or pricing
Worked example with numbers: If a SaaS appliance bundle is declared at $100,000 and faces a 6% tariff, the tariff cost is $6,000. If a trade agreement lowers that to 0%, the product can be priced more aggressively in the partner market.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Assume every market has the same tariff treatment | Check whether the country pair is covered by an agreement |
| Assume a low tariff applies automatically | Verify rules of origin before quoting price |
| Treat services like goods | Confirm whether the agreement covers services, investment, and IP |
| Ignore dispute mechanisms | Use agreements with enforcement paths when the deal is material |
| Plan expansion without exception handling | Factor in national security, health, and safety carve-outs |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: North America launch for a product-led company
Situation: A hardware startup with an AI-enabled device wants to sell into Canada and the US. The team sees a lower tariff under a regional agreement, but only if origin rules are met.
Applicable framework/metric: Rules-of-origin gate; landed cost.
Analysis: If the product clears origin tests, a 6% tariff on a $50,000 shipment falls to $0. That is a $3,000 margin improvement per shipment.
Decision rule: If tariff savings are greater than the compliance cost, use the agreement. If savings are small, simplify the route. If origin cannot be proven, price as if the duty applies.
Action: Build an origin checklist with procurement, finance, and logistics before launch.

Scenario 2: Consulting team serving a regional client base
Situation: A consulting firm wants to staff work from one country and deliver to another through a services agreement. The commercial question is whether the deal supports cross-border services at all.
Applicable framework/metric: Agreement type matrix; services coverage.
Analysis: If the agreement covers services and reduces barriers, the team can treat delivery as a regional operating model instead of a country-by-country workaround.
Decision rule: If services are covered, centralize delivery. If coverage is partial, localize key work. If no services coverage exists, use a local partner model.
Action: Map the agreement’s services clauses before assigning delivery teams.

Scenario 3: AI vendor managing regulated market entry
Situation: An AI product company is entering a market where safety, health, and national security reviews may still apply even if the tariff is low.
Applicable framework/metric: Exception screening; regulatory clearance rate.
Analysis: A zero tariff does not help if the product fails safety review. The real metric is whether the product clears all required reviews on time.
Decision rule: If regulatory clearance is on schedule, proceed. If clearance is uncertain, delay launch. If an exception is likely to be triggered, redesign the launch plan.
Action: Add legal and compliance checkpoints to the release timeline.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build a market-entry matrix that lists tariff rate, rules of origin, services coverage, and exception risks for each target country.
2. Add an origin-qualification checklist to procurement so supplier changes do not break preferential treatment.
3. Insert customs and trade-review gates into pricing approvals for hardware and bundled services.
4. Separate goods, services, investment, and IP assumptions in expansion models instead of treating them as one bucket.
5. Create a negotiation brief for legal and government affairs that tracks reciprocal concessions and dispute mechanisms.
6. Stress-test the go-to-market plan against national security, health, and safety exceptions.
7. Update the sales playbook so account teams know when to promise tariff savings and when to avoid them.
