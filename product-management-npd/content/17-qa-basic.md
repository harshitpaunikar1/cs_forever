# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and concepts in product management and new product development (Q1–Q70).

## Q1. What is product management?

**Level:** Basic

Product management is the discipline of deciding what to build, why, and for whom, and guiding a product from idea to launch and beyond. A product manager owns outcomes, not tasks, and works across engineering, design, marketing, and leadership. The focus is on solving real customer problems while meeting business goals.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting quarterly roadmaps for a SaaS product
- Running customer interviews to uncover unmet needs
- Prioritizing bug fixes versus new features
- Aligning engineering and marketing on launch dates
- Deciding when to kill a feature that isn't working

**Key concepts:** `product-management`, `roadmap`, `outcomes`, `customer-problems`, `prioritization`

---

## Q2. Who is a product manager?

**Level:** Basic

A product manager (PM) is the person accountable for a product's vision, strategy, and results. They lead through influence rather than authority because they often don't manage the people doing the work. A PM translates customer problems and business goals into clear plans the team can execute.

**Real-life applications:**
- Leading a cross-functional scrum team
- Representing the customer in internal meetings
- Writing product requirement documents
- Making go/no-go launch decisions
- Reporting product performance to executives

**Real-life applications:**
- Organizing launch priorities at a fintech startup
- Negotiating scope with engineering leads
- Presenting roadmaps to the CEO
- Coordinating marketing and product timelines
- Analyzing user feedback to pivot strategy

**Key concepts:** `PM-role`, `influence-leadership`, `accountability`, `strategy`, `cross-functional`

---

## Q3. What is the difference between a product manager and a project manager?

**Level:** Basic

A product manager owns the "what" and "why" — what to build and why it matters to customers. A project manager owns the "how" and "when" — how tasks get executed and by when. Product managers focus on outcomes and strategy; project managers focus on schedules and delivery.

**Real-life applications:**
- Splitting PM and PjM roles in a large tech company
- Using project managers on hardware launches with complex timelines
- Product managers setting priorities, project managers driving Gantt charts
- Hiring the right role based on company maturity
- Clarifying responsibilities to avoid duplication

**Key concepts:** `PM-vs-PjM`, `role-clarity`, `outcomes-vs-output`, `delivery`, `strategy`

---

## Q4. What is a product roadmap?

**Level:** Basic

A product roadmap is a visual, time-phased plan showing what a team intends to build and why. It communicates priorities to stakeholders and helps the team stay aligned. Good roadmaps focus on themes and outcomes, not rigid feature lists.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sharing quarterly priorities with leadership
- Communicating direction to sales and support
- Anchoring sprint planning to larger goals
- Adjusting plans as markets change
- Guiding hiring and budget decisions

**Key concepts:** `roadmap`, `themes`, `communication`, `prioritization`, `time-horizon`

---

## Q5. What is new product development (NPD)?

**Level:** Basic

New product development is the full process of bringing a new product from concept to market. It usually moves through stages: ideation, concept, design, prototyping, testing, launch, and post-launch. NPD applies to physical goods, services, and software.

**Real-life applications:**
- Launching a new smartphone model
- Introducing a new banking product
- Adding a new service line at a restaurant chain
- Bringing a medical device to market
- Releasing a new feature-rich software product

**Key concepts:** `NPD`, `stages`, `product-lifecycle`, `launch`, `innovation`

---

## Q6. What does "customer-centric" mean in product management?

**Level:** Basic

Customer-centric means putting the real needs, goals, and pains of customers at the center of every decision. It is the opposite of building what executives assume or what engineers find technically interesting. Customer-centric teams talk to users often and measure success by customer outcomes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hosting monthly customer advisory boards
- Shadowing users in their environment
- Making NPS a team-wide KPI
- Rewriting backlog stories in customer language
- Including customer quotes in roadmap reviews

**Key concepts:** `customer-centric`, `user-research`, `NPS`, `empathy`, `feedback-loops`

---

## Q7. What is a market opportunity?

**Level:** Basic

A market opportunity is a gap in the market where customer demand is unmet or poorly served. Spotting one means identifying a group of buyers with a real problem and enough willingness to pay for a solution. A real opportunity has size, feasibility, and strategic fit.

**Real-life applications:**
- Finding underserved rural customers for a banking app
- Identifying a premium segment in a mass-market category
- Tapping into a new regulatory change
- Filling a technology gap in enterprise software
- Serving emerging demographics like Gen Z

**Key concepts:** `market-opportunity`, `demand`, `segmentation`, `fit`, `willingness-to-pay`

---

## Q8. What is a white space in the market?

**Level:** Basic

A white space is an unmet or underserved market need where no dominant product exists yet. Finding white space lets a company enter with less competition and build strong brand equity. White spaces can come from new technology, changing behavior, or ignored customer segments.

**Real-life applications:**
- Identifying a lack of affordable baby-care products in tier-two cities
- Spotting missing language options in a translation app
- Finding a gap in premium veterinary services
- Creating a new category like "healthy fast food"
- Launching tools for freelancers before incumbents noticed

**Key concepts:** `white-space`, `underserved-segments`, `category-creation`, `market-gap`, `first-mover`

---

## Q9. What is market analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Market analysis is the structured study of customers, competitors, and trends to understand the market landscape. It informs decisions about where to play, what to build, and how to position. Typical inputs include interviews, surveys, industry reports, and public data.

**Real-life applications:**
- Evaluating a new country to enter
- Deciding whether to launch a premium or budget line
- Comparing competitor feature sets
- Forecasting demand for a new category
- Setting pricing and positioning

**Key concepts:** `market-analysis`, `research`, `competitive-landscape`, `trends`, `positioning`

---

## Q10. What are the main sources of product ideas?

**Level:** Basic

Product ideas come from customers, employees, competitors, technology shifts, and adjacent industries. Customer complaints and usage patterns are the richest source. Other valuable sources include support tickets, sales conversations, and market trends reports.

**Real-life applications:**
- Mining support tickets for feature ideas
- Running a quarterly employee suggestion program
- Attending industry conferences for trend scouting
- Reverse-engineering competitor launches
- Watching adjacent categories for inspiration

**Key concepts:** `idea-sources`, `customer-insight`, `trends`, `competitive-scan`, `employee-ideas`

---

## Q11. What is ideation?

**Level:** Basic

Ideation is the structured process of generating many product ideas, then narrowing to the strongest. It separates divergent thinking (create many options) from convergent thinking (pick the best). Good ideation is not random brainstorming but uses techniques to push past obvious ideas.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running a design sprint's "crazy 8s" exercise
- Holding cross-functional hackathons
- Using customer journey maps to spark ideas
- Facilitating "how might we" workshops
- Applying SCAMPER to existing products

**Key concepts:** `ideation`, `divergent-convergent`, `brainstorming`, `creativity`, `workshops`

---

## Q12. What is brainstorming?

**Level:** Basic

Brainstorming is a group method for generating many ideas quickly without early judgment. The aim is quantity first, quality later. It works best with diverse participants, a clear prompt, and a rule that bans criticism during the idea-generation phase.

**Real-life applications:**
- Generating marketing campaign ideas
- Listing solutions to customer pain points
- Naming a new product line
- Finding ways to reduce churn
- Opening a product strategy off-site

**Key concepts:** `brainstorming`, `divergent-thinking`, `group-ideation`, `creativity`, `facilitation`

---

## Q13. What is SCAMPER?

**Level:** Basic

SCAMPER is an ideation technique: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse. Applying each lens to an existing product surfaces new possibilities. It works well to refresh mature products.

**Real-life applications:**
- Redesigning a kitchen appliance
- Refreshing a packaged-food brand
- Modifying a software product's user flow
- Combining two features into one premium offering
- Eliminating steps in a checkout flow

**Key concepts:** `SCAMPER`, `ideation-technique`, `innovation`, `creative-prompts`, `product-refresh`

---

## Q14. What is a customer journey map?

**Level:** Basic

A customer journey map is a visual timeline of a customer's full experience with a product, from awareness through usage and loyalty. It highlights touchpoints, emotions, and pain points. Journey maps help teams spot where they can improve or innovate.

**Real-life applications:**
- Finding drop-off points in onboarding
- Redesigning a hotel guest experience
- Mapping a patient's hospital visit
- Improving an e-commerce return experience
- Launching loyalty programs around pain moments

**Key concepts:** `customer-journey`, `touchpoints`, `pain-points`, `experience-design`, `mapping`

---

## Q15. What is a jobs-to-be-done (JTBD) framework?

**Level:** Basic

Jobs-to-be-done says customers "hire" a product to do a job in their life. The framework shifts focus from product features to the underlying customer goal. Understanding the job reveals what really drives buying and switching.

**Real-life applications:**
- Realizing milkshakes are "hired" for commuter breakfast
- Designing tools around student "pass the exam" jobs
- Positioning SaaS around "look good in Monday's meeting"
- Finding new markets by matching jobs across industries
- Creating bundles that fulfill a broader job

**Key concepts:** `JTBD`, `customer-goal`, `functional-job`, `emotional-job`, `innovation-framework`

---

## Q16. What are the typical stages of product development?

**Level:** Basic

Typical stages are idea generation, idea screening, concept development, business analysis, product development, market testing, and commercialization. Each stage has clear deliverables and a decision gate. Weak ideas are killed early so resources go to stronger ones.

**Real-life applications:**
- Using stage-gate processes in consumer electronics
- Running pilot tests before a national launch
- Conducting business case reviews in pharma
- Killing low-scoring ideas at idea screening
- Setting exit criteria per stage

**Key concepts:** `NPD-stages`, `stage-gate`, `idea-screening`, `concept`, `commercialization`

---

## Q17. What is a stage-gate process?

**Level:** Basic

A stage-gate process is a structured NPD method where each development stage ends with a review (the gate) that decides go, hold, or kill. It controls risk by releasing funds only as ideas prove themselves. Used heavily in consumer goods and pharma.

**Real-life applications:**
- Approving R&D budgets in phases
- Killing weak ideas at the business-case gate
- Aligning leadership on investment progression
- Ensuring cross-functional review at each gate
- Protecting against runaway costs

**Key concepts:** `stage-gate`, `go-kill-decision`, `risk-management`, `phased-funding`, `governance`

---

## Q18. What is a minimum viable product (MVP)?

**Level:** Basic

An MVP is the smallest version of a product that delivers real value and allows learning from real users. It is not a broken product — it is a focused one. The goal is to validate the riskiest assumptions with minimal investment.

**Real-life applications:**
- Shipping a landing page to test demand
- Launching a single-feature app to validate a market
- Running a pilot service in one city
- Testing pricing with a simplified plan
- Gathering real user feedback before scaling

**Real-life applications:**
- Airbnb's first website with only air mattresses
- Dropbox's demo video MVP
- A SaaS MVP with manual backend processes
- A restaurant trialing a single dish as a pop-up
- A fintech MVP that only handles one transaction type

**Key concepts:** `MVP`, `validation`, `lean-startup`, `assumption-testing`, `minimal-scope`

---

## Q19. What is a product concept?

**Level:** Basic

A product concept is a clear written description of a proposed product — what it does, for whom, and why it matters. Concepts help teams align and allow early testing with customers. They usually appear right after ideation and before detailed design.

**Real-life applications:**
- Presenting a concept to a steering committee
- Running a concept test with a focus group
- Pitching internally for project funding
- Sharing a one-page concept with design partners
- Comparing three concepts to pick one

**Key concepts:** `product-concept`, `concept-statement`, `early-testing`, `description`, `alignment`

---

## Q20. What is concept testing?

**Level:** Basic

Concept testing is showing a proposed product idea to potential customers and measuring their interest, understanding, and willingness to pay. It catches weak ideas before engineering spends money. Methods include surveys, interviews, and landing page tests.

**Real-life applications:**
- Surveying 200 users on three concept descriptions
- Showing storyboards to buyers
- Running fake-door tests on a product page
- A/B testing pricing on a concept
- Focus groups evaluating concept variations

**Key concepts:** `concept-testing`, `validation`, `early-feedback`, `willingness-to-pay`, `research`

---

## Q21. What is market segmentation?

**Level:** Basic

Market segmentation is the practice of dividing a market into groups of buyers with similar needs, behaviors, or demographics. It lets teams tailor products and messages to each group. Common bases are geography, demographics, behavior, and psychographics.

**Real-life applications:**
- Targeting millennials vs. boomers with different messaging
- Splitting B2B buyers by company size
- Grouping users by usage frequency
- Offering tiered plans for different segments
- Customizing regional product variants

**Key concepts:** `segmentation`, `demographics`, `behavior`, `psychographics`, `targeting`

---

## Q22. What is a target market?

**Level:** Basic

A target market is the specific group of customers a product aims to serve. Clear targeting sharpens positioning, marketing, and product decisions. Trying to serve everyone usually means serving no one well.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tesla initially targeting luxury early adopters
- A health app focusing on diabetic patients
- A SaaS tool built for solo freelancers
- A food brand targeting gym-goers
- An education app aimed at JEE aspirants

**Key concepts:** `target-market`, `positioning`, `focus`, `niche`, `ideal-customer`

---

## Q23. What is a persona?

**Level:** Basic

A persona is a semi-fictional profile of an ideal customer, based on real data. Personas include goals, pains, behaviors, and context, and help teams make user-centered decisions. Good personas are few in number and grounded in research.

**Real-life applications:**
- Designing features for a "Busy Bookkeeper" persona
- Writing marketing copy tailored to a persona
- Prioritizing backlog items by persona impact
- Training sales teams on buyer personas
- Running usability tests with persona-matched users

**Key concepts:** `persona`, `user-profile`, `empathy`, `design-target`, `research-based`

---

## Q24. What is TAM, SAM, and SOM?

**Level:** Basic

TAM (Total Addressable Market) is the full revenue if you had 100% share. SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market) is the portion your product can realistically reach. SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market) is the share you can actually capture in the near term.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pitching investors with a credible TAM
- Planning three-year revenue targets with SOM
- Sizing a new geographic market
- Validating whether an opportunity is big enough
- Comparing market sizes of two product ideas

**Key concepts:** `TAM`, `SAM`, `SOM`, `market-sizing`, `investor-pitch`

---

## Q25. What is value proposition?

**Level:** Basic

A value proposition is a clear statement of the unique value a product delivers to a specific customer and why it is better than alternatives. It answers: "Why should I buy this, from you, now?" Strong value props are specific, believable, and customer-centered.

**Real-life applications:**
- Writing a one-line value prop on a landing page
- Aligning sales pitches to the value prop
- Distinguishing from competitors in ads
- Validating value props with customer interviews
- Adjusting value props as markets evolve

**Key concepts:** `value-proposition`, `differentiation`, `positioning`, `customer-benefit`, `messaging`

---

## Q26. What is positioning?

**Level:** Basic

Positioning is how a product is perceived in the minds of customers, relative to alternatives. It is defined by target, category, and key benefits. Good positioning is distinct, credible, and meaningful to the target customer.

**Real-life applications:**
- Positioning Volvo as "the safest car"
- Repositioning a mature brand for younger buyers
- Creating clear category leadership
- Avoiding "me too" positioning
- Using positioning to guide product development

**Key concepts:** `positioning`, `perception`, `differentiation`, `category`, `brand`

---

## Q27. What is a product life cycle?

**Level:** Basic

A product life cycle is the sequence of stages a product goes through: introduction, growth, maturity, and decline. Each stage calls for different strategies. Recognizing the stage helps a team invest wisely.

**Real-life applications:**
- Cutting marketing spend on a declining SKU
- Investing heavily during the growth stage
- Repositioning at maturity to extend life
- Launching limited editions to refresh interest
- Retiring products cleanly during decline

**Key concepts:** `lifecycle`, `introduction`, `growth`, `maturity`, `decline`

---

## Q28. What is the introduction stage of the lifecycle?

**Level:** Basic

The introduction stage is when a new product is launched into the market. Sales are low, marketing spend is high, and the goal is awareness and early adoption. Margins are typically thin as the product is unproven.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running teaser campaigns before launch
- Offering early-adopter discounts
- Running PR pushes on launch day
- Collecting feedback from first customers
- Iterating quickly based on launch data

**Key concepts:** `introduction-stage`, `launch`, `early-adopters`, `awareness`, `first-sales`

---

## Q29. What is the growth stage?

**Level:** Basic

The growth stage is when a product's sales rise rapidly as the market accepts it. Teams scale production, expand distribution, and invest in features that widen appeal. Competition usually intensifies in this stage.

**Real-life applications:**
- Scaling manufacturing capacity
- Expanding to new geographic markets
- Adding channel partners
- Hiring more sales staff
- Defending share from new entrants

**Key concepts:** `growth-stage`, `scaling`, `share-gain`, `expansion`, `competition`

---

## Q30. What is the maturity stage?

**Level:** Basic

The maturity stage is when sales growth slows because most target customers have bought. The focus shifts to defending share, extracting margin, and extending lifecycle. Competition is tough and differentiation matters more.

**Real-life applications:**
- Releasing limited editions of a beverage
- Cutting costs in a mature SKU
- Expanding to new use cases
- Bundling with other products
- Targeting emerging-market entry

**Key concepts:** `maturity`, `share-defense`, `margin`, `lifecycle-extension`, `differentiation`

---

## Q31. What is the decline stage?

**Level:** Basic

The decline stage is when sales fall because customer needs or technology have changed. Teams usually harvest profits, reduce investment, or retire the product. Holding on too long wastes resources.

**Real-life applications:**
- Ending production of a legacy phone
- Reducing SKUs of a declining food line
- Sunsetting a software feature
- Shifting customers to a successor product
- Closing regional stores with declining sales

**Key concepts:** `decline`, `harvest`, `sunsetting`, `portfolio-pruning`, `successor-product`

---

## Q32. What is product pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Product pricing is the decision of how much to charge for a product. It considers costs, customer value, competitor pricing, and strategic goals. Pricing drives both revenue and positioning.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting launch price for a new smartphone
- Adjusting SaaS plans annually
- Running promotional discounts for holidays
- Using price tiers for different segments
- Raising prices after a value upgrade

**Key concepts:** `pricing`, `cost-based`, `value-based`, `competitive-pricing`, `revenue`

---

## Q33. What is cost-plus pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Cost-plus pricing sets price as the product's cost plus a fixed markup. It is simple and protects margin but ignores customer willingness-to-pay and competitor moves. Common in manufacturing and retail.

**Real-life applications:**
- Apparel retailers applying a standard markup
- Government-regulated utility pricing
- Contract manufacturing charging cost-plus
- Construction project bids
- Industrial components with stable demand

**Key concepts:** `cost-plus`, `markup`, `margin`, `simplicity`, `limitations`

---

## Q34. What is value-based pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Value-based pricing sets the price based on the value the product delivers to the customer, not the cost to produce. It captures more margin where customers see high value. Requires strong research on customer willingness-to-pay.

**Real-life applications:**
- Enterprise SaaS charging per seat based on ROI
- Luxury brands pricing on brand value
- Medical devices priced on patient outcomes
- Legal services priced by case value
- Consulting firms priced on impact, not hours

**Key concepts:** `value-based-pricing`, `willingness-to-pay`, `customer-value`, `margin`, `differentiation`

---

## Q35. What is penetration pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Penetration pricing uses a low launch price to attract many customers quickly and build share. The bet is that early scale and network effects lock in long-term advantage. Risk is low margin during the early period.

**Real-life applications:**
- Jio entering Indian telecom with free data
- Netflix pricing below local incumbents in new markets
- A startup SaaS offering half the price of a leader
- A new FMCG brand at 20% below category average
- Streaming apps competing on low intro rates

**Key concepts:** `penetration-pricing`, `share-gain`, `low-margin`, `network-effects`, `market-entry`

---

## Q36. What is price skimming?

**Level:** Basic

Price skimming sets a high launch price to capture early adopters who will pay more, then lowers price over time as the market broadens. Works best for novel products with little direct competition. Apple and luxury tech brands use it often.

**Real-life applications:**
- iPhone launch pricing then discounts
- Premium gaming console launches
- Medical innovations with patent protection
- Early electric vehicle launches
- Premium headphone releases

**Key concepts:** `skimming`, `early-adopters`, `premium-pricing`, `price-reduction`, `lifecycle`

---

## Q37. What is freemium pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Freemium offers a free base product with paid upgrades for premium features or higher usage. It lowers the barrier to try and relies on a share of users converting to paid. Common in SaaS and consumer apps.

**Real-life applications:**
- Spotify's free tier with ads and paid premium
- Dropbox offering free storage with paid plans
- LinkedIn's free profile and paid Premium
- Freemium gaming with in-app purchases
- Note-taking apps with paid sync features

**Key concepts:** `freemium`, `free-to-paid`, `conversion-rate`, `low-barrier`, `viral-growth`

---

## Q38. What is customer lifetime value (CLV)?

**Level:** Basic

Customer lifetime value is the total profit a business expects to earn from a customer across the whole relationship. It guides decisions on how much to spend acquiring and retaining customers. Higher CLV means more room for acquisition spend.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting marketing budgets based on CLV
- Prioritizing retention programs
- Deciding which segments to target
- Pricing referral incentives
- Evaluating product changes that lift CLV

**Key concepts:** `CLV`, `lifetime-value`, `retention`, `acquisition-budget`, `profitability`

---

## Q39. What is customer acquisition cost (CAC)?

**Level:** Basic

CAC is the cost to acquire one new paying customer, including marketing and sales spend. Comparing CAC to CLV reveals whether growth is profitable. A common healthy ratio is CLV:CAC of 3:1 or higher.

**Real-life applications:**
- Evaluating Facebook ad efficiency
- Benchmarking SaaS unit economics
- Comparing channel costs
- Deciding when to pause spending
- Planning sales team investments

**Key concepts:** `CAC`, `unit-economics`, `channel-cost`, `payback-period`, `CLV-CAC-ratio`

---

## Q40. What is a go-to-market (GTM) strategy?

**Level:** Basic

A go-to-market strategy is the plan for how a product reaches customers — through which channels, at what price, with what message. It covers target audience, positioning, pricing, channels, and launch tactics. A strong GTM boosts chances of launch success.

**Real-life applications:**
- Launching a new SaaS tool through content marketing
- Rolling out a physical product via retail + e-commerce
- Piloting a B2B product with a single vertical first
- Partnering with influencers on D2C launches
- Running a pre-sale campaign before shipping

**Key concepts:** `GTM`, `launch-plan`, `channels`, `messaging`, `positioning`

---

## Q41. What is a launch plan?

**Level:** Basic

A launch plan is a coordinated document listing all the tasks, owners, and dates required to bring a product to market. It covers engineering readiness, marketing, support, legal, and operations. The goal is to avoid missed items on launch day.

**Real-life applications:**
- Coordinating a feature launch across marketing and product
- Scheduling PR activities around launch
- Aligning engineering cut-off dates with marketing pushes
- Running internal training before launch
- Preparing support teams for expected questions

**Real-life applications:**
- Rolling out a smartphone in three countries
- Launching a SaaS product's new pricing plan
- Releasing a new clothing line on e-commerce
- Introducing a restaurant's new menu
- Launching an ed-tech course before exam season

**Key concepts:** `launch-plan`, `coordination`, `timeline`, `cross-functional`, `readiness`

---

## Q42. What is product-market fit?

**Level:** Basic

Product-market fit is the moment when a product clearly satisfies strong demand from a well-defined market. Signs include organic growth, high retention, and customers recommending it unprompted. Before PMF, growth is forced; after PMF, it is pulled by customers.

**Real-life applications:**
- Slack's early growth at Stewart Butterfield's company
- Airbnb reaching strong repeat usage
- A SaaS startup with week-over-week 20% growth
- Customers asking for more despite rough UX
- Strong word-of-mouth in a niche segment

**Key concepts:** `PMF`, `retention`, `organic-growth`, `demand-pull`, `startup-milestone`

---

## Q43. What is agile methodology?

**Level:** Basic

Agile is a way of building products in short cycles, with frequent feedback and adjustment. It favors working software over heavy documentation, customer collaboration over contracts, and responding to change over following a plan. Common frameworks include Scrum and Kanban.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running 2-week sprints in a software team
- Daily standups for visibility
- Continuous delivery pipelines
- Retrospectives to improve team practices
- Adjusting priorities based on customer feedback

**Key concepts:** `agile`, `iteration`, `sprint`, `scrum`, `kanban`

---

## Q44. What is Scrum?

**Level:** Basic

Scrum is an agile framework with fixed-length sprints (usually 2 weeks), daily standups, sprint reviews, and retrospectives. It uses roles: product owner, scrum master, and team. Widely used for software development.

**Real-life applications:**
- A mobile app team running 2-week sprints
- Product owners grooming backlogs
- Scrum masters facilitating standups
- Sprint reviews showing demos to stakeholders
- Retrospectives improving team process

**Key concepts:** `scrum`, `sprint`, `standup`, `product-owner`, `scrum-master`

---

## Q45. What is Kanban?

**Level:** Basic

Kanban is a flow-based agile method that visualizes work on a board with columns like To Do, In Progress, and Done. It limits work-in-progress to reduce bottlenecks. Works well for support and operations teams where priorities shift often.

**Real-life applications:**
- A support team managing tickets on a Kanban board
- DevOps teams tracking infrastructure tasks
- Hardware teams managing assembly flow
- Marketing teams handling campaign tasks
- Editorial teams tracking article pipelines

**Key concepts:** `kanban`, `wip-limit`, `flow`, `visualization`, `continuous-delivery`

---

## Q46. What is a sprint?

**Level:** Basic

A sprint is a time-boxed period (usually 1-4 weeks) in Scrum during which a team builds a small, shippable piece of product. Each sprint has a goal, a backlog, and a review at the end. Sprints enforce focus and regular delivery.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two-week sprints on a SaaS product
- One-week sprints for a fast-moving startup
- Four-week sprints for large enterprise teams
- Sprint kick-offs aligning teams
- Demoing results in sprint reviews

**Key concepts:** `sprint`, `time-box`, `sprint-goal`, `backlog`, `delivery-cadence`

---

## Q47. What is a user story?

**Level:** Basic

A user story is a short description of a feature from the user's point of view, usually written as "As a [user], I want [action], so that [benefit]." Stories keep teams focused on user value. They are often broken into tasks during sprint planning.

**Real-life applications:**
- Writing a signup flow user story
- Breaking a large feature into smaller stories
- Prioritizing stories by business value
- Writing acceptance criteria
- Using stories to drive design conversations

**Key concepts:** `user-story`, `requirement`, `acceptance-criteria`, `backlog`, `user-value`

---

## Q48. What is a product backlog?

**Level:** Basic

A product backlog is an ordered list of everything a team might work on — features, fixes, enhancements. The product manager owns its prioritization. The top of the backlog is the most important and best-defined work.

**Real-life applications:**
- Grooming a SaaS backlog weekly
- Prioritizing by customer value and effort
- Breaking large items into smaller stories
- Pulling top items into sprint planning
- Archiving stale items

**Key concepts:** `backlog`, `prioritization`, `grooming`, `sprint-planning`, `product-owner`

---

## Q49. What is prioritization?

**Level:** Basic

Prioritization is choosing which problems or features to solve first, using criteria like customer impact, business value, and effort. Frameworks include RICE, MoSCoW, and value-vs-effort matrices. Good prioritization prevents the team from spreading too thin.

**Real-life applications:**
- Ranking quarterly roadmap items
- Choosing between two competing features
- Deciding which bugs to fix first
- Splitting maintenance vs. new features
- Balancing tech debt with feature work

**Key concepts:** `prioritization`, `RICE`, `MoSCoW`, `effort-impact`, `trade-offs`

---

## Q50. What is RICE scoring?

**Level:** Basic

RICE is a prioritization framework: Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort. Each option scores on these factors, and the final score = (Reach x Impact x Confidence) / Effort. It makes prioritization discussions more objective.

**Real-life applications:**
- Scoring backlog items at a SaaS company
- Comparing two feature bets at a B2B product
- Prioritizing marketing experiments
- Ranking growth-team tests
- Aligning teams on what to build next

**Key concepts:** `RICE`, `scoring`, `reach`, `impact`, `effort`

---

## Q51. What is MoSCoW prioritization?

**Level:** Basic

MoSCoW is a method that sorts requirements into Must-have, Should-have, Could-have, and Won't-have (this time). It is simple and widely used in scoping projects. Helps teams cut scope without losing essential features.

**Real-life applications:**
- Scoping a launch's must-haves
- Planning a new release
- Running a requirements workshop
- Aligning stakeholders on MVPs
- Trimming scope to meet a deadline

**Key concepts:** `MoSCoW`, `must-have`, `should-have`, `scope`, `requirements`

---

## Q52. What is design for manufacture (DFM)?

**Level:** Basic

Design for manufacture is the practice of designing products so they are easy and cheap to produce. It reduces parts, uses standard components, and simplifies assembly. Early DFM decisions lock in most of a product's final cost.

**Real-life applications:**
- Reducing screw count in a laptop
- Using injection-molded plastic parts
- Designing for automated assembly
- Sharing parts across product variants
- Standardizing tooling

**Key concepts:** `DFM`, `cost-reduction`, `manufacturability`, `parts-reduction`, `early-design`

---

## Q53. What is design for assembly (DFA)?

**Level:** Basic

Design for assembly aims to make final assembly simpler, faster, and less error-prone. Techniques include self-locating parts, fewer fasteners, and standardized components. DFA often overlaps with DFM but focuses on the human or robotic assembly step.

**Real-life applications:**
- Designing snap-fit parts for speed
- Color-coding components to avoid errors
- Reducing screws in a consumer product
- Ordering parts for stack-assembly
- Training assembly workers on fewer operations

**Key concepts:** `DFA`, `assembly`, `ease-of-build`, `error-reduction`, `manufacturing`

---

## Q54. What is a bill of materials (BOM)?

**Level:** Basic

A bill of materials is the full list of parts and quantities needed to build one unit of a product, along with their costs. It drives purchasing, manufacturing, and cost analysis. Reducing BOM cost is a key lever for margin.

**Real-life applications:**
- Procurement ordering parts using BOM
- Cost engineers reducing BOM total
- Design reviews checking BOM complexity
- Finance forecasting unit costs
- Manufacturing scheduling based on BOM

**Key concepts:** `BOM`, `parts-list`, `unit-cost`, `procurement`, `cost-engineering`

---

## Q55. What is prototyping?

**Level:** Basic

Prototyping is building a rough version of a product to test ideas, learn, and get feedback early. Prototypes range from paper sketches to working hardware or clickable UI. The goal is to fail cheaply before full investment.

**Real-life applications:**
- Clickable Figma prototypes for UX testing
- 3D-printed hardware models
- Working software prototypes with mock data
- Foam-core mockups for physical products
- Role-play prototypes for services

**Key concepts:** `prototyping`, `low-fidelity`, `high-fidelity`, `early-feedback`, `iteration`

---

## Q56. What is A/B testing?

**Level:** Basic

A/B testing compares two versions (A and B) of a feature, page, or experience to see which performs better. Users are randomly split into two groups and a key metric is measured. It replaces opinion with evidence.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing two landing page headlines
- Comparing two checkout flows
- Running two pricing plans on different cohorts
- Trying two different onboarding emails
- Testing button colors in a signup form

**Key concepts:** `ab-test`, `experiment`, `control-variant`, `statistical-significance`, `conversion`

---

## Q57. What is a North Star Metric?

**Level:** Basic

A North Star Metric is the single measure that best reflects the value a product delivers to customers. Examples include weekly active users, nights booked, or monthly trips. A clear North Star aligns the team on one true goal.

**Real-life applications:**
- Airbnb tracking "nights booked"
- Spotify tracking "time spent listening"
- Slack tracking "messages sent in connected teams"
- Uber tracking "weekly trips"
- Zoom tracking "weekly meetings hosted"

**Key concepts:** `north-star`, `key-metric`, `alignment`, `customer-value`, `focus`

---

## Q58. What are vanity metrics?

**Level:** Basic

Vanity metrics are numbers that look impressive but do not drive decisions or reflect real value. Examples include total page views or total signups without context. Teams should track actionable metrics instead.

**Real-life applications:**
- Boasting about total downloads while ignoring retention
- Tracking social media likes over conversions
- Counting all signups, including inactive ones
- Measuring site traffic without engagement
- Reporting total registered but not monthly active

**Key concepts:** `vanity-metrics`, `actionable-metrics`, `misleading-data`, `engagement`, `retention`

---

## Q59. What is retention?

**Level:** Basic

Retention is the share of customers who keep using a product over time. High retention means customers find value repeatedly. It is often tracked as day-7, day-30, and day-90 retention in apps, or monthly churn in SaaS.

**Real-life applications:**
- Measuring day-7 retention of a mobile app
- Tracking monthly churn of a SaaS subscription
- Running win-back campaigns
- Improving onboarding to lift retention
- Identifying churn predictors

**Key concepts:** `retention`, `churn`, `engagement`, `stickiness`, `lifetime`

---

## Q60. What is churn?

**Level:** Basic

Churn is the percentage of customers who stop using a product in a given period. It is the opposite of retention. Reducing churn is usually cheaper than acquiring new customers.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking monthly SaaS churn
- Running exit surveys to learn why users leave
- Targeting at-risk users with support
- Improving product stickiness
- Forecasting MRR with churn assumptions

**Key concepts:** `churn`, `retention`, `exit-survey`, `MRR`, `loyalty`

---

## Q61. What is a focus group?

**Level:** Basic

A focus group is a small moderated discussion with target customers to explore opinions, reactions, and ideas. It is qualitative research and produces rich insight but not statistical proof. Common early in product development.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing new ad concepts with consumers
- Exploring pain points for a new service
- Evaluating packaging designs
- Understanding category perceptions
- Gathering language used by customers

**Key concepts:** `focus-group`, `qualitative-research`, `moderation`, `early-stage`, `insight`

---

## Q62. What is a customer interview?

**Level:** Basic

A customer interview is a one-on-one conversation with a user to understand their needs, behaviors, and pains. Open-ended questions and active listening work best. Interviews often reveal problems that surveys miss.

**Real-life applications:**
- Interviewing 10 churned customers for reasons
- Exploring daily workflows of a target user
- Finding unmet needs in a new segment
- Validating a concept with potential buyers
- Testing usability of a prototype

**Key concepts:** `customer-interview`, `qualitative`, `discovery`, `open-ended`, `empathy`

---

## Q63. What is user testing?

**Level:** Basic

User testing is watching real users try to complete tasks using a product or prototype. It reveals usability issues, confusion, and opportunities. Even testing with 5 users often surfaces the biggest problems.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing a new signup flow
- Watching users try to add a product to a cart
- Moderated remote tests via Zoom
- Unmoderated tests via tools like Maze
- Accessibility testing with screen readers

**Key concepts:** `user-testing`, `usability`, `observation`, `task-completion`, `iteration`

---

## Q64. What is an NPS score?

**Level:** Basic

NPS (Net Promoter Score) measures customer loyalty by asking "How likely are you to recommend us?" on a 0-10 scale. Promoters (9-10) minus detractors (0-6) gives the score. Tracking NPS over time signals product health.

**Real-life applications:**
- Running quarterly NPS surveys
- Linking NPS drops to product issues
- Comparing NPS with competitors
- Following up with detractors for insights
- Using NPS as a team-wide KPI

**Key concepts:** `NPS`, `loyalty`, `promoter`, `detractor`, `survey`

---

## Q65. What is a stakeholder?

**Level:** Basic

A stakeholder is anyone with an interest in the product's direction — customers, executives, sales, support, engineering, legal. Good PMs map stakeholders and communicate with each based on their needs. Missing key stakeholders causes launch surprises.

**Real-life applications:**
- Engaging legal early on data features
- Looping in sales on pricing changes
- Aligning support on new feature questions
- Keeping executives informed with dashboards
- Running stakeholder kick-offs at project start

**Key concepts:** `stakeholder`, `alignment`, `communication`, `mapping`, `influence`

---

## Q66. What is time-to-market (TTM)?

**Level:** Basic

Time-to-market is the duration from idea to a product being available to customers. Shorter TTM means earlier revenue and earlier learning. It is a key competitive metric in fast-moving markets.

**Real-life applications:**
- Cutting TTM by parallelizing workstreams
- Launching MVPs quickly for learning
- Reusing platforms across products
- Eliminating unnecessary approvals
- Running pilots to validate before full launch

**Key concepts:** `TTM`, `speed`, `competitive-advantage`, `launch-speed`, `efficiency`

---

## Q67. What is a cross-functional team?

**Level:** Basic

A cross-functional team brings together people from different departments — engineering, design, marketing, operations, legal — to work toward one product outcome. It removes handoffs and speeds decisions. Common in agile and project-based structures.

**Real-life applications:**
- Launching a new feature with a full-stack team
- Running a growth pod with PM, data, design, engineering
- Cross-functional hackathons
- Integrated launch teams
- Sprint teams with all needed skills

**Key concepts:** `cross-functional`, `team-structure`, `collaboration`, `removal-of-handoffs`, `agile`

---

## Q68. What is a matrix organization?

**Level:** Basic

A matrix organization has employees reporting to both a functional manager (e.g., engineering head) and a project or product manager. It balances functional expertise with project focus. Downsides include dual reporting and potential conflict over priorities.

**Real-life applications:**
- An engineer reporting to tech lead and PM
- Designer shared across three product pods
- Shared data science team serving many products
- Brand managers working with shared R&D
- Regional teams with central product leads

**Key concepts:** `matrix`, `dual-reporting`, `shared-resources`, `org-design`, `trade-offs`

---

## Q69. What is a risk register?

**Level:** Basic

A risk register is a document listing known project risks, their likelihood, impact, owner, and mitigation plan. It is reviewed regularly so risks don't surprise the team. Widely used in hardware, pharma, and enterprise projects.

**Real-life applications:**
- Tracking risks weekly on a launch
- Flagging regulatory risks in a pharma project
- Monitoring tech risks for new AI features
- Assigning owners to each risk
- Updating mitigation plans as status changes

**Key concepts:** `risk-register`, `risk-management`, `mitigation`, `likelihood`, `project-planning`

---

## Q70. What is a growth mindset?

**Level:** Basic

A growth mindset is the belief that skills and abilities develop with effort, feedback, and learning. In product teams, it shows up as willingness to experiment, accept critique, and learn from failure. The opposite — fixed mindset — avoids risk and hides mistakes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Holding blameless post-mortems
- Celebrating learnings from failed tests
- Rotating engineers across tech stacks
- Running frequent retrospectives
- Rewarding bold experiments, not just wins

**Key concepts:** `growth-mindset`, `learning`, `feedback`, `resilience`, `culture`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Stage-Gate NPD
**Purpose:** Break product development into gates that control risk and funding.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Idea -> Screen -> Concept -> Develop -> Test -> Launch
   |      |         |         |        |        |
  Kill   Go/Kill   Go/Kill   Go/Kill   Go/Kill  Scale
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Idea screening - remove weak concepts early.
Component 2: Concept development - define the product and promise.
Component 3: Development and testing - build, test, and learn before scale.
Component 4: Commercialization - launch only when the evidence is good enough.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI copilot for finance teams gets a gate after concept testing, a second gate after MVP usage metrics, and a final gate only when pilot users show repeat weekly use and positive retention.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a team wants to spend heavily before proving the concept.

### Jobs To Be Done and Customer Journey
**Purpose:** Map what the customer is trying to accomplish and where the friction lives.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Need -> Search -> Try -> Use -> Repeat
   ^                              |
   |------------------------------|
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Functional job - the task the user must get done.
Component 2: Emotional job - confidence, relief, status, or safety.
Component 3: Touchpoints - places where the product can help or fail.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: For a payroll app, the functional job is pay employees correctly; the emotional job is confidence that compliance will not break; the journey map shows the biggest pain at onboarding and monthly reconciliation.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When teams are arguing about features without understanding the user workflow.

### TAM / SAM / SOM Funnel
**Purpose:** Size the market without confusing total demand with reachable demand.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
TAM
  SAM
    SOM
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: TAM - total demand if the company could reach everyone.
Component 2: SAM - the part the company can actually serve.
Component 3: SOM - the part the company can realistically win near-term.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A healthcare AI startup may see a $2B TAM, a $300M SAM in one workflow, and a $25M SOM in the first two regions after compliance limits and sales capacity.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a pitch deck inflates the market by using only the largest possible number.

### Product Lifecycle and Pricing
**Purpose:** Match the pricing model to the product's stage and demand curve.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Introduction -> Growth -> Maturity -> Decline
Pricing: skimming -> premium -> competitive -> harvest
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Introduction - learn and validate, often with skimming or freemium.
Component 2: Growth - build adoption and test pricing power.
Component 3: Maturity - defend share, optimize margin, manage competition.
Component 4: Decline - harvest, extend, or sunset.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS feature launches at premium pricing to early adopters, shifts to penetration pricing once it has PMF, and later moves to competitive pricing when the category is crowded.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When pricing is being discussed without reference to lifecycle stage or margin.

### RICE vs MoSCoW Prioritization
**Purpose:** Rank features when you cannot do everything.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
RICE: Reach x Impact x Confidence / Effort
MoSCoW: Must / Should / Could / Won't
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: RICE - a quantitative prioritization score.
Component 2: MoSCoW - a communication-friendly priority bucket.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team uses RICE to score backlog items, then MoSCoW to communicate the final plan to executives and engineering.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When roadmap debates become subjective and noisy.

### Agile Delivery Operating Model
**Purpose:** Keep product work visible, bounded, and shippable.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Backlog -> Sprint -> Review -> Retro -> Backlog
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Sprint cadence - short time-boxed delivery cycles.
Component 2: WIP limits - prevent overload and hidden work.
Component 3: Continuous delivery - shorten the feedback loop.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team delivering a new billing flow limits WIP, ships weekly, and uses sprint reviews to decide whether to iterate, pause, or expand scope.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When deadlines are slipping because too much is in flight.

## 4. Formulas
The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source]; the source names the concepts but does not state the math explicitly.

Formula: CLV:CAC ratio = CLV / CAC
Variables:
CLV = customer lifetime value.
CAC = customer acquisition cost.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the product can buy growth profitably.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 1 -> the business is destroying value -> stop scaling acquisition.
Value 1-3 -> fragile economics -> improve retention or pricing.
Value > 3 -> healthy unit economics -> consider adding spend.
Worked example with numbers: If CLV is $600 and CAC is $150, the ratio is 4.0, which supports more growth spend if retention stays stable.

Formula: Payback period (months) = CAC / monthly gross profit per customer
Variables:
CAC = customer acquisition cost.
Monthly gross profit per customer = monthly revenue minus variable serving cost.
Why this formula exists: It shows how long growth capital is tied up before getting paid back.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 6 months -> fast payback -> aggressive growth is easier to justify.
Value 6-12 months -> watch carefully -> scale only if retention is strong.
Value > 12 months -> slow payback -> tighten acquisition or pricing.
Worked example with numbers: If CAC is $180 and monthly gross profit is $30, payback is 6 months.

Formula: Conversion rate = (Conversions / Visitors) x 100
Variables:
Conversions = users who completed the desired action.
Visitors = total users who saw the page, flow, or offer.
Why this formula exists: It measures whether the product or funnel is persuasive.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 2% -> funnel is weak -> fix messaging or friction.
Value 2%-5% -> acceptable for many offers -> improve with experiments.
Value > 5% -> strong conversion -> scale the channel if margins hold.
Worked example with numbers: If 200 of 8,000 visitors sign up, conversion rate is 2.5%, which suggests A/B testing before increasing spend.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Build features before defining the customer problem. | Start with customer interviews, pain points, and a clear job-to-be-done. |
| Treat roadmap items as a feature wishlist. | Organize the roadmap around outcomes, themes, and business value. |
| Use TAM as if it were reachable revenue. | Separate TAM, SAM, and SOM before making a market-entry call. |
| Scale acquisition before CLV:CAC is healthy. | Validate unit economics and payback period before adding budget. |
| Ship without a kill decision. | Use stage-gates, MVP tests, and go/no-go criteria to control risk. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: SaaS onboarding redesign
Situation: A B2B SaaS product has 10,000 monthly visitors, 250 signups, and only 40 activated accounts. Support tickets are rising because onboarding is too complex.
Applicable framework/metric: Customer journey, conversion rate, and activation.
Analysis: Visitor-to-signup conversion is 2.5%, but signup-to-activation is only 16%. That means the landing page is passable and the real issue is inside the activation flow.
Decision rule: If signup conversion is below 2%, fix messaging; if activation is below 25%, fix onboarding; if both are healthy, add acquisition spend.
Action: Simplify onboarding, reduce mandatory fields, and add in-product guidance before paying for more traffic.

Scenario 2: Freemium pricing test
Situation: A consumer app has 100,000 free users, 4,000 paying users, $1.20 monthly gross profit per free user from ads, and $8 monthly gross profit per paying user.
Applicable framework/metric: CLV:CAC ratio and lifecycle pricing.
Analysis: If free-to-paid conversion is 4% and paid retention is flat, the team should test whether the premium features actually raise CLV enough to justify CAC.
Decision rule: If paid conversion is under 3%, improve value proposition; if 3%-6%, iterate pricing and packaging; if over 6%, scale acquisition carefully.
Action: Run an A/B test on premium packaging, compare churn by cohort, and only raise acquisition budget if payback stays under 12 months.

Scenario 3: Hardware feature prioritization
Situation: A hardware startup wants to add a premium sensor, but the BOM increase is $12 and the expected willingness-to-pay lift is only $8.
Applicable framework/metric: DFM, value-based pricing, and prioritization.
Analysis: The feature fails a simple economic test unless it also improves conversion, retention, or channel access. If not, it is a margin-destroying add-on.
Decision rule: If BOM lift exceeds willingness-to-pay lift, cut or redesign; if the ratio is near break-even, test with a high-fidelity prototype; if it is strongly positive, move to build.
Action: Rework the design-for-manufacturability path, prototype the cheaper version, and re-score it in the backlog.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

2. Create a roadmap that groups work into outcome themes instead of raw feature lists.
3. Run customer interviews and journey mapping before approving major build work.
4. Set a stage-gate or MVP test for every meaningful launch so funding follows evidence.
5. Track CLV, CAC, retention, conversion, and payback period in a weekly dashboard.
6. Use RICE or MoSCoW to prioritize the backlog and publish the scoring rationale.
7. Instrument experiments and keep a decision log that records what was learned, what changed, and why.
