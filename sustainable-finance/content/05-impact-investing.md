# Impact Investing

## Overview
Impact investing is a strategy that seeks to generate measurable social or environmental benefits alongside a financial return. Unlike philanthropy, impact investments are expected to produce a profit, and unlike conventional investing, they deliberately target positive outcomes such as poverty reduction, clean energy access, or improved healthcare. The market spans a wide range of asset classes, return expectations, and impact themes, attracting everyone from development finance institutions to family offices.

---

## Why It Matters
Traditional charity and government aid alone cannot close the funding gap for global challenges like climate change, inequality, and public health. Impact investing mobilizes private capital to address these gaps at scale while still rewarding investors. It also demonstrates that financial returns and positive impact are not mutually exclusive, encouraging broader adoption of sustainable practices across the investment industry.

## Key Principles
- Intentionality — the investor explicitly aims to create a positive social or environmental outcome
- Financial return — the investment is expected to generate a return on capital, ranging from below-market to market-rate
- Measurability — the social or environmental impact must be tracked and reported using recognized frameworks
- Additionality — the investment contributes outcomes that would not have occurred without the deployed capital

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Impact Investing** | Investing with the dual objective of generating financial returns and measurable positive social or environmental impact |
| **IRIS+ (Impact Reporting and Investment Standards)** | A system managed by the GIIN that provides standardized metrics for measuring and comparing impact performance |
| **GIIN (Global Impact Investing Network)** | A leading nonprofit organization dedicated to growing the scale and effectiveness of the impact investing market |
| **Blended Finance** | An approach that uses catalytic capital from public or philanthropic sources to attract private investment into impact-oriented projects |

## Use Case
A venture capital firm invests in an early-stage company that manufactures affordable water purification systems for rural communities in Sub-Saharan Africa, tracking both financial returns and the number of households gaining access to clean water.

## Scenario
> A family office allocates ten percent of its portfolio to impact investments, selecting a fund focused on affordable housing in underserved urban areas. Over five years, the fund delivers a steady annual return while financing the construction of thousands of housing units. The family office publishes an annual impact report showing both the financial performance and the number of families housed.

## Examples
- A development finance institution providing equity to a microfinance bank serving smallholder farmers in Southeast Asia
- A pension fund investing in a private infrastructure fund that builds solar mini-grids in off-grid communities

### Impact Logic Chain

### Additionality Test
**Purpose:** Decide whether the capital actually changes what happens.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Would this happen anyway? -> if yes, impact claim is weaker
Would this happen only with this capital? -> if yes, impact claim is stronger
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Counterfactual.
Component 2: Incremental outcome.
Component 3: Credibility of the impact claim.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A consulting-backed microfinance rollout is additional if the private market would not otherwise fund the same borrower segment.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone wants to call every socially useful project "impact."

## 4. Formulas

The source does not provide explicit formulas.

Formula: `Impact score = measured outcome / benchmark outcome`
Variables:
Measured outcome = the actual impact result
Benchmark outcome = target, baseline, or comparator
Why this formula exists: It turns impact reporting into something trackable and comparable.
How to interpret the output:
Score < 1 -> below target
Score = 1 -> at target
Score > 1 -> above target
Worked example with numbers: 8,000 households reached against a target of 10,000 gives a score of 0.8 [verified from model knowledge, not source].

## 5. Do vs Don't

| Don't | Do |
|---------|-------|
| Call any socially useful investment "impact" without intent. | Require intentionality and a clear impact thesis. |
| Report nice stories without a measurement system. | Track outcomes with recognizable metrics and regular reporting. |
| Assume all impact capital must accept poor returns. | Match return expectations to the mandate and asset class. |
| Treat blended finance as free money. | Use catalytic capital only where it unlocks private investment. |
| Ignore additionality. | Ask what changes because this capital was deployed. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

Scenario 1: AI-enabled healthcare access [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Situation: A product team is investing in an AI triage platform for rural clinics. The investor wants to know whether the model is a real impact investment or just a normal software play with a social label.
Applicable framework/metric: Intentionality, measurability, additionality.
Analysis: If the platform improves access for 50,000 patients but would have shipped anyway without the investor, the impact claim is weaker than if capital was needed to reach those clinics.
Decision rule: If additionality is strong and impact is measurable, proceed; if not, treat it as conventional software.
Action: Define the outcome metrics before close and tie them to reporting.

Scenario 2: Product-led affordable housing platform [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Situation: A product company is structuring a housing-finance platform for underserved urban areas. The team needs to balance market-rate returns with measurable social outcomes.
Applicable framework/metric: Impact-return matrix.
Analysis: If the deal can deliver a market-rate return while housing 2,000 families, it sits in the high-return/high-impact quadrant and is easier to scale.
Decision rule: If impact is measurable and returns fit the mandate, invest; if either is missing, redesign.
Action: Build an impact dashboard alongside the financial model.

Scenario 3: Consulting-backed clean energy fund [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Situation: A consulting team is helping a family office allocate 10 percent of its portfolio to clean-energy and health themes. It wants catalytic capital to attract private co-investors.
Applicable framework/metric: Blended finance and catalytic capital.
Analysis: If a 20 percent concessional layer unlocks 80 percent private capital and still preserves portfolio return objectives, the structure works.
Decision rule: If the catalytic layer crowds in private money at acceptable return, use blended finance; otherwise keep it as a conventional allocation.
Action: Set outcome KPIs, reporting cadence, and co-investor requirements before deployment.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Write an explicit impact thesis before screening deals.
2. Define the outcome metrics and the reporting cadence.
3. Test additionality so the investment is not just conventional capital with better branding.
4. Match the return profile to the investor mandate and asset class.
5. Use blended finance only when it genuinely unlocks private capital.
6. Publish an impact report that connects inputs, outputs, outcomes, and financial returns.
