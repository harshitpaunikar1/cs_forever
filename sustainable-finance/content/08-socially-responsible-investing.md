# Socially Responsible Investing

## Overview
Socially responsible investing (SRI) is an investment approach that selects or excludes assets based on ethical, social, or environmental criteria. It goes beyond financial analysis by incorporating the investor's values into portfolio construction — for example, avoiding companies involved in tobacco, weapons, or fossil fuels. SRI has evolved from simple exclusionary screening to include positive screening, thematic investing, and active shareholder engagement as strategies for aligning portfolios with societal values.

---

## Why It Matters
Investors increasingly want their money to reflect their beliefs about how the world should work. SRI provides a disciplined approach for doing so without necessarily sacrificing returns. Research consistently shows that SRI portfolios can perform on par with or better than conventional benchmarks, challenging the longstanding assumption that ethical investing requires a financial trade-off.

## Key Principles
- Negative screening — excluding companies or sectors that conflict with the investor's ethical or social values
- Positive screening — actively selecting companies that demonstrate strong social or environmental performance
- Shareholder engagement — using ownership rights to influence corporate behavior through voting, dialogue, and resolutions
- Values alignment — ensuring that investment decisions consistently reflect the investor's stated ethical, religious, or social principles

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Negative Screening** | The practice of excluding specific companies, sectors, or countries from a portfolio based on ethical or sustainability criteria |
| **Positive Screening** | The practice of actively selecting companies that score highly on social, environmental, or governance metrics |
| **Shareholder Advocacy** | The use of shareholder rights — such as proxy voting and filing resolutions — to push companies toward more responsible behavior |
| **Norms-Based Screening** | Filtering investments based on compliance with international norms and standards such as the UN Global Compact or ILO conventions |

## Use Case
A university endowment adopts an SRI policy that excludes all companies deriving more than ten percent of revenue from thermal coal, while overweighting companies recognized as leaders in workplace diversity.

## Scenario
> A faith-based investment fund screens out companies involved in gambling, alcohol, and weapons manufacturing. When one of its holdings is found to be a significant arms supplier through a subsidiary, the fund divests and publicly explains its reasoning. The decision attracts new investors who share the fund's values, and the fund's assets under management grow by 15 percent the following year.

## Examples
- A retail investor choosing an SRI mutual fund that excludes fossil fuel and tobacco stocks while favoring companies with strong human rights records
- An institutional investor filing a shareholder resolution requesting that a major retailer publish a report on gender pay equity across its workforce

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Socially responsible investing, or SRI, is the “values filter” layer on top of normal portfolio construction. It helps an IT, AI, Product, or Consulting decision-maker align capital with ethics, environmental concerns, or social priorities without treating financial return as the only objective.
The decision it supports is simple: what should be excluded, favored, or engaged with before capital is committed?

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### SRI Policy Ladder
**Purpose:** Show how SRI moves from avoidance to ownership influence.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Exclude -> Prefer -> Shape
  |         |        |
  |         |        +-- Active shareholder engagement
  |         +----------- Positive screening / thematic investing
  +--------------------- Negative screening
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Negative screening: removes assets that conflict with the policy.
Positive screening: selects better-aligned assets.
Thematic investing: concentrates capital around a specific value or issue.
Shareholder engagement: keeps ownership while trying to change the company.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm managing its partner retirement plan excludes tobacco and weapons, prefers software vendors with strong diversity disclosures, and then votes against a cloud vendor board because of weak privacy oversight.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone asks whether the mandate is “just exclusions” or also includes active ownership.

### Values Alignment Matrix
**Purpose:** Clarify whether the portfolio is being built for moral consistency, social impact, or both.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                     Financial return focus
                 Low                         High
Values focus  +----------------+------------------------+
Low           | Passive market | Conventional investing |
              | exposure       | with no SRI overlay    |
              +----------------+------------------------+
High          | Pure values     | SRI portfolio with     |
              | screen          | screening + engagement  |
              +----------------+------------------------+
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Values focus: how strongly the investor wants beliefs embedded in the portfolio.
Financial return focus: how much the investor still cares about benchmark-like performance.
Pure values screen: maximizes policy consistency, even if it narrows choice.
SRI portfolio with screening + engagement: balances alignment and ownership influence.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI startup’s treasury policy bans fossil-fuel-heavy issuers, keeps a broad tech index for liquidity, and uses proxy voting on data-governance issues.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When stakeholders disagree on whether SRI should be a strict filter or a balanced overlay.

## 4. Formulas
Formula: `exclude if revenue_share_from_excluded_activity > policy_threshold` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
revenue_share_from_excluded_activity = revenue from the prohibited activity divided by total company revenue
policy_threshold = the cutoff written in the mandate
Why this formula exists: It turns a values rule into a consistent, auditable screening test.
How to interpret the output:
Value < threshold → keep for review or inclusion
Value = threshold → apply policy exactly as written
Value > threshold → exclude or escalate for stewardship review
Worked example with numbers: A hardware vendor earns 12% of revenue from weapons-linked contracts. If the policy threshold is 10%, the stock is excluded.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat SRI as a vague “feel-good” label | Write a clear screening policy with inclusion and exclusion rules |
| Assume all ethical screens are the same | Separate negative screening, positive screening, thematic investing, and engagement |
| Ignore hidden exposure through subsidiaries | Trace revenue and control relationships before approval |
| Use proxy voting only as a checkbox | Use shareholder rights to push concrete governance changes |
| Let values drift from portfolio construction | Re-check that holdings still match the stated mandate |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: Enterprise SaaS supplier screen
Situation: A consulting firm is picking an enterprise SaaS vendor for a client’s employee-benefits platform. One bidder earns 14% of revenue from thermal coal logistics software, and the client has a climate-aligned mandate.
Applicable framework/metric: Negative screening, revenue-share threshold.
Analysis: If the policy threshold is 10%, a 14% exposure fails the screen even if the product itself is good.
Decision rule: If revenue share is above the threshold, reject. If it is near the threshold, escalate for legal review. If below, continue with product and security diligence.
Action: Record the screen in the vendor shortlist and request revenue disclosures before final procurement.

Scenario 2: AI portfolio stewardship
Situation: An AI fund owns a large cloud company that has weak reporting on model governance and data privacy. The manager wants to keep the holding but influence behavior.
Applicable framework/metric: Shareholder engagement, proxy voting.
Analysis: The manager can vote against directors, support a resolution, and track whether disclosure improves over the next proxy cycle.
Decision rule: If engagement fails for two cycles, escalate from dialogue to divestment review. If improvement starts, keep engagement active. If the company adopts the requested controls, maintain ownership.
Action: Create a stewardship tracker with vote outcome, company response, and next review date.

Scenario 3: Product-launch capital allocation
Situation: A product team is deciding whether to park launch reserves in a fund that excludes weapons, gambling, and alcohol while preferring companies with high workplace diversity scores.
Applicable framework/metric: Positive screening plus negative screening.
Analysis: The team wants a cash-like reserve vehicle, but the mandate should still fit the founder’s values and the brand story told to customers and employees.
Decision rule: If the fund violates any exclusion list item, do not use it. If it passes the exclusions and has better diversity performance than peers, prefer it. If no suitable option exists, relax only the return constraint, not the values constraint.
Action: Compare three approved funds and document the screen in the treasury policy.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Draft the values policy as a one-page mandate with explicit excluded sectors and preferred traits.
2. Build a screening checklist covering tobacco, weapons, fossil fuels, gambling, alcohol, and thermal coal.
3. Add a subsidiary-tracing step so hidden exposure is not missed.
4. Set a stewardship calendar for proxy voting, dialogue, and resolution tracking.
5. Define escalation rules for borderline holdings and failed engagement.
6. Report portfolio fit with a simple dashboard showing passes, fails, and watchlist names.
7. Review the mandate annually with the investment committee or leadership team.
