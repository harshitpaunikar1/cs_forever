# Climate Risk and Financial Impact

## Overview
Climate risk refers to the potential for financial losses arising from the effects of climate change. These risks fall into two broad categories: physical risks, such as damage from floods, wildfires, and rising sea levels, and transition risks, such as policy changes, technological shifts, and market adjustments as economies move toward lower carbon emissions. Understanding climate risk is essential for accurate asset valuation, credit assessment, and long-term strategic planning.

---

## Why It Matters
Climate change is no longer a distant threat — it is already affecting asset values, insurance costs, supply chains, and credit ratings. Financial institutions that fail to account for climate risk face unexpected losses, regulatory action, and erosion of stakeholder trust. Incorporating climate risk into financial models helps organizations protect value and identify opportunities in the transition to a sustainable economy.

## Key Principles
- Distinguishing between physical risks (acute events and chronic shifts) and transition risks (policy, technology, market, and reputation changes)
- Using scenario analysis to model potential financial outcomes under different climate pathways
- Integrating climate risk into existing risk management frameworks rather than treating it as a separate concern
- Recognizing that climate risk is systemic and affects all sectors, geographies, and asset classes

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Physical Risk** | Financial exposure arising from the direct effects of climate change, including extreme weather events and long-term environmental shifts |
| **Transition Risk** | Financial exposure arising from the process of adjusting to a low-carbon economy, including changes in regulation, technology, and consumer behavior |
| **Stranded Assets** | Resources or infrastructure that lose value prematurely due to climate-related policy changes, technological disruption, or shifting market demand |
| **Scenario Analysis** | A method for evaluating the potential financial impact of different climate futures, such as a 1.5-degree versus a 3-degree warming pathway |

## Use Case
An insurance company uses climate scenario analysis to reassess its exposure to coastal property portfolios, adjusting premiums and coverage limits to reflect the rising probability of flood damage over the next two decades.

## Scenario
> A major coal mining company sees its stock price decline steadily as governments announce stricter emissions regulations and utilities accelerate their shift to natural gas and renewables. Investors who failed to account for transition risk find themselves holding stranded assets. Meanwhile, a competitor that diversified into clean energy early captures market share and attracts new capital.

## Examples
- A bank stress-testing its mortgage portfolio against flood-risk maps to identify loans at elevated default risk
- A sovereign wealth fund divesting from oil and gas companies after modeling the financial impact of a global carbon tax

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Climate risk is the chance that climate change lowers asset value, raises costs, or weakens credit quality.
It matters because physical risk and transition risk can both hit cash flow, valuation, and financing terms.
The decision it supports is whether to reprice, hedge, retrofit, insure, divest, or redesign an asset before the loss becomes obvious.

## 4. Formulas

The source does not provide a formal formula.

Formula: `Climate stress impact = baseline value - stressed value`
Variables:
Baseline value = current or base-case asset value
Stressed value = value under climate or transition scenario
Why this formula exists: It converts climate analysis into a financial number the board can act on.
How to interpret the output:
Impact small -> monitor
Impact moderate -> mitigate or insure
Impact large -> reprice, exit, or redesign
Worked example with numbers: A mortgage portfolio worth 100 falls to 92 in a flood scenario, so the stress impact is 8 [verified from model knowledge, not source].

## 5. Do vs Don't

| Don't | Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat climate risk as a distant ESG issue. | Treat it as a current valuation and credit issue. |
| Focus only on floods and storms. | Include transition risk, policy change, and market shifts. |
| Use one climate scenario and stop there. | Compare at least a low and high warming pathway. |
| Ignore supply-chain knock-on effects. | Model indirect operating and customer impacts too. |
| Wait for regulatory pressure before acting. | Fold climate risk into strategy, insurance, and capital planning now. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

Scenario 1: AI data center site selection [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Situation: A product/AI company is choosing between two data-center sites. One is cheaper but has higher flood exposure; the other costs more but has lower downtime risk and better insurance terms.
Applicable framework/metric: Physical risk and scenario analysis.
Analysis: If the cheaper site saves 2 percent upfront but increases expected annual loss by 3 percent, the lifetime cost is worse even before reputational damage is included.
Decision rule: If climate-adjusted NPV is worse, pick the safer site; if the risk difference is marginal, mitigate with design and insurance.
Action: Add flood and heat stress maps to site selection and vendor scoring.

Scenario 2: Product company supply-chain redesign [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Situation: A consumer-product team is sourcing materials from a region exposed to drought and chronic heat. The team wants to keep cost low but avoid future stockouts and price spikes.
Applicable framework/metric: Chronic shifts, supply chains, stranded assets.
Analysis: If a 10 percent supply disruption probability turns into a 2 percent revenue hit, the apparently cheaper supplier is no longer cheap after climate stress.
Decision rule: If the climate-adjusted cost delta exceeds the price gap, shift suppliers or dual-source.
Action: Put climate risk into procurement scorecards and inventory strategy.

Scenario 3: Consulting-led portfolio review [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Situation: A consulting team is helping a bank review a lending book concentrated in coastal property and carbon-intensive manufacturing. The client wants to know whether to tighten credit, raise insurance requirements, or reallocate capital.
Applicable framework/metric: Transition risk, credit assessment, credit ratings.
Analysis: If sector stress raises expected losses by more than the margin buffer, the portfolio needs repricing or de-risking. A 3-degree scenario may also justify exiting the most exposed names.
Decision rule: If stressed expected loss exceeds the spread cushion, reduce exposure; if not, monitor and disclose.
Action: Integrate climate scenarios into annual credit review and board reporting.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Map physical and transition risks separately for each major asset or portfolio.
2. Run at least two climate scenarios and translate them into cash, value, and credit effects.
3. Update insurance, procurement, and capital planning based on the stress results.
4. Add climate metrics to portfolio review and credit memo templates.
5. Decide whether to mitigate, transfer, redesign, or exit each high-risk exposure.
6. Disclose the methodology so stakeholders can see the logic, not just the conclusion.
