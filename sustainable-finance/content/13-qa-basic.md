# Basic Questions — Q1 to Q70

## Q1. What is sustainable finance?

**Level:** Basic

Sustainable finance is the practice of incorporating environmental, social, and governance considerations into financial decisions. It aims to direct capital toward activities that support long-term economic health while addressing challenges like climate change, resource depletion, and social inequality.

**Real-life applications:**
- Banks offering green loans to businesses adopting renewable energy
- Investors screening portfolios for companies with strong ESG practices
- Governments issuing green bonds to fund clean infrastructure
- Insurance companies adjusting policies based on climate risk assessments

**Key concepts:** `sustainable finance`, `ESG`, `capital allocation`, `long-term value`, `climate change`

---

## Q2. What do the letters E, S, and G stand for in ESG?

**Level:** Basic

E stands for Environmental, which covers a company's impact on the natural world. S stands for Social, which addresses relationships with employees, communities, and other stakeholders. G stands for Governance, which concerns how a company is led, including board structure, ethics, and transparency.

**Real-life applications:**
- Rating agencies scoring companies across all three ESG dimensions
- Job seekers evaluating potential employers based on social policies
- Shareholders voting on governance resolutions at annual meetings
- Regulators requiring companies to report on environmental impacts

**Key concepts:** `environmental`, `social`, `governance`, `ESG pillars`, `corporate responsibility`

---

## Q3. What is a green bond?

**Level:** Basic

A green bond is a fixed-income instrument whose proceeds are exclusively used to finance projects with environmental benefits, such as renewable energy, energy efficiency, or pollution prevention. Green bonds follow the same financial mechanics as conventional bonds but include commitments about how the raised capital will be spent.

**Real-life applications:**
- Cities funding public transit electrification through green bond issuance
- Corporations refinancing renewable energy projects with green bonds
- Development banks channeling capital to clean water projects in emerging markets
- Investors building portfolios focused exclusively on green fixed-income assets

**Key concepts:** `green bond`, `use of proceeds`, `fixed income`, `environmental benefit`, `bond issuance`

---

## Q4. What is climate risk in the context of finance?

**Level:** Basic

Climate risk refers to the potential for financial losses caused by the effects of climate change. It includes physical risks from extreme weather events and environmental shifts, as well as transition risks from policy changes, technological disruption, and shifting market preferences as economies move toward lower emissions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Banks assessing flood risk before approving coastal property mortgages
- Insurers repricing policies in wildfire-prone regions
- Energy companies evaluating the impact of carbon pricing on their profitability
- Investors divesting from sectors vulnerable to regulatory changes

**Key concepts:** `climate risk`, `physical risk`, `transition risk`, `financial loss`, `extreme weather`

---

## Q5. What is impact investing?

**Level:** Basic

Impact investing is an investment approach that intentionally seeks to generate measurable social or environmental benefits alongside a financial return. Unlike philanthropy, impact investments expect to earn a profit, and unlike conventional investing, they deliberately target positive outcomes such as clean energy access or poverty reduction.

**Real-life applications:**
- Venture funds investing in affordable healthcare startups in low-income countries
- Family offices allocating capital to clean water infrastructure projects
- Community development financial institutions lending to underserved neighborhoods
- Pension funds investing in affordable housing development

**Key concepts:** `impact investing`, `measurable impact`, `financial return`, `social benefit`, `intentionality`

---

## Q6. What is socially responsible investing?

**Level:** Basic

Socially responsible investing (SRI) is an investment strategy that selects or excludes assets based on ethical, social, or environmental criteria. It allows investors to align their portfolios with their values — for example, by avoiding companies involved in tobacco, weapons, or fossil fuels while favoring those with strong labor or environmental practices.

**Real-life applications:**
- Faith-based funds excluding gambling and alcohol companies
- University endowments divesting from fossil fuel producers
- Retail investors selecting mutual funds labeled as socially responsible
- Pension funds adopting exclusion lists for controversial weapons manufacturers

**Key concepts:** `SRI`, `ethical investing`, `negative screening`, `values alignment`, `exclusion criteria`

---

## Q7. What is the difference between physical risk and transition risk?

**Level:** Basic

Physical risk arises from the direct effects of climate change, such as floods, hurricanes, droughts, and rising sea levels that can damage assets and disrupt operations. Transition risk arises from the economic adjustments associated with moving to a low-carbon economy, including new regulations, carbon pricing, technological shifts, and changes in consumer behavior.

**Real-life applications:**
- A real estate firm assessing flood exposure in its property portfolio
- An auto manufacturer planning for the shift from combustion engines to electric vehicles
- A utility company budgeting for compliance with new emissions regulations
- An agricultural business adapting to changing rainfall patterns

**Key concepts:** `physical risk`, `transition risk`, `climate adaptation`, `low-carbon economy`, `regulatory change`

---

## Q8. What is the EU Taxonomy?

**Level:** Basic

The EU Taxonomy is a classification system that defines which economic activities can be considered environmentally sustainable under European Union law. It provides a common language for investors, companies, and policymakers to determine whether an investment or business activity genuinely contributes to environmental objectives such as climate change mitigation or pollution prevention.

**Real-life applications:**
- Fund managers classifying their products based on taxonomy alignment
- Companies reporting the percentage of revenue from taxonomy-aligned activities
- Banks assessing the green asset ratio of their loan portfolios
- Regulators using the taxonomy to evaluate corporate sustainability claims

**Key concepts:** `EU Taxonomy`, `classification system`, `environmental objectives`, `taxonomy alignment`, `green activity`

---

## Q9. What is a carbon credit?

**Level:** Basic

A carbon credit is a certificate representing the reduction or removal of one metric ton of carbon dioxide equivalent from the atmosphere. Companies and individuals can purchase carbon credits to offset their own emissions, typically from projects like reforestation, renewable energy installations, or methane capture at landfills.

**Real-life applications:**
- Airlines purchasing credits to offset flight emissions
- Tech companies buying credits to achieve carbon-neutral operations
- Forest conservation projects generating credits for sale on voluntary markets
- Event organizers offsetting the carbon footprint of large conferences

**Key concepts:** `carbon credit`, `carbon offset`, `metric ton CO2e`, `emissions reduction`, `voluntary market`

## Q10. What does ESG reporting mean?

**Level:** Basic

ESG reporting is the practice of publicly disclosing a company's performance on environmental, social, and governance metrics. Reports typically cover topics such as greenhouse gas emissions, workforce diversity, board composition, and community impact. Standardized frameworks like GRI, SASB, and ISSB help ensure that disclosures are consistent and comparable.

**Real-life applications:**
- Listed companies publishing annual sustainability reports alongside financial statements
- Investors using ESG reports to inform portfolio decisions
- Regulators auditing companies for compliance with disclosure mandates
- Rating agencies collecting reported data to generate ESG scores

**Key concepts:** `ESG reporting`, `disclosure`, `sustainability report`, `transparency`, `standardized frameworks`

---

## Q11. What is greenwashing?

**Level:** Basic

Greenwashing is the practice of making misleading or exaggerated claims about the environmental or social benefits of a product, service, or company. It undermines trust in sustainable finance by making it difficult for investors and consumers to distinguish genuine sustainability efforts from marketing spin.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company labeling a product as eco-friendly without evidence to support the claim
- A fund marketing itself as green while holding significant fossil fuel assets
- A bank advertising sustainable lending while financing deforestation
- Regulators imposing fines on companies for false environmental advertising

**Key concepts:** `greenwashing`, `misleading claims`, `false advertising`, `trust`, `regulatory enforcement`

---

## Q12. What is the GRI framework?

**Level:** Basic

The Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) is an international organization that provides a widely used set of standards for sustainability reporting. GRI standards cover economic, environmental, and social topics and are designed to help organizations report on their impacts in a consistent, comparable, and transparent manner.

**Real-life applications:**
- Multinational companies using GRI standards to prepare their sustainability reports
- Stakeholders comparing sustainability performance across companies using GRI disclosures
- Governments referencing GRI standards in national reporting regulations
- NGOs analyzing GRI reports to assess corporate accountability

**Key concepts:** `GRI`, `sustainability reporting`, `reporting standards`, `comparability`, `transparency`

## Q13. What is a sustainability-linked loan?

**Level:** Basic

A sustainability-linked loan is a credit facility where the interest rate or other terms adjust based on the borrower achieving predefined sustainability performance targets. Unlike green loans, the proceeds are not restricted to specific projects — the borrower can use the funds for general corporate purposes, but the cost of borrowing changes depending on ESG performance.

**Real-life applications:**
- A retailer receiving a lower interest rate after reducing packaging waste by a set percentage
- A logistics company with loan terms tied to fleet emissions reduction milestones
- A real estate developer whose borrowing costs decrease as buildings achieve energy certifications
- A food manufacturer with loan pricing linked to sustainable sourcing targets

**Key concepts:** `sustainability-linked loan`, `performance targets`, `interest rate adjustment`, `ESG metrics`, `general purpose lending`

---

## Q14. What is the TCFD framework?

**Level:** Basic

The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) is a framework that guides companies in disclosing climate-related risks and opportunities. It is organized around four pillars: governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets. TCFD has become one of the most widely adopted climate disclosure frameworks globally.

**Real-life applications:**
- Companies reporting their governance structures for managing climate risk
- Investors using TCFD disclosures to evaluate portfolio climate exposure
- Regulators mandating TCFD-aligned reporting for listed companies
- Banks assessing climate risk in their lending portfolios using TCFD guidance

**Key concepts:** `TCFD`, `climate disclosure`, `governance`, `risk management`, `metrics and targets`

---

## Q15. What are Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions?

**Level:** Basic

Scope 1 emissions are direct greenhouse gas emissions from a company's own operations, such as fuel burned in company vehicles or on-site manufacturing. Scope 2 emissions come from purchased electricity, heat, or steam. Scope 3 emissions are all other indirect emissions across the value chain, including those from suppliers, customers, and transportation.

**Real-life applications:**
- A manufacturer reporting Scope 1 emissions from its factory smokestacks
- A company calculating Scope 2 by analyzing electricity bills and grid carbon intensity
- A retailer estimating Scope 3 from shipping, product use, and end-of-life disposal
- Investors comparing companies on total carbon footprint across all three scopes

**Key concepts:** `Scope 1`, `Scope 2`, `Scope 3`, `greenhouse gas emissions`, `value chain`

---

## Q16. What is the role of a second-party opinion in green bond issuance?

**Level:** Basic

A second-party opinion (SPO) is an independent assessment conducted by an external reviewer that evaluates whether a green bond framework aligns with recognized standards such as the Green Bond Principles. It provides investors with confidence that the bond's environmental claims are credible and that proceeds will be used as stated.

**Real-life applications:**
- An issuer hiring a firm like Sustainalytics or Cicero to review its green bond framework
- Investors requiring SPOs before purchasing green bonds
- Rating agencies incorporating SPO findings into their assessments
- Regulators referencing SPOs when evaluating green bond compliance

**Key concepts:** `second-party opinion`, `green bond verification`, `independent review`, `Green Bond Principles`, `investor confidence`

---

## Q17. What is a cap-and-trade system?

**Level:** Basic

A cap-and-trade system is a regulatory mechanism in which a government sets an overall limit on greenhouse gas emissions and issues a fixed number of emission allowances to covered entities. Companies that emit less than their allowance can sell surplus permits to those that exceed theirs, creating a market-based incentive to reduce emissions efficiently.

**Real-life applications:**
- The European Union Emissions Trading System covering power plants and heavy industry
- California's cap-and-trade program regulating emissions from major polluters
- Companies trading allowances on exchanges to manage compliance costs
- Governments auctioning permits and using revenue for clean energy programs

**Key concepts:** `cap and trade`, `emissions cap`, `allowance trading`, `market-based mechanism`, `compliance`

---

## Q18. What does fiduciary duty mean in the context of sustainable finance?

**Level:** Basic

Fiduciary duty is the legal obligation of financial professionals to act in the best interest of their clients. In sustainable finance, this concept has been expanded to include consideration of ESG risks, since ignoring material sustainability factors could expose clients to financial losses and would therefore breach the duty of care.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pension fund trustees integrating ESG analysis into investment selection
- Financial advisors recommending portfolios that account for climate risk
- Regulators clarifying that fiduciary duty does not preclude ESG considerations
- Legal opinions confirming that ignoring material ESG risks may itself be a breach of duty

**Key concepts:** `fiduciary duty`, `duty of care`, `client interest`, `ESG integration`, `legal obligation`

---

## Q19. What is double materiality?

**Level:** Basic

Double materiality is the concept that companies should report both how sustainability issues affect their financial performance and how their operations affect society and the environment. It goes beyond traditional financial materiality, which only considers impacts on the company, by also requiring disclosure of the company's outward impacts on the world.

**Real-life applications:**
- A chemical company reporting both the financial cost of pollution lawsuits and its total emissions impact
- The EU CSRD requiring double materiality assessments from covered companies
- Investors using double materiality data to understand full risk profiles
- Sustainability teams mapping two-way impacts for each material topic

**Key concepts:** `double materiality`, `financial materiality`, `impact materiality`, `CSRD`, `two-way impact`

---

## Q20. What is the SFDR?

**Level:** Basic

The Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR) is an EU regulation that requires financial market participants to disclose how they integrate sustainability risks into their investment processes. It categorizes financial products into Article 6, Article 8, and Article 9 based on their level of sustainability commitment, from basic risk integration to having a specific sustainable investment objective.

**Real-life applications:**
- Asset managers classifying funds as Article 8 or Article 9 under SFDR
- Investors using SFDR classifications to select funds that match their sustainability preferences
- Compliance teams preparing pre-contractual disclosures for new fund launches
- Regulators auditing fund classifications to prevent greenwashing

**Key concepts:** `SFDR`, `disclosure regulation`, `Article 8`, `Article 9`, `sustainability risk`

---

## Q21. What is a stranded asset?

**Level:** Basic

A stranded asset is a resource, infrastructure, or investment that loses value prematurely because of changes related to the transition to a sustainable economy. Common causes include new environmental regulations, technological disruption, or shifting market demand that makes previously profitable assets uneconomic before the end of their expected useful life.

**Real-life applications:**
- Coal-fired power plants being retired early due to renewable energy competition
- Oil reserves that become unextractable under strict carbon budgets
- Real estate losing value due to rising sea levels or flood zoning changes
- Investors writing down fossil fuel assets on their balance sheets

**Key concepts:** `stranded asset`, `premature devaluation`, `carbon budget`, `transition risk`, `write-down`

---

## Q22. What is the Green Bond Principles framework?

**Level:** Basic

The Green Bond Principles (GBP) are voluntary guidelines published by the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) that recommend transparency and disclosure for green bond issuance. They are organized around four core components: use of proceeds, process for project evaluation and selection, management of proceeds, and reporting.

**Real-life applications:**
- Issuers structuring green bond frameworks in accordance with the GBP
- Investors referencing GBP compliance when evaluating green bonds
- Second-party opinion providers assessing alignment with the GBP
- Regulators using the GBP as a benchmark for national green bond standards

**Key concepts:** `Green Bond Principles`, `ICMA`, `use of proceeds`, `voluntary guidelines`, `reporting`

---

## Q23. What is supply chain finance?

**Level:** Basic

Supply chain finance (SCF) is a set of financial solutions that optimize cash flow across a supply chain by allowing suppliers to receive early payment on their invoices, typically at a discount based on the buyer's stronger creditworthiness. It benefits suppliers by improving liquidity and benefits buyers by extending payment terms without hurting supplier relationships.

**Real-life applications:**
- A large retailer offering early payment programs to small suppliers
- Banks providing platforms that connect buyers and suppliers for invoice financing
- Technology companies using SCF to strengthen relationships with component manufacturers
- Procurement departments using SCF to negotiate better terms with vendors

**Key concepts:** `supply chain finance`, `invoice financing`, `cash flow optimization`, `early payment`, `buyer creditworthiness`

---

## Q24. What are carbon markets?

**Level:** Basic

Carbon markets are trading systems where emission allowances or carbon credits are bought and sold. They exist in two main forms: compliance markets, created by government regulation such as cap-and-trade systems, and voluntary markets, where companies and individuals choose to purchase credits to offset their emissions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Governments operating emissions trading systems for regulated industries
- Companies buying voluntary credits to meet corporate net-zero pledges
- Brokers facilitating trades between carbon credit sellers and buyers
- Project developers generating credits from reforestation or renewable energy projects

**Key concepts:** `carbon market`, `compliance market`, `voluntary market`, `emission allowance`, `carbon trading`

---

## Q25. What is negative screening in investing?

**Level:** Basic

Negative screening is the practice of excluding specific companies, industries, or countries from an investment portfolio based on ethical, social, or environmental criteria. It is one of the oldest and most straightforward approaches to socially responsible investing, commonly used to avoid exposure to sectors like tobacco, weapons, or fossil fuels.

**Real-life applications:**
- A pension fund excluding all tobacco manufacturers from its portfolio
- An SRI mutual fund screening out companies involved in weapons production
- A faith-based investor avoiding gambling and alcohol companies
- An endowment removing fossil fuel producers from its holdings

**Key concepts:** `negative screening`, `exclusion`, `ethical criteria`, `portfolio construction`, `SRI`

---

## Q26. What is positive screening in investing?

**Level:** Basic

Positive screening is the practice of actively selecting companies for an investment portfolio based on their strong performance on environmental, social, or governance criteria. Instead of merely avoiding harmful companies, it seeks out those that are leaders in sustainability within their sectors, rewarding good corporate behavior with investor capital.

**Real-life applications:**
- A fund selecting only the top-rated ESG performers in each industry
- An investor overweighting companies with high renewable energy usage
- A portfolio manager favoring firms with above-average gender diversity on their boards
- An index provider creating a best-in-class ESG benchmark

**Key concepts:** `positive screening`, `best-in-class`, `ESG leaders`, `active selection`, `sustainability performance`

---

## Q27. What is corporate governance?

Corporate governance refers to the system of rules, practices, and processes by which a company is directed and controlled. It defines the relationships between a company's board of directors, management, shareholders, and other stakeholders. Good governance ensures accountability, fairness, and transparency in how a company operates and makes decisions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Shareholders voting on board member elections at annual meetings
- Companies publishing codes of conduct and ethics policies
- Audit committees overseeing financial reporting integrity
- Regulators requiring independent directors on company boards

**Key concepts:** `corporate governance`, `board of directors`, `accountability`, `transparency`, `shareholder rights`

## Q28. What is a transition bond?

**Level:** Basic

A transition bond is a debt instrument designed to help companies in high-emitting or hard-to-abate industries finance their shift toward lower-carbon operations. Unlike green bonds, which fund projects that are already environmentally friendly, transition bonds fund the process of becoming more sustainable, such as upgrading industrial equipment or switching to cleaner fuels.

**Real-life applications:**
- A steel company issuing a transition bond to fund hydrogen-based production technology
- An airline raising capital for sustainable aviation fuel adoption
- A cement producer financing carbon capture installation through a transition bond
- Investors seeking exposure to companies actively decarbonizing their operations

**Key concepts:** `transition bond`, `hard-to-abate`, `decarbonization`, `industrial transition`, `clean technology`

- Investors comparing companies globally using a common disclosure baseline

## Q30. What is scenario analysis in climate risk?

**Level:** Basic

Scenario analysis is a method used to evaluate the potential financial impact of different climate futures on a company, portfolio, or economy. It typically models outcomes under multiple pathways — such as a 1.5-degree, 2-degree, or 3-degree warming scenario — to help decision-makers understand the range of possible risks and opportunities.

**Real-life applications:**
- Banks stress-testing loan portfolios under different warming scenarios
- Insurers modeling future claims under various sea-level-rise projections
- Companies using TCFD-aligned scenarios to inform their strategic planning
- Regulators requiring financial institutions to conduct climate scenario analysis

**Key concepts:** `scenario analysis`, `climate pathways`, `stress testing`, `TCFD`, `risk assessment`

---

## Q31. What is the Paris Agreement?

**Level:** Basic

The Paris Agreement is an international treaty adopted in 2015 under which nearly every nation committed to limit global warming to well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, with efforts to limit it to 1.5 degrees. It serves as a foundational reference point for sustainable finance, shaping climate targets, regulations, and investment strategies worldwide.

**Real-life applications:**
- Countries setting nationally determined contributions to reduce emissions
- Companies aligning their climate strategies with Paris Agreement temperature goals
- Investors using Paris-aligned benchmarks to construct portfolios
- Financial institutions committing to net-zero financed emissions by 2050

**Key concepts:** `Paris Agreement`, `temperature target`, `1.5 degrees`, `net zero`, `international treaty`

## Q33. What does net zero mean?

**Level:** Basic

Net zero means achieving a balance between the greenhouse gases emitted into the atmosphere and those removed from it. A company or country reaches net zero when its remaining emissions are fully offset by removal activities such as reforestation, direct air capture, or carbon sequestration. It does not mean zero emissions, but rather that any residual emissions are neutralized.

**Real-life applications:**
- Companies setting net-zero targets for their operations by 2050
- Financial institutions committing to net-zero financed emissions
- Governments legislating net-zero goals into national climate laws
- Investors using net-zero frameworks to guide portfolio decarbonization

**Key concepts:** `net zero`, `carbon neutrality`, `emissions removal`, `carbon sequestration`, `climate target`

---

## Q34. What is ESG integration?

**Level:** Basic

ESG integration is the systematic inclusion of environmental, social, and governance factors into traditional financial analysis and investment decision-making. Unlike screening approaches that simply include or exclude companies, ESG integration adjusts valuations, risk assessments, and forecasts based on how ESG factors are likely to affect financial performance.

**Real-life applications:**
- An equity analyst factoring water scarcity risk into a beverage company's valuation model
- A credit analyst adjusting a borrower's risk rating based on governance quality
- A portfolio manager incorporating carbon pricing scenarios into earnings forecasts
- An asset owner requiring all managers to demonstrate ESG integration in their process

**Key concepts:** `ESG integration`, `financial analysis`, `risk-adjusted return`, `valuation`, `systematic inclusion`

---

## Q35. What is the CSRD?

**Level:** Basic

The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) is an EU directive that requires large companies and listed small and medium enterprises to report detailed sustainability information using European Sustainability Reporting Standards. It significantly expands the scope and depth of mandatory sustainability disclosure compared to its predecessor, the Non-Financial Reporting Directive.

**Real-life applications:**
- Large EU companies preparing detailed sustainability reports under CSRD requirements
- Auditors providing assurance on CSRD-mandated disclosures
- Non-EU companies with significant EU operations assessing their CSRD obligations
- Reporting teams mapping existing ESG data to European Sustainability Reporting Standards

**Key concepts:** `CSRD`, `EU directive`, `mandatory disclosure`, `European Sustainability Reporting Standards`, `assurance`

---

## Q36. What is a climate stress test?

**Level:** Basic

A climate stress test is a regulatory exercise that evaluates how a financial institution would perform under various adverse climate scenarios. Similar to traditional financial stress tests, it models the impact of physical and transition risks on a bank's assets, lending portfolio, and capital adequacy to identify vulnerabilities and ensure resilience.

**Real-life applications:**
- Central banks requiring major banks to run climate stress tests annually
- Insurers modeling the impact of extreme weather on their claims reserves
- Banks identifying which loan sectors are most vulnerable to carbon pricing
- Regulators using stress test results to set climate-related capital requirements

**Key concepts:** `climate stress test`, `financial resilience`, `capital adequacy`, `regulatory exercise`, `risk modeling`

---

## Q37. What is blended finance?

Blended finance is an approach that uses catalytic capital from public or philanthropic sources to reduce risk and attract private investment into projects that deliver both financial returns and positive development outcomes. It is commonly used to fund projects in emerging markets or sectors where perceived risk would otherwise deter private investors.

**Real-life applications:**
- Development finance institutions providing first-loss capital to attract private investors
- Philanthropic foundations guaranteeing loans to de-risk renewable energy projects in developing countries
- Multilateral banks co-investing with private equity funds in climate adaptation infrastructure
- Governments offering concessional financing to crowd in private capital for clean water projects

**Key concepts:** `blended finance`, `catalytic capital`, `de-risking`, `private capital mobilization`, `development outcomes`

## Q41. What is the CDP?

It collects data on climate change, water security, and deforestation through annual questionnaires and scores respondents on the quality and completeness of their disclosures.

- Companies completing CDP questionnaires to disclose emissions and water usage data
- Investors using CDP scores to assess environmental risk in their portfolios
- Cities reporting climate actions through CDP to attract green investment

## Q42. What is norms-based screening?

**Real-life applications:**

- An asset manager screening out companies involved in child labor violations
- A sovereign wealth fund using norms-based screening to enforce human rights standards

## Q43. What is the difference between green bonds and sustainability bonds?

**Level:** Basic

Green bonds fund projects with exclusively environmental benefits, while sustainability bonds fund projects that combine both environmental and social benefits. A sustainability bond might finance a project that provides affordable clean energy to low-income communities, addressing both the environmental goal of reducing emissions and the social goal of energy access equity.

**Real-life applications:**
- A development bank issuing a sustainability bond for clean water and sanitation in rural areas
- An issuer choosing between green and sustainability bond labels based on project mix
- Investors building portfolios that include both green and sustainability bonds for diversified impact
- Rating agencies evaluating whether bond proceeds qualify as green, social, or sustainability

**Key concepts:** `green bond`, `sustainability bond`, `social bond`, `use of proceeds`, `dual benefit`

- A tech company purchasing voluntary credits to claim carbon-neutral operations
- A coffee brand buying credits from agroforestry projects to offset supply chain emissions
- A credit standard body like Verra or Gold Standard certifying voluntary market projects

## Q45. What is stakeholder capitalism?

**Level:** Basic

Stakeholder capitalism is a model of corporate governance that holds that companies should serve the interests of all stakeholders — including employees, customers, suppliers, communities, and the environment — not only shareholders. It contrasts with shareholder primacy, which focuses solely on maximizing financial returns for equity owners.

**Real-life applications:**
- Companies publishing stakeholder impact reports alongside financial statements
- Business roundtables issuing statements on the purpose of a corporation beyond profit
- Executives balancing shareholder returns with employee welfare and community impact
- Investors supporting companies that adopt stakeholder-oriented governance models

**Key concepts:** `stakeholder capitalism`, `shareholder primacy`, `stakeholder theory`, `corporate purpose`, `inclusive governance`

## Q46. What is ESG-linked compensation?

**Level:** Basic

ESG-linked compensation ties a portion of executive pay — such as bonuses or long-term incentive plans — to the achievement of specific environmental, social, or governance targets. This approach aligns management incentives with sustainability goals, ensuring that executives are financially rewarded for delivering on ESG commitments.

**Real-life applications:**
- A CEO receiving a bonus based partly on emissions reduction milestones
- A board linking executive pay to employee safety incident rates
- A company tying long-term share awards to diversity hiring targets
- Proxy advisors evaluating the quality and rigor of ESG-linked pay plans

**Key concepts:** `ESG-linked compensation`, `executive incentives`, `performance targets`, `alignment`, `accountability`

---

## Q47. What is board diversity?

**Level:** Basic

Board diversity refers to the inclusion of directors with varied backgrounds, skills, gender, ethnicity, age, and perspectives on a company's board. Diverse boards are associated with better decision-making, improved risk oversight, and stronger financial performance because they bring a wider range of viewpoints to strategic discussions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Stock exchanges requiring companies to disclose board diversity statistics
- Investors voting against all-male or non-diverse board slates
- Companies setting targets for gender and ethnic representation on their boards
- Regulators introducing quotas for board gender balance in certain jurisdictions

**Key concepts:** `board diversity`, `gender balance`, `inclusive governance`, `decision quality`, `representation`

## Q48. What is a Say on Climate vote?

**Level:** Basic

A Say on Climate vote is a shareholder resolution that gives investors the opportunity to vote on a company's climate transition plan at its annual general meeting. It is designed to increase accountability by letting shareholders express approval or disapproval of the company's strategy for managing climate risk and reducing emissions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Oil and gas companies putting their net-zero plans to a shareholder vote
- Activist investors proposing Say on Climate resolutions at annual meetings
- Companies strengthening their climate plans in response to low shareholder approval
- Proxy advisors issuing recommendations on Say on Climate resolutions

**Key concepts:** `Say on Climate`, `shareholder vote`, `climate transition plan`, `accountability`, `annual general meeting`

## Q49. What is the difference between ESG integration and SRI?

**Level:** Basic

ESG integration incorporates environmental, social, and governance factors into financial analysis to improve risk-adjusted returns without necessarily excluding any sector or company. SRI goes further by applying ethical or values-based criteria to include or exclude investments, even if doing so means avoiding financially attractive opportunities that conflict with the investor's principles.

**Real-life applications:**
- An ESG integration approach adjusting a company's valuation based on carbon risk without excluding the sector
- An SRI approach excluding all tobacco companies regardless of their financial attractiveness
- A fund offering both ESG-integrated and SRI strategies to different client segments
- Advisors explaining the distinction to clients when building customized portfolios

**Key concepts:** `ESG integration`, `SRI`, `values-based investing`, `risk-adjusted returns`, `exclusion`

## Q50. What is science-based target setting?

**Level:** Basic

Science-based target setting is the process of establishing greenhouse gas emissions reduction goals that align with the level of decarbonization required to meet the Paris Agreement's temperature objectives. The Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi) validates corporate targets to ensure they are ambitious enough to contribute to limiting global warming.

**Real-life applications:**
- Companies submitting emissions reduction targets to SBTi for validation
- Investors preferring companies with validated science-based targets
- Supply chain programs requiring key suppliers to set science-based targets
- Financial institutions setting portfolio-level science-based targets for financed emissions

**Key concepts:** `science-based targets`, `SBTi`, `Paris alignment`, `emissions reduction`, `target validation`

---

## Q51. What is sustainable supply chain finance?

**Level:** Basic

Sustainable supply chain finance integrates ESG criteria into the financing arrangements between buyers, suppliers, and banks across a supply chain. Suppliers that meet sustainability targets — such as reducing emissions or improving labor conditions — receive better financing terms, creating a financial incentive to adopt more responsible practices throughout the value chain.

**Real-life applications:**
- A fashion brand offering better payment terms to factories with fair labor certifications
- A bank adjusting invoice financing rates based on supplier sustainability scores
- A food company incentivizing organic farming through preferential supply chain finance
- A tech firm linking supplier financing to conflict-free mineral sourcing compliance

**Key concepts:** `sustainable SCF`, `ESG criteria`, `supplier incentives`, `value chain`, `responsible sourcing`

## Q52. What is traceability in supply chains?

**Level:** Basic

Traceability is the ability to track and verify the origin, journey, and conditions under which a product or material was produced, processed, and transported throughout the supply chain. In sustainable finance, traceability helps ensure that sustainability claims — such as deforestation-free sourcing or ethical labor — can be independently verified.

**Real-life applications:**
- A chocolate company using blockchain to trace cocoa beans from farm to factory
- A jeweler verifying the origin of diamonds through a chain-of-custody system
- A retailer publishing supply chain maps showing the source of its cotton
- A bank requiring traceability data before approving sustainability-linked loans

**Key concepts:** `traceability`, `supply chain transparency`, `blockchain`, `chain of custody`, `verification`

## Q53. What is the difference between compliance and voluntary carbon markets?

**Level:** Basic

Compliance carbon markets are created by government regulations that require certain companies to hold emission allowances and stay within mandated caps. Voluntary carbon markets allow companies and individuals to buy carbon credits without a legal requirement, typically to meet self-imposed sustainability goals or corporate net-zero commitments.

**Real-life applications:**
- Power plants buying allowances under the EU ETS to meet regulatory obligations
- A consumer brand purchasing voluntary credits to offset product lifecycle emissions
- A project developer selling credits to both compliance and voluntary buyers
- A broker facilitating carbon credit trades across both market types

**Key concepts:** `compliance market`, `voluntary market`, `emission allowance`, `carbon credit`, `regulatory requirement`

---

## Q54. What is additionality in carbon markets?

**Level:** Basic

Additionality means that the emissions reductions achieved by a carbon credit project would not have occurred without the revenue generated from selling carbon credits. It is a critical quality criterion because credits that fund projects that would have happened anyway do not represent genuine additional reductions in greenhouse gases.

**Real-life applications:**
- A certification body verifying that a wind farm would not have been built without credit revenue
- Buyers rejecting credits from projects that were already profitable without carbon finance
- Standard-setting organizations tightening additionality requirements to improve market integrity
- Auditors assessing whether a reforestation project is additional or business-as-usual

**Key concepts:** `additionality`, `carbon credit quality`, `baseline scenario`, `project finance`, `market integrity`

---

## Q55. What is a green loan?

**Level:** Basic

A green loan is a credit facility whose proceeds are designated for use in environmentally beneficial projects, similar to a green bond but in loan form. The borrower commits to using the funds for eligible green purposes such as energy efficiency upgrades, renewable energy installation, or pollution control, and typically reports on how the proceeds are spent.

**Real-life applications:**
- A small business taking out a green loan to install rooftop solar panels
- A hospital borrowing through a green loan to upgrade to energy-efficient HVAC systems
- A property developer financing a green building certification through a green loan
- A bank creating a dedicated green loan product line for environmentally focused borrowers

**Key concepts:** `green loan`, `use of proceeds`, `eligible project`, `environmental benefit`, `loan reporting`

## Q56. What is the carbon price?

**Level:** Basic

The carbon price is the cost assigned to emitting one metric ton of carbon dioxide equivalent into the atmosphere. It can be determined by market trading in an emissions trading system or set directly by a government through a carbon tax. A higher carbon price increases the financial incentive for businesses to reduce their emissions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Companies factoring the carbon price into investment decisions for new projects
- Governments setting carbon tax rates to discourage fossil fuel consumption
- Traders buying and selling carbon allowances at market-determined prices
- Economists modeling the social cost of carbon to inform policy

**Key concepts:** `carbon price`, `carbon tax`, `emissions trading`, `cost per ton`, `financial incentive`

---

## Q57. What is financed emissions?

**Level:** Basic

Financed emissions are the greenhouse gas emissions associated with a financial institution's lending and investment activities. They represent the portion of a borrower's or investee's emissions that can be attributed to the bank, insurer, or asset manager that provides the financing. Measuring financed emissions helps financial institutions understand their indirect climate impact.

**Real-life applications:**
- A bank calculating the total emissions of companies in its loan portfolio
- An asset manager reporting the carbon footprint of its equity holdings
- A financial institution setting targets to reduce financed emissions over time
- Industry alliances like the Partnership for Carbon Accounting Financials standardizing measurement methods

**Key concepts:** `financed emissions`, `portfolio carbon footprint`, `PCAF`, `indirect emissions`, `financial institution`

## Q58. What is an ESG score?

**Level:** Basic

An ESG score is a numerical rating that summarizes a company's performance across environmental, social, and governance dimensions. Rating agencies such as MSCI, Sustainalytics, and S&P Global assign these scores based on publicly available data, company disclosures, and proprietary methodologies to help investors quickly assess ESG risk and performance.

**Real-life applications:**
- Investors filtering investment opportunities by minimum ESG score thresholds
- Index providers constructing ESG-weighted benchmarks
- Companies tracking their ESG scores and engaging with rating agencies to improve
- Asset managers using ESG scores as one input in their investment process

**Key concepts:** `ESG score`, `rating agency`, `MSCI`, `Sustainalytics`, `performance assessment`

## Q59. What is the UN Global Compact?

**Level:** Basic

The UN Global Compact is a voluntary initiative in which companies commit to aligning their strategies and operations with ten universal principles covering human rights, labor, environment, and anti-corruption. It is the world's largest corporate sustainability initiative and serves as a reference standard for norms-based screening in investment.

**Real-life applications:**
- Companies signing onto the UN Global Compact and publishing annual progress reports
- Investors screening out companies that violate Global Compact principles
- Supply chain auditors using Global Compact principles as a compliance benchmark
- NGOs monitoring whether signatory companies uphold their commitments

**Key concepts:** `UN Global Compact`, `ten principles`, `voluntary commitment`, `human rights`, `anti-corruption`

## Q60. What is the SASB framework?

**Level:** Basic

The Sustainability Accounting Standards Board (SASB) framework identifies the ESG issues most likely to affect the financial condition or operating performance of companies within 77 specific industries. Unlike GRI, which focuses on broad stakeholder reporting, SASB is designed for investor-focused disclosure of financially material sustainability information.

**Real-life applications:**
- A technology company using SASB standards to identify material ESG topics for its industry
- Investors comparing companies within the same industry using SASB-aligned disclosures
- Companies mapping their existing reporting to SASB topics and metrics
- Analysts incorporating SASB-identified material issues into financial models

**Key concepts:** `SASB`, `industry-specific`, `financial materiality`, `investor disclosure`, `material ESG issues`

## Q61. What is a social bond?

**Level:** Basic

A social bond is a fixed-income instrument whose proceeds are exclusively used to fund projects with positive social outcomes, such as affordable housing, healthcare access, education, or food security. Like green bonds, social bonds follow use-of-proceeds principles but focus on addressing social challenges rather than environmental ones.

**Real-life applications:**
- A government issuing a social bond to fund affordable housing construction
- A development bank raising capital for healthcare infrastructure in low-income countries
- An investor purchasing social bonds to support education programs for underserved populations
- A corporation issuing social bonds to finance job training and workforce development programs

**Key concepts:** `social bond`, `social outcomes`, `affordable housing`, `use of proceeds`, `fixed income`

## Q62. What is the green asset ratio?

**Level:** Basic

The green asset ratio is a metric used by banks to measure the proportion of their balance sheet that finances activities qualifying as environmentally sustainable under a recognized taxonomy. It is calculated by dividing the value of taxonomy-aligned assets by total covered assets. The ratio helps investors and regulators assess how green a bank's lending and investment activities are.

**Real-life applications:**
- European banks calculating and disclosing their green asset ratio under EU regulations
- Investors comparing banks based on their green asset ratios
- Bank management teams setting targets to increase taxonomy-aligned lending
- Regulators using the green asset ratio to monitor the greening of the financial sector

**Key concepts:** `green asset ratio`, `taxonomy alignment`, `bank lending`, `balance sheet`, `sustainable assets`

## Q63. What is a materiality assessment?

**Level:** Basic

A materiality assessment is a process through which a company identifies and prioritizes the ESG topics that are most significant to its business and stakeholders. The resulting materiality matrix helps the company focus its sustainability strategy, reporting, and resource allocation on the issues that matter most rather than trying to address everything equally.

**Real-life applications:**
- A mining company identifying water management and community relations as top material topics
- A technology firm determining that data privacy and energy consumption are its most material ESG issues
- Sustainability teams surveying stakeholders to rank ESG topics by importance
- Reporting teams using materiality assessments to decide what to include in sustainability reports

**Key concepts:** `materiality assessment`, `materiality matrix`, `stakeholder input`, `ESG prioritization`, `strategic focus`

## Q64. What is a carbon footprint?

**Level:** Basic

A carbon footprint is the total amount of greenhouse gases produced directly and indirectly by an individual, organization, event, or product, expressed in metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent. Measuring a carbon footprint is the first step in understanding and reducing climate impact.

**Real-life applications:**
- Companies calculating the carbon footprint of their products for labeling purposes
- Individuals using online calculators to estimate their personal carbon footprint
- Organizations measuring their organizational footprint across all operations
- Supply chain managers assessing the carbon footprint of each tier of suppliers

**Key concepts:** `carbon footprint`, `CO2 equivalent`, `measurement`, `direct emissions`, `indirect emissions`

## Q65. What does Article 8 mean under SFDR?

**Level:** Basic

Under the EU's Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation, Article 8 applies to financial products that promote environmental or social characteristics, provided the companies in which investments are made follow good governance practices. These are often called "light green" funds and must disclose how they promote those characteristics.

**Real-life applications:**
- A fund marketing itself as Article 8 because it promotes low-carbon characteristics
- Investors selecting Article 8 funds to align portfolios with environmental preferences
- Compliance teams preparing the required disclosures for Article 8 products
- Asset managers reclassifying funds between Article 6 and Article 8 based on strategy updates

**Key concepts:** `Article 8`, `SFDR`, `light green fund`, `environmental characteristics`, `promotion`

## Q66. What does Article 9 mean under SFDR?

**Level:** Basic

Under the SFDR, Article 9 applies to financial products that have sustainable investment as their core objective. These are often called "dark green" funds and must demonstrate that every investment in the portfolio contributes to a specific environmental or social objective while doing no significant harm to other sustainability objectives.

**Real-life applications:**
- A climate-focused fund classified as Article 9 because its sole objective is reducing emissions
- Investors seeking Article 9 funds for the highest level of sustainability commitment
- Fund managers documenting how each holding contributes to the stated sustainable objective
- Regulators auditing Article 9 funds to verify that claims match actual portfolio composition

**Key concepts:** `Article 9`, `SFDR`, `dark green fund`, `sustainable investment objective`, `do no significant harm`

## Q67. What is a sustainability report?

**Level:** Basic

A sustainability report is a document published by a company that discloses its performance, activities, and impacts across environmental, social, and governance dimensions. It may be standalone or integrated with the annual financial report. Sustainability reports serve as the primary vehicle through which companies communicate their ESG progress to investors, regulators, and the public.

**Real-life applications:**
- A listed company publishing an annual sustainability report on its website
- Investors reviewing sustainability reports before making allocation decisions
- Rating agencies using sustainability reports as a data source for ESG scoring
- Regulators requiring companies to file sustainability reports alongside financial statements

**Key concepts:** `sustainability report`, `ESG disclosure`, `annual report`, `stakeholder communication`, `performance tracking`

## Q68. What is assurance in ESG reporting?

**Level:** Basic

Assurance in ESG reporting is the process of having an independent third party verify the accuracy, completeness, and reliability of a company's sustainability disclosures. It functions similarly to a financial audit and can be provided at a limited or reasonable level, with reasonable assurance representing a higher degree of confidence.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company hiring an external auditor to provide limited assurance on its emissions data
- Regulators requiring mandatory assurance for sustainability reports under the CSRD
- Investors giving more weight to ESG data that has been independently assured
- Audit firms expanding their services to include sustainability assurance

**Key concepts:** `assurance`, `independent verification`, `limited assurance`, `reasonable assurance`, `data reliability`

---

## Q69. What is the purpose of a green bond framework?

**Level:** Basic

A green bond framework is a document prepared by an issuer that outlines how a green bond will comply with recognized standards such as the Green Bond Principles. It describes the types of projects that will be funded, the criteria for project selection, how proceeds will be tracked, and what reporting will be provided to investors.

**Real-life applications:**
- An issuer publishing its green bond framework before launching a bond offering
- Investors reviewing the framework to assess the credibility of the green bond
- Second-party opinion providers evaluating the framework against the Green Bond Principles
- Regulators referencing frameworks when assessing green bond market integrity

**Key concepts:** `green bond framework`, `project eligibility`, `proceeds management`, `investor reporting`, `Green Bond Principles`

---

## Q70. What is the difference between carbon neutrality and net zero?

**Level:** Basic

Carbon neutrality means balancing carbon dioxide emissions with offsets, often through purchasing carbon credits, without necessarily requiring absolute emission reductions. Net zero is a more rigorous concept that requires deep cuts in emissions across all scopes first, with offsets used only for truly unavoidable residual emissions. Net zero demands systemic change, while carbon neutrality can be achieved primarily through offsetting.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company claiming carbon neutrality by buying credits without reducing its own emissions
- A corporation pursuing net zero by decarbonizing operations and only offsetting residual emissions
- Standard-setting bodies establishing rules to distinguish between the two claims
- Consumers and investors scrutinizing whether a company is truly net zero or just carbon neutral

**Key concepts:** `carbon neutrality`, `net zero`, `emissions reduction`, `carbon offsets`, `residual emissions`

## 4. Formulas
Formula: `green_asset_ratio = taxonomy_aligned_assets / covered_assets` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
taxonomy_aligned_assets = assets that meet the taxonomy definition
covered_assets = total assets subject to the ratio
Why this formula exists: It answers how much of a balance sheet or portfolio is actually aligned with sustainable activity.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 10% → low alignment; review pipeline and criteria
Value 10%–50% → mixed alignment; explain the gap
Value > 50% → strong alignment; maintain and verify
Worked example with numbers: If a bank has 18 aligned units out of 100 covered units, the green asset ratio is 18%.

Formula: `financed_emissions = issuer_emissions × attribution_share` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
issuer_emissions = the company’s total emissions
attribution_share = the portion attributable to the lender or investor
Why this formula exists: It attributes part of a company’s emissions to the financial institution backing it.
How to interpret the output:
Value falls → portfolio climate exposure improves
Value flat → no real change yet
Value rises → climate exposure is worsening
Worked example with numbers: If a portfolio attributes 1,000 tonnes of emissions at 10% share, financed emissions are 100 tonnes.

Formula: `carbon_footprint = total_emissions / portfolio_value` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
total_emissions = aggregate emissions tied to the portfolio
portfolio_value = total invested value
Why this formula exists: It normalizes emissions so portfolios can be compared.
How to interpret the output:
Value low → cleaner portfolio
Value medium → monitor and compare
Value high → shift allocation or engage holdings
Worked example with numbers: A $2 million portfolio tied to 400 tonnes gives 0.0002 tonnes per dollar, or 200 tonnes per million dollars.

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: Cloud infrastructure financing
Situation: An AI company wants a sustainability-linked loan for a new data center while also claiming taxonomy alignment on part of the buildout. The consulting team needs to decide whether the capital stack is credible.
Applicable framework/metric: Sustainability-linked loan, green asset ratio, financed emissions.
Analysis: If the green asset ratio is weak and financed emissions stay flat, the deal is more label than transition. If emissions intensity drops and reporting is assured, the structure is defensible.
Decision rule: If targets and verification are present, proceed. If targets exist but are untested, stage the capital. If the claim is mostly marketing, reject the label.

Scenario 2: SaaS vendor and disclosure choice

Scenario 3: Consulting client with supply-chain exposure
Situation: A consulting firm is advising a hardware manufacturer that has strong revenue but weak supplier traceability. The client wants to issue a green loan and publish a sustainability report.
Applicable framework/metric: Supply chain finance, traceability, ESG reporting, assurance.
