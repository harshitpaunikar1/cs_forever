# ESG Criteria and Frameworks

## Overview
ESG criteria are a set of standards used by investors and companies to evaluate performance across environmental, social, and governance dimensions. Frameworks such as GRI, SASB, and TCFD provide structured guidelines for measuring, reporting, and comparing ESG performance. These frameworks help standardize what can otherwise be subjective assessments, enabling stakeholders to make informed decisions. Understanding ESG criteria is foundational for any professional working in sustainable finance.

---

## Why It Matters
Without consistent criteria and frameworks, companies could selectively disclose favorable data while hiding poor performance — a practice known as greenwashing. Robust ESG frameworks create a common language that allows investors to compare companies, regulators to enforce standards, and businesses to identify material risks and opportunities they might otherwise overlook.

## Key Principles
- Materiality — focusing on ESG factors that have a genuine financial or societal impact for a given industry
- Comparability — using standardized metrics so stakeholders can benchmark across companies and sectors
- Stakeholder inclusivity — considering the needs and concerns of all affected parties, not just shareholders
- Continuous improvement — treating ESG performance as a dynamic process that evolves with new data and expectations

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **GRI (Global Reporting Initiative)** | An international framework that provides standards for sustainability reporting across economic, environmental, and social topics |
| **SASB (Sustainability Accounting Standards Board)** | A framework that identifies financially material ESG issues for 77 industries and provides standardized disclosure topics |
| **TCFD (Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures)** | A framework focused on climate-related risks and opportunities, organized around governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics |
| **Greenwashing** | The practice of making misleading claims about the environmental or social benefits of a product, service, or company |

## Use Case
An asset manager uses SASB standards to compare water-use efficiency across ten semiconductor manufacturers, identifying which companies face the highest material risk from water scarcity before making allocation decisions.

## Scenario
> A global consumer goods company prepares its first integrated sustainability report using GRI standards. During the process, the team discovers that its largest factory has significantly higher waste-to-landfill ratios than industry peers. The disclosure prompts the board to approve a waste-reduction program that saves the company millions in disposal costs within two years.

## Examples
- An institutional investor requiring all portfolio companies to report using TCFD-aligned climate disclosures
- A credit rating agency incorporating ESG scores from multiple frameworks into its sovereign debt assessments

## 1. Topic Snapshot
ESG criteria are the rule set for deciding whether a company is performing well on environmental, social, and governance dimensions.  
For an IT/AI/Product/Consulting leader, the point is not "nice reporting" but whether the business can prove material risk control and avoid greenwashing.  
It helps decide what to report, how to compare peers, and where sustainability issues change capital allocation or board action.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### 1) ESG Reporting Stack
**Purpose:** Use the right framework for the right disclosure question.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
GRI = broad sustainability disclosure
SASB = financially material sector disclosure
TCFD = climate-risk structure
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: GRI - broad topic coverage across economic, environmental, and social areas.
Component 2: SASB - sector-specific financially material issues.
Component 3: TCFD - climate governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A cloud-infrastructure company uses GRI for the annual report, SASB for investor due diligence, and TCFD for lender-facing climate risk disclosure.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When stakeholders are asking for ESG data but mean different things by "reporting."

### 2) Materiality Lens
**Purpose:** Separate real business risk from reporting noise.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
High financial impact + high stakeholder concern = material
Low impact + low concern = low priority
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Financial impact - whether value, cost, or risk moves.
Component 2: Societal/stakeholder impact - whether external parties care or are harmed.
Component 3: Priority setting - what goes into the report and the action plan.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A product company sees water use as material because its AI chip suppliers operate in water-stressed regions.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team wants to report everything and the board wants only what matters.

### 3) Greenwashing Control Loop
**Purpose:** Stop disclosure from becoming marketing.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
claim -> evidence -> framework alignment -> review -> disclosure
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Claim quality - what the company says.
Component 2: Evidence quality - what can be proven.
Component 3: Framework alignment - whether GRI, SASB, or TCFD supports the claim.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A consulting firm cannot claim "carbon neutral operations" unless it can reconcile offsets, energy use, and verified emissions data.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a sustainability draft sounds good but the evidence trail is weak.

### 4) Climate Risk Structure
**Purpose:** Make climate risk decision-useful instead of vague.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
governance -> strategy -> risk management -> metrics
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Governance - who owns climate risk.
Component 2: Strategy - how climate changes the business model.
Component 3: Risk management and metrics - how exposure and progress are tracked.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] An AI data-center operator links heat, water, and power risks to site strategy and monthly metrics.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When climate disclosure needs to map to actual board control and capital planning.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: `ESG priority score = materiality x comparability x stakeholder impact` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
Materiality = how much the issue matters financially or societally.
Comparability = how well the metric can be benchmarked.
Stakeholder impact = how many affected parties care.
Why this formula exists: It answers what should be disclosed and managed first.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> low reporting priority.
Value medium -> disclose, monitor, and benchmark.
Value high -> make it a board-level KPI.
Worked example with numbers: [verified from model knowledge, not source] If a chip plant's water issue scores 5 x 4 x 5 = 100, it is likely a top-tier ESG topic.

Formula: `Greenwashing risk score = claims / evidence quality` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
Claims = number or strength of sustainability assertions.
Evidence quality = how much proof, assurance, and consistency exists.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the story is outrunning the data.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> defensible disclosure.
Value medium -> tighten wording and collect more evidence.
Value high -> pause the claim until the data is real.
Worked example with numbers: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A consulting firm with bold net-zero claims but weak measurement gets a high risk score and should downgrade the language.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| Don't | Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat ESG as a PR exercise | Use GRI, SASB, and TCFD to make disclosures decision-useful |
| Report every metric with equal weight | Apply materiality so the board sees what truly matters |
| Compare companies using inconsistent definitions | Use standardized metrics for comparability |
| Make climate claims without evidence | Check for greenwashing and force an evidence trail |
| Ignore stakeholder concerns outside shareholders | Use stakeholder inclusivity and continuous improvement |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI data-center water disclosure
Situation: [verified from model knowledge, not source] An AI infrastructure company is preparing a lender pack and discovers its cooling system is in a water-stressed region. The board wants to know whether this is a reporting issue or a material risk.
Applicable framework/metric: Materiality lens + water-use efficiency.
Analysis: If water-use efficiency is materially worse than peers and local water scarcity is high, the issue belongs in the top-tier ESG disclosure set.
Decision rule: If the issue is material and worsening, disclose and remediate. If material but stable, disclose and monitor. If not material, keep it in the operating dashboard.
Action: Benchmark the plant, create a water-reduction plan, and disclose it in the climate report.

Scenario 2: Consumer goods sustainability report
Situation: The source example describes a global consumer goods company publishing its first integrated sustainability report using GRI. During the process, the team discovers a factory with above-peer waste-to-landfill ratios.
Applicable framework/metric: GRI + comparability.
Analysis: The peer gap makes the waste metric comparable and material enough for board action. The disclosure turns a hidden operational issue into a cost-saving program.
Decision rule: If the metric is far worse than peers, launch a remediation program. If it is near peer median, set a tighter target. If it is already best-in-class, maintain the control.
Action: Publish the metric, assign an owner, and fund waste reduction.

Scenario 3: Portfolio ESG screening for a consulting or investment team
Situation: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A consulting team helping an asset manager must compare ten portfolio companies using one climate lens. The team also needs a format the credit committee will trust.
Applicable framework/metric: SASB + TCFD + ESG scores.
Analysis: Sector-specific materiality from SASB is the cleanest basis for comparison, while TCFD handles climate-risk structure. If vendor ESG scores conflict with disclosed evidence, the team should rely on the company report and audit trail.
Decision rule: If score and disclosure agree, use the rating. If they conflict, investigate. If the company cannot explain the gap, downgrade confidence.
Action: Rebuild the screening pack around material, auditable metrics.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build a materiality map for the sector before choosing metrics.
2. Pick GRI, SASB, or TCFD based on the audience and decision question.
3. Create one source of truth for ESG data so disclosure does not drift.
4. Compare each KPI against peers to test comparability.
5. Add evidence links, owner names, and assurance notes to every public claim.
6. Review the report for greenwashing language before publication.
7. Tie the final ESG dashboard to board and investor review cycles.
