# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and core concepts in strategic pricing. Questions 1 through 70.

## Q1. What is a pricing method?

**Level:** Basic

A pricing method is the rule or formula a business uses to set the selling price of a product or service. Common methods include cost-plus, value-based, competition-based, and psychological pricing. Each method approaches the price decision from a different angle — cost, customer value, rivals, or buyer perception.

**Real-life applications:**
- Retailers pick cost-plus for steady commodity goods.
- Software firms use value-based pricing for premium tools.
- Airlines blend competition-based and dynamic methods.
- Startups test several methods before committing.

**Key concepts:** `pricing method`, `cost-plus`, `value-based`, `competition-based`, `price setting`

---

## Q2. Why is pricing analytics important?

**Level:** Basic

Pricing analytics uses historical data to check whether prices are winning deals, holding margin, and beating rivals. Without it, managers rely on gut feel and miss both risks and opportunities. Good analytics turn pricing from art into a measurable process.

**Real-life applications:**
- Supermarkets analyze weekly promo impact on margin.
- SaaS teams study churn versus price changes.
- Manufacturers review discount leakage each quarter.
- Marketplaces use analytics to price thousands of SKUs.

**Key concepts:** `pricing analytics`, `data-driven`, `margin`, `promotion analysis`, `decision making`

---

## Q3. What does cost-plus pricing mean?

**Level:** Basic

Cost-plus pricing sets the price by adding a fixed markup on top of total cost. If a product costs $10 to make and the markup is 40%, the price is $14. It is simple, transparent, and easy to defend inside a company.

**Real-life applications:**
- Government contractors use cost-plus for large projects.
- Construction firms quote based on material and labor plus markup.
- Small manufacturers price by cost and target margin.
- Retailers price basic grocery items with fixed markups.

**Key concepts:** `cost-plus`, `markup`, `total cost`, `margin`, `pricing formula`

---

## Q4. What is value-based pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Value-based pricing sets the price based on what a customer thinks the product is worth to them. It ignores cost and focuses on the benefit delivered — time saved, revenue gained, or pain avoided. Prices can be far higher than cost-plus would suggest.

**Real-life applications:**
- Enterprise software charges on ROI delivered.
- Medical devices price on patient outcomes.
- Luxury brands price on aspiration and experience.
- Consultants price by project value, not hours.

**Key concepts:** `value-based pricing`, `willingness to pay`, `customer value`, `ROI`, `benefit`

---

## Q5. What is competition-based pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Competition-based pricing sets your price by looking at what rivals charge and choosing to match, undercut, or stay above them. It is common in crowded markets where shoppers compare listings quickly. Rival prices become the reference point.

**Real-life applications:**
- E-commerce sellers auto-match marketplace rivals.
- Gas stations adjust within minutes of neighbors.
- Banks tweak savings rates against top competitors.
- Airlines watch rival fares on shared routes.

**Key concepts:** `competition-based`, `price match`, `undercutting`, `competitive set`, `reference price`

---

## Q6. What is psychological pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Psychological pricing uses the way people read numbers to nudge buying behavior. Prices ending in .99, decoy options, and bundled deals make products feel cheaper or better value. Small framing changes can shift conversion meaningfully.

**Real-life applications:**
- Supermarkets end prices in .99 to feel cheaper.
- Subscriptions show a decoy tier to steer buyers.
- Restaurants drop dollar signs to reduce spending anchors.
- Apps frame prices as "per day" instead of "per year."

**Key concepts:** `psychological pricing`, `charm pricing`, `anchoring`, `decoy`, `framing`

---

## Q7. What is price elasticity?

**Level:** Basic

Price elasticity measures how much demand changes when the price changes. An elasticity of 2 means a 10% price cut lifts sales by 20%. It tells managers whether a product is sensitive or resistant to price moves.

**Real-life applications:**
- Telecoms set data-plan prices using elasticity.
- Retailers pick which items to promote.
- Pharma firms study patient price response.
- Ride-hail apps use elasticity to set surge levels.

**Key concepts:** `elasticity`, `demand`, `price sensitivity`, `quantity`, `percentage change`

---

## Q8. What does dynamic pricing mean?

**Level:** Basic

Dynamic pricing is a system that changes a product's price often, based on demand, supply, and timing. It is common in airlines, hotels, and ride-hailing. The goal is to capture the most each buyer is willing to pay at that moment.

**Real-life applications:**
- Airlines change ticket prices many times a day.
- Ride apps surge fares during peak demand.
- Hotels raise rates as occupancy climbs.
- E-commerce tools re-price products hourly.

**Key concepts:** `dynamic pricing`, `real-time`, `demand-based`, `surge`, `algorithm`

---

## Q9. What is revenue management?

**Level:** Basic

Revenue management is the discipline of selling the right product to the right customer at the right time and price. It is used by businesses with fixed capacity, such as hotels and airlines. The aim is to fill seats or rooms profitably.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hotels manage room rates by season.
- Airlines set fare classes for routes.
- Cruise lines plan pricing 12 months ahead.
- Event venues protect VIP seats from early buyers.

**Key concepts:** `revenue management`, `fixed capacity`, `fare class`, `yield`, `RevPAR`

---

## Q10. What is AI-based predictive pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Predictive pricing uses AI and machine learning to forecast how customers will respond to different prices. Models learn from past sales, promotions, and competitor data, then recommend the best price. They replace manual analysis with faster, broader coverage.

**Real-life applications:**
- Fashion retailers automate markdowns.
- Marketplaces suggest prices to sellers.
- Grocers price thousands of SKUs daily.
- Airlines forecast segment demand.

**Key concepts:** `predictive model`, `machine learning`, `price recommendation`, `AI`, `training data`

---

## Q11. What is new product launch pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Launch pricing is the initial price a firm sets on a new product. The two main strategies are skimming, which starts high and lowers over time, and penetration, which starts low to capture share quickly. The choice depends on competition and goals.

**Real-life applications:**
- Smartphone brands use skimming on flagship launches.
- Streaming services use penetration to build users.
- Startups price low to seed early adoption.
- Pharma firms launch high before generics hit.

**Key concepts:** `launch pricing`, `skimming`, `penetration`, `introduction`, `market entry`

---

## Q12. What is price skimming?

**Level:** Basic

Price skimming is launching a new product at a high price and lowering it over time. It targets early adopters willing to pay a premium, then captures mass-market buyers later. It works best when competition is limited.

**Real-life applications:**
- Gaming consoles launch high and drop annually.
- Luxury EVs use skimming on new models.
- Tech gadgets launch at premium prices.
- Pharma drugs skim before patent expiry.

**Key concepts:** `skimming`, `early adopters`, `premium`, `price decline`, `innovation`

---

## Q13. What is penetration pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Penetration pricing launches a product at a low price to gain market share quickly. Low prices draw mass buyers, trigger word of mouth, and discourage rivals. Prices usually rise later once the brand is established.

**Real-life applications:**
- Streaming services offer cheap intro tiers.
- Grocery chains enter new regions with low prices.
- Apps offer free plans to build users.
- Challenger telcos undercut incumbents.

**Key concepts:** `penetration`, `market share`, `low price`, `mass market`, `entry`

---

## Q14. What are competitor price monitoring tools?

**Level:** Basic

Competitor monitoring tools automatically scrape rival websites to track their prices, stock, and promotions. They replace manual checks with live data across thousands of SKUs. They let teams respond to price moves quickly.

**Real-life applications:**
- Retailers run daily scrapes on rival sites.
- Airlines watch fares across booking sites.
- Hotels track local competitor rates.
- Marketplaces automate repricing rules.

**Key concepts:** `monitoring tools`, `scraping`, `competitor prices`, `repricer`, `alerts`

---

## Q15. What is segment pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Segment pricing charges different groups of customers different prices for the same product. Segments can be based on age, region, volume, or channel. The goal is to capture more value from each group's willingness to pay.

**Real-life applications:**
- Student discounts on streaming apps.
- Senior citizen fares on airlines.
- Bulk pricing for business buyers.
- Off-peak rates for gym memberships.

**Key concepts:** `segment pricing`, `willingness to pay`, `discrimination`, `price fence`, `groups`

---

## Q16. What is B2B pricing?

**Level:** Basic

B2B pricing covers how a business sells to other businesses. It usually involves negotiated contracts, volume tiers, and longer sales cycles. Prices are rarely published and often vary by customer.

**Real-life applications:**
- Software vendors quote enterprise licenses.
- Chemical suppliers respond to RFPs.
- Manufacturers set volume tiers.
- Logistics firms offer multi-year contracts.

**Key concepts:** `B2B`, `negotiation`, `contracts`, `volume tiers`, `quote`

---

## Q17. What is B2C pricing?

**Level:** Basic

B2C pricing is selling directly to consumers at a displayed, fixed price. It covers retail stores, e-commerce, and direct-to-consumer brands. Prices are usually public and change less often than in B2B.

**Real-life applications:**
- Grocery stores post shelf prices.
- Apps show subscription tiers.
- Cars have sticker prices.
- Online retailers show final price at checkout.

**Key concepts:** `B2C`, `retail`, `consumer`, `list price`, `displayed price`

---

## Q18. What is a markup?

**Level:** Basic

Markup is the amount added to cost to set the selling price, usually shown as a percentage. A 50% markup on a $10 cost gives a $15 price. It is the core lever in cost-plus pricing.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hardware stores apply standard markups.
- Restaurants mark up wine by 200%.
- Distributors use tiered markup by volume.
- Fashion retailers mark up 2x to 4x.

**Key concepts:** `markup`, `cost`, `selling price`, `percentage`, `cost-plus`

---

## Q19. What is gross margin?

**Level:** Basic

Gross margin is selling price minus cost of goods, shown as a percentage of price. If a product sells for $100 with a cost of $60, the gross margin is 40%. It is a key health check on product pricing.

**Real-life applications:**
- Retailers track gross margin weekly.
- SaaS firms report gross margin in filings.
- Manufacturers compare margin across SKUs.
- Investors judge firms on gross margin trend.

**Key concepts:** `gross margin`, `COGS`, `selling price`, `profitability`, `percentage`

---

## Q20. What is willingness to pay?

**Level:** Basic

Willingness to pay is the maximum price a customer would accept for a product before walking away. It varies by person, segment, and time. Learning it is the foundation of value-based pricing.

**Real-life applications:**
- Surveys measure WTP before launches.
- A/B tests reveal WTP across pages.
- B2B buyers share WTP through RFPs.
- Negotiations probe WTP directly.

**Key concepts:** `willingness to pay`, `maximum price`, `WTP`, `consumer surplus`, `value`

---

## Q21. What is consumer surplus?

**Level:** Basic

Consumer surplus is the difference between what a buyer was willing to pay and what they actually paid. A customer ready to spend $80 who pays $50 gets $30 of surplus. Sellers aim to capture more of this surplus through pricing.

**Real-life applications:**
- Dynamic pricing reduces surplus at peaks.
- Loyalty programs share surplus back.
- Auctions extract near-full WTP.
- Tiered pricing shifts surplus to segments.

**Key concepts:** `consumer surplus`, `WTP`, `paid price`, `value capture`, `economics`

---

## Q22. What is charm pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Charm pricing is ending prices in .99 or .95 to make them feel cheaper. $9.99 reads as "nine dollars" instead of "ten dollars" in a shopper's mind. It is one of the oldest and most tested tricks in retail.

**Real-life applications:**
- Grocery shelves everywhere.
- Fast fashion price tags.
- Online checkout pages.
- Gas station pump boards.

**Key concepts:** `charm pricing`, `.99`, `left-digit bias`, `perception`, `framing`

---

## Q23. What is price anchoring?

**Level:** Basic

Anchoring is showing a high price first to make later prices feel reasonable. A $200 item next to a $500 item suddenly feels affordable. The first number sets the mental reference.

**Real-life applications:**
- Menus list expensive items first.
- Sales show "was $100, now $60" on tags.
- Real estate brokers tour pricey homes first.
- SaaS shows enterprise tier before mid tier.

**Key concepts:** `anchoring`, `reference price`, `perception`, `framing`, `comparison`

---

## Q24. What is a decoy price?

**Level:** Basic

A decoy is an unattractive extra option added to steer buyers toward a preferred one. A $125 print-only tier next to a $125 print+digital tier pushes buyers to the bundle. The decoy is not meant to be chosen.

**Real-life applications:**
- Magazine subscription pages.
- Popcorn sizes at cinemas.
- SaaS plan comparisons.
- Coffee-shop drink sizes.

**Key concepts:** `decoy`, `asymmetric dominance`, `choice architecture`, `behavior`, `framing`

---

## Q25. What is elastic demand?

**Level:** Basic

Elastic demand means customers react strongly to price changes — a small price cut creates a big sales lift. Elasticity is greater than 1. Products with substitutes tend to be elastic.

**Real-life applications:**
- Branded cereals versus store brands.
- Mid-tier hotels near alternatives.
- Electronics with many competitors.
- Apparel during seasonal transitions.

**Key concepts:** `elastic demand`, `substitutes`, `price sensitivity`, `elasticity > 1`, `response`

---

## Q26. What is inelastic demand?

**Level:** Basic

Inelastic demand means customers barely change their buying when prices move. Elasticity is less than 1. Essentials, addictive goods, and monopolies tend to be inelastic.

**Real-life applications:**
- Prescription medicines.
- Utilities like electricity.
- Cigarettes and alcohol.
- Commuter rail tickets.

**Key concepts:** `inelastic demand`, `necessities`, `low sensitivity`, `elasticity < 1`, `monopoly`

---

## Q27. What is cross-price elasticity?

**Level:** Basic

Cross-price elasticity measures how the price of one product changes demand for another. Positive numbers mean substitutes; negative numbers mean complements. It helps avoid promotions that cannibalize siblings.

**Real-life applications:**
- Coke price affects Pepsi sales.
- Printer prices affect ink sales.
- Gas prices affect EV demand.
- Bread prices affect butter demand.

**Key concepts:** `cross-elasticity`, `substitutes`, `complements`, `cannibalization`, `basket analysis`

---

## Q28. What is a price fence?

**Level:** Basic

A price fence is a rule that keeps one group from buying another group's lower price. Student IDs, advance purchase, or membership tiers are all fences. They protect segmentation from being gamed.

**Real-life applications:**
- Student discount verified by .edu email.
- Early-bird fares must be booked 21 days out.
- Corporate rates require company accounts.
- Senior rates require ID at check-in.

**Key concepts:** `price fence`, `segmentation`, `eligibility`, `discount protection`, `rules`

---

## Q29. What is surge pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Surge pricing is a short-term jump in price triggered by high demand. Ride-hailing apps popularized it by raising fares when many people request rides at once. It signals drivers to come online and riders to wait or pay more.

**Real-life applications:**
- Uber during rainstorms.
- Concert parking during events.
- Holiday flight tickets.
- Marketplace items after viral posts.

**Key concepts:** `surge pricing`, `peak demand`, `real-time`, `algorithm`, `supply balance`

---

## Q30. What is a price floor?

**Level:** Basic

A price floor is the minimum price an algorithm or team will accept for a product. It prevents auto-repricers from cutting below cost or brand-safe levels. Every pricing system needs one.

**Real-life applications:**
- Retailers set floors above SKU cost.
- Marketplaces forbid below-cost sales.
- Ride apps hold a minimum fare.
- Luxury brands set MAP policies.

**Key concepts:** `price floor`, `minimum price`, `MAP`, `cost protection`, `guardrail`

---

## Q31. What is a price ceiling?

**Level:** Basic

A price ceiling is the maximum price allowed, often set by company policy or law. It caps how high algorithms can push prices during demand spikes. Ceilings protect customer trust and legal standing.

**Real-life applications:**
- Surge caps during state emergencies.
- Regulated utility rate caps.
- Internal brand pricing policies.
- Government medicine price caps.

**Key concepts:** `price ceiling`, `maximum price`, `regulation`, `guardrail`, `consumer protection`

---

## Q32. What is yield management?

**Level:** Basic

Yield management is a form of revenue management that optimizes price and availability for perishable inventory. Airlines, hotels, and events use it to fill as many seats as possible at the best average price. Empty seats are lost forever.

**Real-life applications:**
- Airlines managing fare buckets.
- Hotels closing discounts near capacity.
- Stadiums pricing seats by section.
- Rental-car fleets managing daily rates.

**Key concepts:** `yield management`, `perishable inventory`, `capacity`, `RM`, `booking curve`

---

## Q33. What is RevPAR?

**Level:** Basic

RevPAR stands for revenue per available room. It is a hotel industry metric that combines occupancy and average daily rate. It shows whether a hotel is making the most of its rooms.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hotel owners compare RevPAR to market.
- Brands benchmark RevPAR across regions.
- Revenue teams target RevPAR weekly.
- Investors judge chains on RevPAR growth.

**Key concepts:** `RevPAR`, `occupancy`, `ADR`, `hotel metric`, `revenue per room`

---

## Q34. What is a fare class?

**Level:** Basic

A fare class is a price tier on an airline ticket with specific rules — refundability, baggage, changes, advance purchase. Classes sort buyers into segments without altering the seat itself. Inventory is allocated across classes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Economy saver versus economy flex.
- Business class flexible versus restricted.
- First class with full services.
- Basic economy with no changes.

**Key concepts:** `fare class`, `tiers`, `rules`, `segmentation`, `airline RM`

---

## Q35. What is overbooking?

**Level:** Basic

Overbooking is the practice of selling more units than actual capacity, based on a forecast that some buyers will cancel or no-show. If forecasts are right, the business fills capacity and maximizes revenue. If wrong, the business must compensate bumped customers.

**Real-life applications:**
- Airlines overbook flights.
- Hotels overbook peak nights.
- Restaurants overbook tables.
- Events overbook parking.

**Key concepts:** `overbooking`, `no-show`, `compensation`, `forecast`, `capacity`

---

## Q36. What is a reference price?

**Level:** Basic

A reference price is the price a customer mentally compares new prices against. It can come from past purchases, competitor prices, or displayed "original" prices. It shapes whether a new price feels fair.

**Real-life applications:**
- "Was $50, now $30" creates a reference.
- Menu items show a market price benchmark.
- Buyers anchor on last year's fare.
- Shoppers recall last week's grocery cost.

**Key concepts:** `reference price`, `anchor`, `perception`, `was price`, `fairness`

---

## Q37. What is price discrimination?

**Level:** Basic

Price discrimination is charging different prices to different customers for the same or similar product. It is legal and common when based on legitimate factors like volume, region, or channel. It becomes illegal when based on protected attributes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Bulk B2B discounts.
- Student and senior fares.
- Regional pricing variations.
- Time-based peak/off-peak pricing.

**Key concepts:** `price discrimination`, `segmentation`, `volume`, `legal`, `pricing ethics`

---

## Q38. What is a volume discount?

**Level:** Basic

A volume discount is a lower unit price offered for larger orders. It rewards buyers who commit to bigger quantities and helps sellers move more units. It is a core tool in B2B pricing.

**Real-life applications:**
- Wholesale clubs' case pricing.
- Enterprise software user tiers.
- Manufacturer shipment discounts.
- Airline group fares.

**Key concepts:** `volume discount`, `tier`, `bulk`, `B2B`, `unit price`

---

## Q39. What is a rebate?

**Level:** Basic

A rebate is a refund paid to a buyer after purchase, usually when they hit a volume or spend target. Unlike a discount, the customer pays full price first and gets money back later. Rebates encourage future buying.

**Real-life applications:**
- Annual volume rebates in B2B.
- Consumer mail-in rebates.
- Credit card cashback offers.
- Industrial quarterly rebates.

**Key concepts:** `rebate`, `post-purchase`, `target`, `volume`, `loyalty`

---

## Q40. What is a list price?

**Level:** Basic

List price is the published reference price of a product before any discounts, rebates, or negotiations. It is the starting point for both B2C displays and B2B quotes. Actual transaction prices often sit below it.

**Real-life applications:**
- Car MSRPs on window stickers.
- Software list-price sheets.
- Industrial equipment catalogs.
- Retail shelf price tags.

**Key concepts:** `list price`, `MSRP`, `reference`, `starting point`, `catalog`

---

## Q41. What is a net price?

**Level:** Basic

Net price is the final price a customer actually pays after all discounts, promotions, and rebates. It is what the seller actually collects, not what is printed on the tag. Pricing teams watch net price more than list price.

**Real-life applications:**
- Retailers track net price per transaction.
- B2B firms use net-net price after rebates.
- Pharmaceutical firms monitor net realized price.
- Distributors pass list minus discount.

**Key concepts:** `net price`, `realization`, `after discount`, `actual`, `transaction`

---

## Q42. What is price realization?

**Level:** Basic

Price realization is the actual price collected divided by the list or target price. A realization of 85% means the firm collected 85 cents for each dollar of list price. Leakage from discounts and rebates drops realization.

**Real-life applications:**
- B2B sales teams track realization by deal.
- Manufacturers compare realization across regions.
- Pharma firms measure realization by payer.
- Industrial firms target realization KPIs.

**Key concepts:** `realization`, `leakage`, `discount`, `net to list`, `KPI`

---

## Q43. What is a loss leader?

**Level:** Basic

A loss leader is a product sold below cost to attract shoppers who then buy other profitable items. Grocery stores use milk and eggs as classic loss leaders. It is a traffic-building tactic, not a pricing mistake.

**Real-life applications:**
- Supermarkets pricing milk below cost.
- Retailers discounting printers to sell ink.
- Streaming services free tiers.
- Console makers pricing hardware below cost.

**Key concepts:** `loss leader`, `traffic`, `cross-sell`, `basket`, `margin mix`

---

## Q44. What is bundle pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Bundle pricing groups products together at a single combined price, usually lower than buying each separately. It lifts average basket size and moves slower-selling items. It also simplifies the buying decision.

**Real-life applications:**
- Fast food combo meals.
- Software suite bundles.
- Telecom triple-play packages.
- Hotel room-plus-breakfast rates.

**Key concepts:** `bundle`, `basket size`, `cross-sell`, `combined price`, `simplification`

---

## Q45. What is a promotional price?

**Level:** Basic

A promotional price is a temporary reduction to boost short-term sales or clear inventory. It is usually time-limited and marketed heavily. Promotions can lift volume but may also hurt brand perception if overused.

**Real-life applications:**
- Black Friday retail deals.
- Coupon codes for online stores.
- Seasonal clearance sales.
- Buy-one-get-one events.

**Key concepts:** `promotion`, `temporary`, `discount`, `BOGO`, `clearance`

---

## Q46. What is a markdown?

**Level:** Basic

A markdown is a permanent price cut, often used to clear aging inventory. It differs from a promotion, which is temporary. Markdowns are common in fashion and seasonal retail.

**Real-life applications:**
- Clothing retailers at season end.
- Grocery near-expiry markdowns.
- Bookstore remainder pricing.
- Electronics at product end-of-life.

**Key concepts:** `markdown`, `inventory`, `clearance`, `permanent`, `seasonal`

---

## Q47. What is price transparency?

**Level:** Basic

Price transparency is how easily customers can find and compare prices. The internet has raised transparency sharply. It forces sellers to justify differences and compete harder on value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparison websites for insurance.
- Flight aggregators like Kayak.
- E-commerce marketplace listings.
- Hospital chargemaster disclosures.

**Key concepts:** `transparency`, `comparison`, `online`, `information`, `competition`

---

## Q48. What is predatory pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Predatory pricing is setting prices very low to drive rivals out of business, with the plan to raise prices later. It is illegal in most countries under antitrust law. Regulators watch dominant firms for this behavior.

**Real-life applications:**
- Antitrust cases against big platforms.
- Telecom incumbent investigations.
- Grocery chain complaints.
- Airline route lawsuits.

**Key concepts:** `predatory pricing`, `antitrust`, `monopoly`, `illegal`, `below cost`

---

## Q49. What is a price war?

**Level:** Basic

A price war is when rivals keep cutting prices in response to each other, shrinking margins for everyone. It often starts from a small move and escalates quickly. The only winners are usually customers in the short term.

**Real-life applications:**
- Airline fare wars on busy routes.
- Ride-hailing app competition.
- Telecom data-plan cuts.
- E-commerce flash-sale cycles.

**Key concepts:** `price war`, `escalation`, `margin erosion`, `competitive response`, `race to bottom`

---

## Q50. What is MSRP?

**Level:** Basic

MSRP stands for manufacturer's suggested retail price. It is the price the maker recommends retailers charge. Retailers are free to sell below it, but it serves as an anchor and limit for promotions.

**Real-life applications:**
- Car window stickers show MSRP.
- Electronics list MSRP on packaging.
- Fashion brands publish MSRP in catalogs.
- Home goods show MSRP as anchor.

**Key concepts:** `MSRP`, `manufacturer`, `suggested price`, `anchor`, `retail`

---

## Q51. What is MAP?

**Level:** Basic

MAP stands for minimum advertised price. Manufacturers set it to stop retailers from publicly advertising prices below a floor. Retailers can still sell lower in-store or at checkout, but not advertise it.

**Real-life applications:**
- Electronics brands enforcing MAP online.
- Appliance makers policing listings.
- Cosmetics brands protecting premium image.
- Tool makers setting MAP for big-box sellers.

**Key concepts:** `MAP`, `minimum advertised price`, `floor`, `brand protection`, `retail`

---

## Q52. What is a premium price?

**Level:** Basic

A premium price is one set above the market average, positioning the product as higher-quality or more desirable. It works when brand, design, or service justifies the gap. Luxury products rely on premium pricing.

**Real-life applications:**
- Apple versus Android phones.
- Premium coffee shops versus chains.
- Boutique hotels versus budget.
- Organic food versus conventional.

**Key concepts:** `premium price`, `positioning`, `luxury`, `brand`, `perception`

---

## Q53. What is price leadership?

**Level:** Basic

Price leadership is when one firm sets the reference price in a market and rivals follow. The leader is often the largest or strongest brand. It reduces price-war risk when followed responsibly.

**Real-life applications:**
- Dominant airlines setting fare trends.
- Gas-station chains anchoring regional rates.
- Cereal brand leading category pricing.
- Banking leader setting savings rates.

**Key concepts:** `price leadership`, `follower`, `market leader`, `signaling`, `stability`

---

## Q54. What is A/B price testing?

**Level:** Basic

A/B testing runs two prices in parallel on similar groups to see which performs better. It is common in e-commerce and SaaS. Good tests need enough traffic and clean controls.

**Real-life applications:**
- SaaS landing-page price tests.
- E-commerce checkout experiments.
- App in-app purchase tests.
- Subscription plan comparisons.

**Key concepts:** `A/B test`, `experiment`, `control`, `pricing test`, `conversion`

---

## Q55. What is a conjoint analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Conjoint analysis is a survey method that shows buyers bundles of features and prices to estimate how they value each attribute. It reveals willingness to pay for specific features. Product teams use it to decide what to build and how to price.

**Real-life applications:**
- Consumer electronics launch research.
- Auto feature pricing studies.
- Insurance product design.
- Airline add-on pricing.

**Key concepts:** `conjoint`, `feature value`, `WTP`, `survey`, `research`

---

## Q56. What is a Van Westendorp study?

**Level:** Basic

A Van Westendorp study asks buyers four price questions to estimate an acceptable price range. Questions cover "too cheap," "too expensive," "cheap," and "expensive." The crossovers give useful price points.

**Real-life applications:**
- New-product pricing surveys.
- SaaS tier calibration.
- Consumer goods pre-launch.
- Service pricing studies.

**Key concepts:** `Van Westendorp`, `price sensitivity`, `survey`, `acceptable range`, `research`

---

## Q57. What is customer lifetime value?

**Level:** Basic

Customer lifetime value (CLV) is the total profit a customer brings over their relationship with a firm. It informs how much to spend acquiring them and how to price. High-CLV segments can absorb higher prices.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q69. What is the drawback of cost-plus pricing?

**Level:** Basic

Cost-plus pricing ignores what customers are willing to pay. It under-prices high-value products and over-prices weak ones. It also gives no incentive to reduce costs, since cuts shrink revenue in lockstep.

**Real-life applications:**
- Government contractors over-billing.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Pricing Method Choice Tree
**Purpose:** Match the pricing method to the business problem.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
cost-plus -> margin control
value-based -> willingness to pay
competition-based -> rival benchmark
psychological -> choice framing
dynamic -> live demand and capacity
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: cost-plus - useful when cost control and defendability matter most.
Component 2: value-based pricing - useful when customer value varies widely.
Component 3: competition-based - useful when the market is crowded and reference price is clear.
Component 4: psychological pricing - useful when framing changes the decision without changing the economics.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A software company launching an enterprise analytics tool should not use cost-plus only because it is easy. If customers pay for business impact, value-based pricing is the better anchor; if rivals are easy to compare, competition-based pricing becomes a guardrail.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is mixing up pricing method, pricing tactic, and pricing governance.

### Price Waterfall Diagnostic
**Purpose:** Show how price leaks between list price and realized price.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
list price -> discount -> rebate -> markdown -> net price -> pocket price -> realization
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: list price / MSRP - the posted starting point.
Component 2: discount / rebate / markdown - the concessions given along the way.
Component 3: net price / pocket price - what the firm really collects after leakage.
Component 4: realization - the final margin and cash outcome.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A B2B supplier posts a strong list price but gives heavy rebates and end-of-quarter discounts. The waterfall shows that the headline price is irrelevant if the pocket price collapses. The decision is to tighten discount authority, not just raise the list price.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When revenue looks fine on paper but margin keeps disappearing.

### Willingness-to-Pay Discovery Toolkit
**Purpose:** Find the price range the market can accept before launch or re-pricing.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
survey -> conjoint -> Van Westendorp -> A/B test -> price range
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: survey and interview input - early read on customer value and reference price.
Component 2: conjoint - trade-off testing across features and prices.
Component 3: Van Westendorp - a structured way to find acceptable price bounds.
Component 4: A/B test - live validation against real behavior.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A startup does not know whether to price an AI note-taking app at a flat fee or a subscription tier. Conjoint testing shows buyers prefer a mid-tier monthly plan, while an A/B test confirms that the trial-to-paid conversion is stronger than with a one-time fee.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When nobody knows the real willingness to pay.

### Revenue Management Control Loop
**Purpose:** Balance demand, capacity, and fairness in time-sensitive categories.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
demand -> capacity -> price floor/ceiling -> surge/pricing rules -> monitor -> adjust
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: demand and capacity - whether the market is tight or soft.
Component 2: price floor and ceiling - the legal and commercial guardrails.
Component 3: surge pricing / yield management - the live response to scarcity.
Component 4: monitoring - how the team tracks revenue, customer response, and exceptions.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A hotel has high demand for conference week and low demand the next week. The control loop says raise rates within a ceiling during the peak, then relax them when occupancy drops. The decision is not just higher price; it is disciplined pricing by time window and capacity.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When supply is fixed and the only question is how aggressively to price the scarce units.

## 4. Formulas
The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are operational heuristics [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Markup
Formula: `Markup = (price - cost) / cost`
Variables:
price = the selling price
cost = the unit cost
Why this formula exists: It answers how much above cost the firm is charging.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> weak cushion; watch margin pressure
Value moderate -> workable pricing room
Value high -> strong pricing power, if the market accepts it
Worked example with numbers: If cost is 100 and price is 140, markup is 40%. Decision: compare the markup to the segment's willingness to pay before assuming it is enough.

### Formula 2: Gross Margin
Formula: `Gross Margin = (price - cost) / price`
Variables:
price = the selling price
cost = the unit cost
Why this formula exists: It answers how much of revenue is left after direct cost.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> price is too close to cost
Value moderate -> acceptable, but still improvable
Value high -> strong margin, if volume holds
Worked example with numbers: If price is 200 and cost is 120, gross margin is 40%. Decision: preserve the margin unless volume loss is clearly justified by higher share.

### Formula 3: Price Elasticity
Formula: `Price Elasticity = % change in quantity / % change in price`
Variables:
quantity = units sold
price = the selling price
Why this formula exists: It answers how sensitive demand is to price moves.
How to interpret the output:
Value > 1 -> elastic; demand moves a lot, so price changes need care
Value around 1 -> mixed response; test carefully
Value < 1 -> inelastic; there may be room to raise price
Worked example with numbers: If a 10% price cut raises quantity by 15%, elasticity is 1.5. Decision: a lower price can grow revenue only if margin loss is acceptable.

### Formula 4: Consumer Surplus
Formula: `Consumer Surplus = willingness to pay - price`
Variables:
willingness to pay = the highest price the customer would still accept
price = the actual amount charged
Why this formula exists: It answers how much value the customer keeps after paying.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> the customer is near indifference or unhappy
Value moderate -> a normal value split
Value high -> the customer may have room to pay more
Worked example with numbers: If willingness to pay is 1,000 and price is 750, consumer surplus is 250. Decision: there may be room to test a higher tier or premium bundle.

### Formula 5: Price Waterfall Gap
Formula: `Price Waterfall Gap = list price - pocket price` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
list price = the starting posted price
pocket price = the amount actually retained after all concessions
Why this formula exists: It answers how much revenue is leaking before the firm recognizes it.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> tight discount discipline
Value moderate -> manageable leakage
Value high -> pricing and sales are giving away margin
Worked example with numbers: If list price is 500 and pocket price is 360, the gap is 140. Decision: reduce rebates, discount approvals, or untracked promotions.

### Formula 6: CLV
Formula: `CLV = average order value x purchase frequency x customer lifespan` [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
average order value = average revenue per transaction
purchase frequency = how often the customer buys
customer lifespan = how long the customer stays active
Why this formula exists: It answers whether a pricing move hurts or helps the lifetime economics of the segment.
How to interpret the output:
Value low -> be cautious with acquisition spend
Value moderate -> improve retention and mix
Value high -> the segment can support premium pricing or more aggressive acquisition
Worked example with numbers: If average order value is 2,000, frequency is 3 per year, and lifespan is 4 years, CLV is 24,000. Decision: price the offer to protect retention, not just the first sale.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Use cost-plus when customers are buying outcomes. | Use value-based pricing when willingness to pay is driven by value delivered. |
| Manage the list price and ignore the waterfall. | Track rebates, markdowns, discounts, and pocket price. |
| Cut price without checking elasticity and CLV. | Test demand sensitivity and lifetime economics first. |
| Launch with one price structure and hope it fits all. | Use skimming, penetration, or tiered pricing based on the market. |
| Let sales give away margin in ad hoc discounts. | Put approval rules and price fences around concessions. |
| Personalize pricing without fairness controls. | Keep transparency, legal review, and customer-perception metrics in the loop. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: AI SaaS Launch
Situation: A product team is launching an AI note-taking tool and is unsure whether to use freemium, subscription, or usage-based pricing. The team wants fast adoption without locking itself into a weak price.
Applicable framework/metric: Willingness-to-Pay Discovery Toolkit and CLV.
Analysis: Conjoint testing shows the strongest response to a mid-tier subscription, while the free plan converts many users but produces weak CLV. A/B tests show that a tiered paid offer improves trial-to-paid conversion more than a one-time fee.
Decision rule: If CLV is low, favor a cheaper entry and better retention. If willingness to pay is strong, use value-based pricing or a premium tier. If conversion is flat across options, simplify the structure.
Action: Launch with a clear tier ladder, measure churn by cohort, and revisit the pricing page after the first 30 days.

### Scenario 2: Industrial Supplier Price Waterfall
Situation: A B2B manufacturer shows a healthy list price but keeps missing margin targets. Sales gives rebates, markdowns, and special terms to close deals, and nobody has a clean view of the true realized price.
Applicable framework/metric: Price Waterfall Diagnostic and Gross Margin.
Analysis: The waterfall shows that a 500 list price becomes a 360 pocket price after discounts and rebates. Gross margin compresses even though the headline price never changed.
Decision rule: If the waterfall gap is large, fix discount governance. If gross margin is weak but volume is strong, check whether volume discounts are earning their keep. If the realized price is stable, leave the list price alone and improve mix.
Action: Build an approval workflow, expose pocket price in the CRM, and stop untracked concessions.

### Scenario 3: Airline or Hotel Revenue Management
Situation: An airline or hotel faces fixed capacity and time-sensitive demand. The team wants to protect revenue during peak periods without alienating customers.
Applicable framework/metric: Revenue Management Control Loop and RevPAR.
Analysis: As occupancy rises, the system can raise fares or room rates within a price ceiling. If overbooking and fare class control are disciplined, RevPAR rises without exploding customer outrage.
Decision rule: If demand is spiky and capacity is fixed, use revenue management. If the price ceiling is being hit too often, widen the band only after checking fairness. If occupancy is soft, relax the price instead of forcing a surge.
Action: Align fare classes or room tiers with demand windows, monitor exceptions, and publish a clear policy for major price jumps.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Classify every product or segment by the pricing method that best fits it.
2. Build a price waterfall that shows list price, discount, rebate, net price, pocket price, and realization.
3. Test willingness to pay with surveys, conjoint, and live A/B experiments before a major launch.
4. Put floor, ceiling, and approval rules into the pricing engine before automation goes live.
5. Monitor elasticity, churn, and customer-perception metrics together so revenue does not crowd out trust.
6. Define price fences for B2B, B2C, and segment pricing so discounts do not leak across segments.
7. Review pricing monthly for leakage, fairness, and margin drift, then update the rules instead of overriding them ad hoc.
