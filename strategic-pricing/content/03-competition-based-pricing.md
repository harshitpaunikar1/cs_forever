# Competition-Based Pricing

## Overview

Competition-based pricing sets prices by looking at what rivals charge and choosing a position: match them, undercut them, or charge a premium. It is common in crowded markets where buyers compare listings side by side. The price of the competitor becomes the anchor.

---

## Why It Matters

If you ignore competitors, you can get wiped out on e-commerce marketplaces where a single click shows every rival price. But blindly matching rivals drags everyone into a margin-destroying race. The skill is knowing when to match, when to hold firm, and when to walk away.

## Key Principles

- Track the right competitor set — not every seller is a true rival.
- Monitor prices daily on volatile categories, weekly on stable ones.
- Decide price position by brand strategy, not by reflex.
- Factor in shipping, bundles, and promotions, not just the sticker price.
- Avoid public price wars; use private negotiation when possible.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Price Match** | Pricing a product equal to a named competitor's price. |
| **Price Premium** | Charging more than rivals, justified by brand or features. |
| **Undercutting** | Pricing deliberately below a rival to steal share. |
| **Competitive Set** | The defined list of rivals whose prices you monitor. |

## Use Case

An electronics retailer sees a popular TV model drop 8% on a rival marketplace. They check margin headroom, confirm their inventory position, and match the price for 48 hours to protect search ranking and customer traffic.

## Scenario

> A mid-tier hotel chain kept rates 5% above the market average for a decade. When a budget chain entered the city, they panicked and cut rates 20%. Revenue fell because loyal guests felt the brand had cheapened. They restored rates and added a free breakfast instead — occupancy recovered in two months.

## Examples

- An online shoe store auto-matches Amazon prices within a 2% band.
- A premium coffee brand refuses to price-match and competes on origin stories.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Competitive Response Ladder
**Purpose:** Choose the right response when a competitor changes price.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
define competitor set -> compare effective price -> check margin headroom -> choose response -> monitor traffic
            |                    |                        |                     |                |
       true rivals         sticker + shipping       profitable room?     match/premium/undercut   search ranking
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Define competitor set: only the sellers that truly affect your customer choice.
Compare effective price: use sticker price plus shipping and promotions, not just the visible number.
Check margin headroom: confirm whether a response preserves profitability.
Choose response: match, hold premium, undercut briefly, or walk away.
Monitor traffic: watch customer traffic, conversion, and search ranking after the move.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A marketplace vendor sees a rival TV price drop by 8%. The team checks whether the competitor is in the true set, confirms margin headroom, matches for 48 hours, and watches whether search ranking and traffic recover. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

When to pull this out in a meeting: When pricing wants a fast answer to a competitor move and finance wants proof that the move will not destroy margin.

### Price Pressure Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether to match, defend, premium-price, or exit based on market pressure and financial room.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    Margin headroom
                 low                        high
High pressure  | temporary undercut   | selective match
Low pressure   | hold or exit         | premium defend
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Market pressure: how intense the competitor comparison is for the buyer.
Margin headroom: how much price flexibility exists without breaking the economics.
Temporary undercut: a short tactical response when pressure is high but room is limited.
Selective match: match only where the competitor set or customer segment justifies it.
Hold or exit: do not fight a losing category with no margin room.
Premium defend: keep price above market when the brand can justify it.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A premium coffee brand can stay above market if loyal buyers value origin story and quality. A commodity accessories seller in a search-driven marketplace may need selective matching on key SKUs because the buyer is comparing identical listings. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team needs to separate a true price war from a category where premium positioning is still viable.

## 4. Formulas
The source is conceptual, so these metrics are standard pricing controls used to turn competitor observation into action. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: `Relative price index = Our effective price / Competitor effective price`
Variables:
Our effective price = sticker price adjusted for shipping, promotions, and bundles.
Competitor effective price = rival price adjusted the same way.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether we are below, at, or above the market after all add-ons.
How to interpret the output:
Value below 0.95 -> undercut territory.
Value 0.95 to 1.05 -> parity territory.
Value above 1.05 -> premium territory. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Worked example with numbers: If our effective price is 1,020 and the competitor's is 1,000, the relative price index is 1.02. That is near parity. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: `Price gap % = (Our effective price - Competitor effective price) / Competitor effective price`
Variables:
Our effective price = what the customer really pays us.
Competitor effective price = what the customer really pays the rival.
Why this formula exists: It answers how far we are from the rival in percentage terms.
How to interpret the output:
Value below -5% -> we are aggressively undercutting.
Value -5% to +5% -> we are effectively at parity.
Value above +5% -> we are charging a premium. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Worked example with numbers: If our effective price is 950 and the competitor's is 1,000, the price gap is -5%. That is a mild undercut, not a full price war. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: `Margin after match = Contribution margin before match - Price concession`
Variables:
Contribution margin before match = profit per unit before the price response.
Price concession = the amount cut from the current price.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether matching the rival still leaves a viable unit economics story.
How to interpret the output:
Value below 0 -> do not match; the sale loses money.
Value 0 to 10% of price -> only match for a short tactical window.
Value above 10% of price -> there is room to defend the price move. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Worked example with numbers: If unit contribution margin is 120 and the match requires a 40 cut, margin after match is 80. The response is safe only if the customer value or traffic lift justifies it. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Track every seller as if they were a true rival. | Define a competitive set that reflects real substitution. |
| Match a competitor before checking landed price. | Compare sticker, shipping, bundles, and promotions together. |
| Cut price just because the market moved. | Tie the response to brand strategy and margin headroom. |
| Start a public price war with no exit rule. | Use a short, controlled response and monitor the result. |
| Ignore premium options when the brand can justify them. | Defend a price premium when service or differentiation supports it. |
| Treat private negotiation as an afterthought in B2B. | Use private negotiation to protect value when list prices are visible. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: TV price response in e-commerce
Situation: An electronics retailer sees a popular TV model drop 8% on a rival marketplace. Search ranking matters because buyers compare listings side by side.  
Applicable framework/metric: Competitive Response Ladder and relative price index.  
Analysis: If the competitor's effective price is 92 and the retailer's is 100, the gap is +8.7%. Matching for 48 hours keeps the listing competitive without committing to a permanent cut.  
Decision rule: If margin headroom stays positive and traffic is at risk, match briefly. If the product is already a low-margin loss leader, avoid a reflex match.  
Action: Reprice the SKU, watch traffic and conversion daily, and revert once ranking stabilizes.

### Scenario 2: Mid-tier hotel under budget pressure
Situation: A mid-tier hotel chain keeps rates 5% above the market average until a budget chain enters the city. The team considers a 20% rate cut to protect occupancy.  
Applicable framework/metric: Price Pressure Matrix and price gap %.  
Analysis: A 20% cut moves the hotel from slight premium into a deep undercut, which can cheapen the brand and train guests to wait for discounts. Restoring rates and adding free breakfast can preserve the price story while still improving occupancy.  
Decision rule: If premium positioning still matters, defend the price with value-added benefits. If occupancy falls below the minimum acceptable level, use a limited tactical cut instead of a permanent repositioning.  
Action: Restore the rate card, add a bundled breakfast or late checkout, and review occupancy against ADR weekly. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Scenario 3: Premium coffee brand vs commodity pressure
Situation: A premium coffee brand is compared against a cheaper private-label competitor. The product team wants to know whether to price-match or hold firm.  
Applicable framework/metric: Margin after match and relative price index.  
Analysis: If the premium brand sits 12% above the competitor but retains loyal demand, the relative price index supports premium defense. If a match would push margin after match below zero, the response should be non-price: story, origin, or bundle.  
Decision rule: If the brand can justify premium and margin stays healthy, hold firm. If traffic collapses, use targeted promotions or a private negotiation rather than a public cut.  
Action: Reinforce origin story, test bundle offers, and monitor repeat purchase instead of racing to the bottom. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Define the true competitive set for each product, channel, and geography.
2. Compute effective prices with shipping, bundles, and promotions included.
3. Set a response rule for each segment: match, premium, temporary undercut, or walk away.
4. Tie any price move to margin headroom and a clear time window.
5. Monitor traffic, conversion, search ranking, and occupancy after the move.
6. Use private negotiation or bundle design when public price cuts would damage the brand.
7. Review the price architecture weekly in volatile categories and monthly in stable ones.
