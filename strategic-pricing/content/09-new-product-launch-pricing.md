# New Product Launch Pricing

## Overview

Launch pricing is the first price set on a new product when it enters the market. The two main approaches are skimming — starting high and lowering over time — and penetration — starting low to grab share fast. The choice shapes how the product is perceived and how quickly it scales.

---

## Why It Matters

A launch price anchors customer expectations for years. Price too high and you kill adoption; price too low and you starve funding and devalue the brand. Recovering from a bad launch price is slower than almost any other pricing mistake.

## Key Principles

- Match price strategy to competitive intensity and production capacity.
- Validate willingness to pay with conjoint or Van Westendorp studies before launch.
- Plan the price trajectory for the first 12–24 months, not just day one.
- Reserve introductory offers for early customers; avoid permanent discounts.
- Align launch price with distribution channel economics.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Skimming** | Launching at a high price to capture early-adopter surplus, then cutting. |
| **Penetration Pricing** | Launching low to build volume, trust, and market share quickly. |
| **Van Westendorp Study** | Survey method that estimates acceptable price ranges. |
| **Price Trajectory** | Planned sequence of price moves after launch. |

## Use Case

A consumer electronics firm launches a new smartwatch at $399 and plans three $50 cuts across two years as rivals catch up. Early adopters pay the premium; late mass-market buyers still see the product as aspirational.

## Scenario

> A startup launched a fitness app free for six months to build a user base, then introduced a $12 monthly plan. The free period hit 2 million users, 18% of whom converted to paid, generating $26M in year-one revenue — more than a higher-priced launch would have earned.

## Examples

- A gaming console launches high, knowing super-fans will pre-order at any price.
- A new cola brand prices 20% below market leaders to encourage first trials.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Launch pricing is the first price customers see, so it becomes the anchor for adoption, brand perception, and future repricing.
For IT, AI, Product, and Consulting leaders, the question is whether the launch should maximize early profit, build share quickly, or create a premium signal.
The decision it helps make is how to choose between skimming and penetration, and how to plan the price path after day one.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Launch Strategy Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether a launch should skim, penetrate, delay, or stay in a test-and-learn mode.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                        Competitive intensity
                      low                    high
Production   high  [ skim and scale ]   [ penetration ]
capacity      low   [ staged skim ]      [ cautious pilot ]
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Competitive intensity - how crowded and price-sensitive the launch market is.
Component 2: Production capacity - whether you can serve volume if demand spikes.
Component 3: Skim and scale - use when the product is differentiated and supply is ready.
Component 4: Penetration - use when fast adoption matters more than early margin.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consumer electronics company with limited supply and a clearly premium smartwatch can launch with skimming. A SaaS startup entering a crowded category with low switching costs may need penetration to build installed base and trust quickly.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is stuck between "go premium" and "go cheap" without mapping supply and competition.

### Price Trajectory Map
**Purpose:** Turn launch pricing into a sequence instead of a one-time decision.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
launch price -> intro offer -> first cut -> second cut -> mature price
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Launch price - the initial market anchor.
Component 2: Intro offer - a temporary deal for early customers.
Component 3: Price cuts - planned reductions as the category matures.
Component 4: Mature price - the steady-state price after learning and competition.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A fitness app launches free for six months, then moves to a $12 monthly plan, then later tests a family bundle. The launch team is not just choosing a price; it is designing a sequence that converts early curiosity into sustainable revenue.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When finance wants only one price and product knows the launch will go through multiple phases.

### Validation Stack
**Purpose:** Reduce the risk of a bad launch price by validating willingness to pay before launch.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
conjoint -> Van Westendorp -> pilot test -> launch
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Conjoint - estimates tradeoffs and willingness to pay across feature bundles.
Component 2: Van Westendorp - estimates an acceptable price range from survey responses.
Component 3: Pilot test - checks actual behavior before full rollout.
Component 4: Launch - sets the final price with evidence, not hope.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team uses conjoint to test feature value, Van Westendorp to bound the acceptable range, and a small pilot to see if customers actually pay. That sequence helps the team avoid overconfidence in a single survey number.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the launch price is about to be locked but there is no behavioral evidence yet.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: Launch Premium = (launch price - reference price) / reference price
Variables:
Launch price = the initial price customers see.
Reference price = the competitor or mental benchmark customers already have.
Why this formula exists: It answers how aggressive the launch price is relative to the market anchor.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> penetration launch -> push trial and adoption
Value 0-0.20 -> competitive to slightly premium -> good if the product is differentiated
Value > 0.20 -> strong premium -> only use if WTP and positioning justify it
Worked example with numbers: If the reference price is $349 and the launch price is $399, the premium is (399 - 349) / 349 = 14.3%. That is a premium launch, but not so high that it automatically blocks adoption.

Formula: Intro Discount = 1 - (intro price / regular price)
Variables:
Intro price = the temporary launch offer.
Regular price = the intended steady-state price after launch.
Why this formula exists: It answers how much of a launch incentive you are giving away.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.10 -> small nudge -> use when the product already has strong pull
Value 0.10-0.30 -> meaningful incentive -> useful for trials and early adopters
Value > 0.30 -> heavy discount -> use carefully because it can anchor low
Worked example with numbers: If the regular price is $20 and the launch price is $12, the intro discount is 1 - 12/20 = 0.40. That is a strong launch offer and should be treated as temporary, not as the new normal.

Formula: Trial Conversion Rate = paid users / trial users
Variables:
Paid users = users who convert to a paid plan or purchase.
Trial users = users who experienced the launch offer or trial.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the launch price and offer are turning interest into revenue.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.10 -> weak conversion -> pricing or product fit needs work
Value 0.10-0.25 -> acceptable early signal -> iterate on onboarding and value proof
Value > 0.25 -> strong launch signal -> scale with control
Worked example with numbers: If 2,000,000 users try the product and 18% convert to paid, the rate is 0.18. That is a solid launch if the unit economics can support the free period.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Pick a launch price without testing WTP. | Use conjoint or Van Westendorp to bound the acceptable range first. |
| Assume low launch price is always safer. | Use penetration only when adoption speed matters more than early margin. |
| Make introductory discounts permanent. | Reserve the discount for early customers and plan the step-up. |
| Ignore channel margins and app-store fees. | Check distribution channel economics before finalizing the launch price. |
| Set day-one price and forget the rest of the lifecycle. | Define a 12-24 month price trajectory before the launch goes live. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: Premium smartwatch launch
Situation: A consumer electronics company launches a smartwatch with strong design and health features. The team debates whether to price it as a flagship premium device or use a lower entry price to broaden adoption.
Applicable framework/metric: Launch Strategy Matrix and Launch Premium.
Analysis: If the reference price for comparable devices is $349 and the new watch launches at $399, the premium is 14.3%. With limited production capacity and a premium brand signal, skimming is defensible.
Decision rule: If WTP is above the launch price and capacity is tight, skim. If WTP is uncertain but the product is highly substitutable, penetrate. If neither is true, run a pilot.
Action: Launch at a premium, keep a planned price-cut calendar, and monitor early adopter feedback for the next price move.

Scenario 2: Fitness app freemium conversion
Situation: A startup launches a fitness app free for six months to build a user base, then plans to charge monthly. The product and consulting teams need to know whether the free period is generating enough paid conversion to justify the launch.
Applicable framework/metric: Trial Conversion Rate and Price Trajectory Map.
Analysis: If 2,000,000 users try the app and 18% convert to paid, trial conversion is 0.18. At $12 per month, the launch strategy is working if retention is strong enough to recover acquisition and support costs.
Decision rule: If conversion is below 10%, tighten value proof or shorten the free period. If between 10% and 25%, keep iterating. If above 25%, scale carefully and move toward a paid bundle.
Action: Keep the free period time-boxed, measure activation by cohort, and prepare the month-seven price step before the free offer expires.

Scenario 3: AI productivity tool launch
Situation: A B2B AI product introduces a report-writing feature that promises to save users 10 hours a week. The team worries that a high launch price could slow adoption, but a low price may undercut the premium positioning.
Applicable framework/metric: Validation Stack and Intro Discount.
Analysis: If the regular price is $20 and the launch price is $12, the intro discount is 40%. That is a substantial adoption nudge, but it should only be used if the pilot shows strong conversion and the channel can support scale.
Decision rule: If pilot conversion is below 10%, the launch offer is still too weak. If conversion is 10%-25%, keep testing. If conversion is above 25%, move to a fuller rollout with a planned price step-up.
Action: Test the value proposition in a pilot, use the free or discounted period to collect proof, and set the post-launch price before the trial ends.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Estimate willingness to pay with a Van Westendorp study or conjoint survey before the launch price is fixed.
2. Map the competitive intensity, production capacity, and channel economics so the launch price fits the operating reality.
3. Decide whether the product needs skimming or penetration, then write the rationale in one sentence for finance and sales.
4. Build a 12-24 month price trajectory that includes any introductory offer, planned cuts, and mature-state pricing.
5. Separate launch pricing for early customers from the steady-state price so the discount does not become permanent.
6. Measure trial conversion, activation, and retention by cohort so the team can see whether the launch price is helping or hurting adoption.
7. Revisit the price after the first evidence cycle instead of waiting for the launch mistake to harden into brand memory.
