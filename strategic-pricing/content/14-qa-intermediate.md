# Practice Q&A — Intermediate

Analysis, comparison, and applied reasoning on pricing. Questions 71 through 140.

## Q71. When should a firm switch from cost-plus to value-based pricing?

**Level:** Intermediate

A firm should switch when its product delivers measurable customer value beyond what cost-plus captures. Triggers include high differentiation, strong ROI claims, and a sales team that keeps hearing "this is cheap." Sticking with cost-plus in these cases leaves meaningful money on the table.

**Real-life applications:**
- Software firms moving to per-outcome pricing.
- Medical devices switching after ROI evidence.
- Industrial tools bundling service-value packages.
- Consultancies moving from hourly to project fees.

**Key concepts:** `pricing migration`, `value-based`, `cost-plus`, `differentiation`, `ROI`

---

## Q72. How does price elasticity guide promotional decisions?

**Level:** Intermediate

Elasticity tells a manager whether a promotion will pay back through volume. Highly elastic products respond strongly to discounts and can hit revenue targets with modest cuts. Inelastic products generally should not be promoted because the price cut simply gives away margin without a matching volume lift.

**Real-life applications:**
- Grocery promo planners pick elastic SKUs.
- Apparel retailers mark down elastic items first.
- Telecoms promote elastic add-ons.
- Hotels discount elastic mid-week stays.

**Key concepts:** `elasticity`, `promotion ROI`, `volume lift`, `margin`, `planning`

---

## Q73. Compare skimming and penetration pricing.

**Level:** Intermediate

Skimming starts high to capture early adopters, then drops. Penetration starts low to build share and trust. Skimming protects margin and brand but invites competition; penetration accelerates adoption but sacrifices early profit and can anchor customers on low prices.

**Real-life applications:**
- Phone makers skim flagships.
- Streaming apps use penetration tiers.
- Pharma skims before generics.
- New entrants penetrate mature markets.

**Key concepts:** `skimming`, `penetration`, `trade-off`, `adoption`, `margin`

---

## Q74. How does psychological pricing interact with perceived quality?

**Level:** Intermediate

Charm pricing signals value but can undercut premium positioning. Round prices suggest quality and deliberateness. Luxury brands avoid .99 endings because they cheapen the feel, while mass retailers lean into them. The right choice depends on the brand's promise.

**Real-life applications:**
- Luxury watches priced at round numbers.
- Fast fashion ending in .99.
- Premium autos using clean digits.
- Discount grocers charming every SKU.

**Key concepts:** `charm pricing`, `quality signal`, `luxury`, `positioning`, `perception`

## Q75. What risks does dynamic pricing pose to customer trust?

**Level:** Intermediate

When prices change visibly and frequently, customers can feel manipulated. Surge pricing during emergencies has triggered outrage and regulation. Firms mitigate risk with transparent reasons, price caps, and advance notice. Trust lost to unfair pricing is slow to rebuild.

**Real-life applications:**
- Ride-hailing surge complaints.
- Concert ticket dynamic pricing backlash.
- Retailer personalized price exposés.
- Airline last-minute fare gripes.

**Key concepts:** `dynamic pricing`, `trust`, `fairness`, `backlash`, `transparency`

---

## Q76. How does revenue management differ from pricing?

**Level:** Intermediate

Pricing decides the price of a product. Revenue management decides which customer gets which price when — coordinating price, inventory, and segment. RM treats capacity as a finite good to allocate across segments, using price as one of several levers.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hotel RM systems closing low fares near capacity.
- Airline fare-class allocation.
- Rental-car inventory controls.
- Event-ticket holdbacks for premium buyers.

**Key concepts:** `revenue management`, `pricing`, `allocation`, `inventory`, `segment`

## Q77. Why does ML outperform manual pricing at scale?

**Level:** Intermediate

ML models can evaluate thousands of SKUs with many demand signals far faster than humans. They catch cross-product effects, seasonality, and competitor moves that human teams miss. At scale, the speed and granularity usually lift revenue by several percentage points over manual work.

**Real-life applications:**
- Retailer with 30,000 SKUs automating markdowns.
- Marketplace recommending seller prices.
- Airline re-pricing thousands of routes.
- Grocery chain nightly ML reprice.

**Key concepts:** `machine learning`, `scale`, `granularity`, `automation`, `lift`

## Q78. How do firms estimate willingness to pay before launch?

**Level:** Intermediate

Common methods include conjoint analysis, Van Westendorp surveys, in-market price tests, and qualitative buyer interviews. Each method has blind spots, so firms usually triangulate two or more. Pre-launch WTP estimates feed into the launch price and the trajectory that follows.

**Real-life applications:**
- Consumer electronics conjoint studies.
- SaaS Van Westendorp surveys.
- Beta-user price sensitivity tests.
- B2B prospect pricing interviews.

**Key concepts:** `WTP research`, `conjoint`, `Van Westendorp`, `triangulation`, `pre-launch`

## Q79. How does competitor monitoring improve pricing decisions?

**Level:** Intermediate

Monitoring turns slow rumor into daily facts. Teams see how rivals position, promote, and react, and can decide whether to match, hold, or differentiate. Without monitoring, firms either react late or overreact to noise. Clean monitoring data also reveals when a rival is actually weakening.

**Real-life applications:**
- Buy-box recovery on marketplaces.
- Category managers adjusting weekly.
- Hoteliers tracking rate shops.
- Airlines monitoring route fares.

**Key concepts:** `monitoring`, `competitive response`, `data`, `decision speed`, `positioning`

## Q80. When is segment pricing unfair?

**Level:** Intermediate

Segment pricing is usually fair when the segments are distinct and the fences transparent. It becomes unfair if buyers in one segment feel cheated because they cannot access a similar price elsewhere. Using sensitive attributes like race or unrelated personal data crosses into illegal discrimination.

**Real-life applications:**
- Gender-based insurance rules under scrutiny.
- Device-based price differences debated.
- ZIP-code discrimination lawsuits.
- Personalized pricing backlash.

**Key concepts:** `fairness`, `discrimination`, `fence`, `perception`, `ethics`

## Q81. How does B2B pricing balance rebates and list discounts?

**Level:** Intermediate

Rebates reward future behavior while discounts cut price today. Too much upfront discount erodes realization immediately; too much rebate delays cash and may never be earned. Strong B2B programs balance both, tying rebates to volume growth and keeping list discounts narrow.

**Real-life applications:**
- Distributor rebate ladders.
- Healthcare GPO rebates.
- Auto-industry volume tiers.
- Industrial firms tightening discount rules.

**Key concepts:** `rebate`, `list discount`, `realization`, `B2B`, `incentive design`

## Q82. How should a firm respond to a price war?

**Level:** Intermediate

The first step is to avoid escalation. A firm should isolate the competitor's move — which segment, which SKU — and match only where strategic, not across the board. Often the right move is adding value (service, bundle, loyalty) rather than matching the cut.

**Real-life applications:**
- Airlines holding premium fares while cutting economy.
- Retailers bundling rather than matching.
- Telcos adding data rather than cutting price.
- Hotels adding perks, not rate cuts.

**Key concepts:** `price war`, `strategic response`, `value add`, `segmentation`, `escalation control`

## Q83. How does price elasticity differ across product categories?

**Level:** Intermediate

Categories with many substitutes or discretionary demand tend to be elastic — snacks, fashion, entertainment. Categories that are essential, habit-driven, or regulated tend to be inelastic — medicine, utilities, salt. Even within a category, branded goods are usually less elastic than private labels.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pharma setting prices with low elasticity.
- Fashion running elastic-item promotions.
- Grocery pricing private label against brand.
- Utilities pricing under regulation.

**Key concepts:** `elasticity`, `category`, `substitutes`, `essentials`, `brand strength`

## Q84. Why are pricing rules important for automation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Rules act as guardrails on algorithms, preventing them from making disastrous moves. Without rules, models can price below cost, match flawed rival prices, or react to scraping errors. Rules translate business judgment into machine-readable limits.

**Real-life applications:**
- Retailer minimum-margin rules.
- Marketplace brand-MAP rules.
- Ride-app surge caps.
- Hotel floor rates.

**Key concepts:** `rules`, `guardrails`, `automation`, `business logic`, `risk`

## Q85. How does pricing affect brand perception?

**Level:** Intermediate

Price sends a quality signal even before the customer tries the product. Too-low prices hint at poor quality; too-high prices can feel exploitative. Consistent pricing across channels reinforces trust, while erratic pricing fuels suspicion. Luxury brands in particular defend price to protect perception.

**Real-life applications:**
- Apple maintaining premium prices.
- Luxury brands rejecting discounts.
- Budget brands avoiding high prices.
- DTC startups picking a clear price position.

**Key concepts:** `brand perception`, `signal`, `consistency`, `luxury`, `positioning`

## Q86. When does penetration pricing backfire?

**Level:** Intermediate

Penetration can backfire when low prices anchor customers who refuse to pay more later. It also fails if the low price attracts unprofitable users who churn once prices rise. Firms need a clear plan for lifting prices and for pruning bad-fit customers.

**Real-life applications:**
- Ride-apps struggling to raise prices.
- Streaming services stuck at intro rates.
- Startups with free-tier addiction.
- Consumer brands fighting discount expectations.

**Key concepts:** `penetration`, `anchoring`, `stuck prices`, `bad customers`, `trap`

## Q87. How can firms prevent discount leakage?

**Level:** Intermediate

Firms prevent leakage by tightening approval rules, tracking discounts by rep and deal, and building price waterfalls that show the true net price. Training sales teams on value selling and tying variable pay to realization also helps. Leakage discipline can lift margin by several points.

**Real-life applications:**
- B2B firms cleaning up price waterfalls.
- Distributors auditing discount trees.
- Pharma reviewing gross-to-net.
- Industrial firms updating approval limits.

**Key concepts:** `leakage`, `discount discipline`, `waterfall`, `realization`, `governance`

## Q88. What pricing risks appear when launching in new geographies?

**Level:** Intermediate

Different geographies have different WTP, purchasing power, regulation, and rival sets. A price that works in one country can be too high in another. Firms also face currency risk, grey-market arbitrage, and reference-price leakage across borders.

**Real-life applications:**
- Pharma differential pricing global rollouts.
- Electronics grey-market imports.
- Streaming tier-one vs tier-two country pricing.
- Software local-currency pricing.

**Key concepts:** `geographic pricing`, `purchasing power`, `arbitrage`, `localization`, `regulation`

## Q91. Compare good-better-best to two-tier pricing.

**Level:** Intermediate

Good-better-best offers three tiers; two-tier offers two. Three tiers anchor the top, steer to the middle, and keep a budget option available. Two tiers simplify choice and work well when the segment is narrow. Most SaaS firms converge on good-better-best because it raises average revenue per user.

**Real-life applications:**
- SaaS vendors moving from two to three tiers.
- Streaming services adjusting tier counts.
- Insurance adding middle tiers.
- Mobile plans expanding options.

**Key concepts:** `three-tier`, `two-tier`, `anchor`, `ARPU`, `choice architecture`

## Q92. How do managers avoid cannibalization in bundles?

**Level:** Intermediate

Managers design bundles so that the bundle adds clear value and does not simply displace existing higher-margin purchases. Tracking standalone sales after a bundle launch reveals cannibalization. Good bundles bring incremental buyers rather than shifting current ones.

**Real-life applications:**
- Fast-food combo cannibalization checks.
- Telecom triple-play adoption.
- SaaS suite cannibalization of standalone.
- Retail kit cannibalization of base SKUs.

**Key concepts:** `bundle`, `cannibalization`, `incrementality`, `mix shift`, `analysis`

## Q93. What is pocket-price waterfall analysis used for?

**Level:** Intermediate

Pocket-price waterfall analysis shows how list prices erode to the actual cash kept by the seller. It exposes where discounts, rebates, freight, and service costs leak margin. Companies use it to identify fixable leaks and redesign pricing governance.

**Real-life applications:**
- Industrial firm margin projects.
- Pharma pricing committee reviews.
- Distributor deal audits.
- Consumer goods trade-spend reviews.

**Key concepts:** `pocket price`, `waterfall`, `leakage`, `margin`, `governance`

## Q94. How do subscription models complicate pricing?

**Level:** Intermediate

Subscriptions tie revenue to retention, so pricing must balance acquisition, upgrade, and churn. A price that wins signups may drive later churn; a price that slows signups may protect long-term revenue. Pricing teams must think in cohorts, not transactions.

**Real-life applications:**
- SaaS cohort retention analysis.
- Streaming price-change churn tests.
- Meal-kit retention pricing.
- Media subscription trials.

**Key concepts:** `subscription`, `cohort`, `churn`, `LTV`, `signup rate`

## Q95. How should a pricing team use A/B tests responsibly?

**Level:** Intermediate

Tests should be short, clearly controlled, and segment-consistent to avoid exposing one buyer to both prices. Ethical guardrails matter — sensitive customer groups should be excluded. Clean statistical power prevents firms from chasing noise.

**Real-life applications:**
- E-commerce carefully isolated tests.
- SaaS landing-page experiments.
- App paywall tests.
- Subscription price-change cohorts.

**Key concepts:** `A/B testing`, `ethics`, `control`, `statistical power`, `responsibility`

## Q96. How does price elasticity change over the product life cycle?

**Level:** Intermediate

New products often have low elasticity because early adopters are less price-sensitive. As competition rises and the product matures, elasticity increases. Late-stage products are usually highly elastic as features become commoditized.

**Real-life applications:**
- Smartphone flagship vs older models.
- Pharma pre- and post-patent.
- Streaming services post-launch.
- SaaS category as it matures.

**Key concepts:** `life cycle`, `maturity`, `elasticity`, `adopters`, `commoditization`

## Q98. How do airlines use fare fences?

**Level:** Intermediate

Fences like Saturday-night stay, advance purchase, and change fees separate leisure from business travelers. Business travelers pay more for flexibility; leisure travelers accept restrictions for lower fares. Fences keep each segment in its lane and maximize total revenue.

**Real-life applications:**
- Saturday-stay discount fares.
- 21-day advance purchase rules.
- Non-refundable tickets.
- Basic economy restrictions.

**Key concepts:** `fare fence`, `segmentation`, `restriction`, `airline RM`, `leisure vs business`

## Q103. What role does elasticity play in promotional depth?

**Level:** Intermediate

Promotional depth — the size of the discount — must match elasticity. Highly elastic products need smaller cuts to move volume; inelastic products respond poorly regardless of depth. Over-cutting elastic products burns margin needlessly.

**Real-life applications:**
- Grocery chain standard promo depths.
- Fashion markdown ladders.
- Electronics Black Friday depths.
- CPG trade-spend decisions.

**Key concepts:** `depth`, `elasticity`, `promo design`, `margin`, `volume lift`

## Q104. How should a firm decide whether to launch a freemium tier?

**Level:** Intermediate

The decision hinges on whether free users can be converted affordably to paid and whether the free tier raises awareness without cannibalizing paid. Freemium fits products with strong network effects or low marginal cost. It fails when free users never convert.

**Real-life applications:**
- Slack freemium success.
- Dropbox freemium success.
- Failed freemium startups.
- Gaming free-to-play models.

Outcome pricing is chosen when the firm can measure and attribute results — sales closed, patients treated, leads delivered. Buyers like it because cost ties to value. Sellers need strong tracking and risk appetite; bad outcomes mean no revenue.

**Real-life applications:**
- Marketing agency ad-spend attribution.
- Healthcare pay-for-outcomes.

- Ad-tech performance deals.

## Q106. How does CAC affect pricing strategy?

**Level:** Intermediate

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) sets the floor on sustainable pricing. If pricing cannot recover CAC plus serve costs within a target payback, the model breaks. Firms tune pricing tiers and retention to push LTV-to-CAC ratio above 3.

**Real-life applications:**
- SaaS LTV-to-CAC targets.
- Consumer DTC startups.
- Telecom acquisition pricing.
- Subscription media cost models.

**Key concepts:** `CAC`, `LTV ratio`, `payback period`, `unit economics`, `sustainability`

## Q109. How should a firm balance speed and governance in pricing?

**Level:** Intermediate

Speed lets firms respond to markets; governance prevents chaos. The answer is tiered authority — small changes auto-execute, medium changes need a check, large changes need committee review. Good systems make most changes fast and slow only a handful.

**Real-life applications:**
- Retailer auto-reprice with thresholds.
- SaaS deal desks with fast-track deals.
- Airline RM with pricing committees.
- Hotel RM with exception overrides.

**Key concepts:** `speed`, `governance`, `tiered authority`, `auto-approval`, `exception`

**Real-life applications:**
- Pharma wholesaler rebates.
- Consumer-goods trade programs.

## Q113. How do firms price during inflation?

**Level:** Intermediate

Firms pass along cost rises selectively — more aggressively on inelastic goods, softer on elastic ones. They often shrink packs (shrinkflation), add value rather than cut prices, and communicate transparently to protect trust. Blanket price hikes usually backfire.

**Real-life applications:**
- CPG shrinkflation tactics.
- Restaurant menu re-engineering.
- Retail selective price hikes.
- Industrial surcharges on fuel.

**Key concepts:** `inflation`, `cost pass-through`, `shrinkflation`, `communication`, `elasticity`

## Q114. What are the ethical limits of anchoring?

**Real-life applications:**
- UK retail fake-sale crackdowns.
- US FTC false-price rulings.
- EU consumer protection cases.
- Retailer internal anchor audits.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices
The frameworks below synthesize the chapter's themes into decision tools [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Pricing Regime Selector
**Purpose:** Choose the pricing model that fits the product and market.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
commodity -> cost-plus
differentiated value -> value-based
early launch -> skimming or penetration
fixed capacity -> revenue management
large SKU set -> ML pricing
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: differentiation - whether the product can justify value-based pricing.
Component 2: demand shape - whether demand is elastic, fixed-capacity, or launch-stage.
Component 3: operational complexity - whether the team can support dynamic or ML-driven pricing.
Component 4: customer trust - whether the market will accept the logic.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A software product with strong ROI evidence should migrate from cost-plus to value-based pricing, while a hotel should use revenue management because capacity is fixed and perishable.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is debating a pricing model instead of a price point.

### Elasticity and Promotion Grid
**Purpose:** Decide how deep promotions should be and which products deserve them.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
high elasticity / high margin risk -> shallow promo
high elasticity / low margin risk -> deeper promo
low elasticity / high margin risk -> avoid promo
low elasticity / low margin risk -> test carefully
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: elasticity - how strongly demand moves with price.
Component 2: margin risk - how much gross margin a discount destroys.
Component 3: volume lift - whether the promo actually brings incremental sales.
Component 4: cannibalization - whether the promo just shifts existing demand.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A grocery SKU with high elasticity can absorb a sharper promotion, but a low-elasticity SaaS add-on should usually not be discounted just to chase volume.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the promo calendar is getting crowded and no one has checked lift.

### Revenue Management and Leakage Loop
**Purpose:** Protect value when pricing meets capacity, discounting, and late-booking demand.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
booking curve -> fare fence -> discount control -> realization -> no-show / leak review
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: booking curve - when demand arrives.
Component 2: fare fence - how segments are separated.
Component 3: realization - the net price actually kept.
Component 4: leakage review - how much margin is lost to exceptions, rebates, or mistakes.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A hotel closes low fares early, uses a stay restriction, and then checks whether late business bookings and no-show data justify the remaining inventory policy.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When capacity is fixed and the team wants to discount too early.

### Fairness and Trust Filter
**Purpose:** Stop pricing tactics that are technically clever but commercially destructive.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
clear reason + transparent fence + normal expectations -> acceptable
hidden logic + sensitive proxies + surprise pricing -> reject
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: reasonableness - can the customer understand why the price differs?
Component 2: transparency - is the rule visible?
Component 3: sensitivity - does the policy rely on protected or suspicious data?
Component 4: trust impact - will this create backlash or compliance risk?

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A dynamic-price model that jumps during demand spikes may be acceptable if disclosed and capped. A personalized price that quietly depends on sensitive proxies should be blocked.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When legal, brand, and growth teams are fighting over a pricing move.

## 4. Formulas
The source is strategy-heavy, so the formulas below are practical pricing metrics [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Elasticity
Formula: `Elasticity = % change in quantity / % change in price`
Variables:
quantity = units sold
price = selling price
Why this formula exists: It answers whether a price change will help or hurt revenue.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 1 -> inelastic; price changes have smaller quantity effects
Value 1-2 -> moderately elastic; promotions can move volume
Value > 2 -> highly elastic; discounts move demand strongly
Worked example with numbers: If price falls 10% and quantity rises 15%, elasticity is 1.5. Decision: a promotion may be worthwhile if margin can survive the cut.

### Formula 2: Realization Rate
Formula: `Realization Rate = net price / list price`
Variables:
net price = price after discounts, rebates, and concessions
list price = headline price
Why this formula exists: It answers how much of the quoted price is actually kept.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.80 -> heavy leakage
Value 0.80-0.95 -> normal in many B2B and segment-pricing settings
Value > 0.95 -> strong price capture
Worked example with numbers: If list price is 100 and net price is 88, realization is 88%. Decision: audit discounts, rebates, and approval behavior. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 3: Discount Leakage Rate
Formula: `Discount Leakage Rate = unauthorized discount value / total discount value`
Variables:
unauthorized discount value = discount outside policy
total discount value = all discounts granted
Why this formula exists: It answers whether discount governance is working.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.05 -> controlled
Value 0.05-0.10 -> needs review
Value > 0.10 -> pricing governance is weak
Worked example with numbers: If 50,000 of 400,000 in discounts are off-policy, leakage is 12.5%. Decision: tighten approvals and clean up the waterfall.

### Formula 4: CLV-to-Price Ratio
Formula: `CLV-to-Price Ratio = customer lifetime value / initial price`
Variables:
customer lifetime value = expected future margin from the customer
initial price = first purchase or entry price
Why this formula exists: It answers whether a low introductory price is justified by downstream value.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 2 -> the entry price may be too aggressive
Value 2-5 -> workable for many subscription and SaaS models
Value > 5 -> you can afford a lower entry price if retention is strong
Worked example with numbers: If CLV is 600 and entry price is 120, ratio is 5. Decision: a lower entry tier is acceptable if churn stays under control.

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Software Firm Moving to Value-Based Pricing
Situation: A software firm keeps hearing that its product is cheap relative to the ROI it creates. The product has measurable customer value, but the pricing model is still cost-plus.
Applicable framework/metric: Pricing Regime Selector and CLV-to-Price Ratio.
Analysis: If CLV is 500 and the entry price is 100, the ratio is 5. That suggests the business can support a higher-value, outcome-based model if churn remains stable. The move should be tied to differentiation and not just price hiking. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If ROI is visible and the sales team hears "this is cheap," migrate away from cost-plus. If the new value is hard to prove, keep the simpler model until evidence improves.
Action: Rewrite packaging around outcomes, test a higher-value tier with existing customers, and compare renewal behavior.

### Scenario 2: Hotel and Airline Revenue Control
Situation: A hotel and a route-based travel business need to protect capacity for late-booking buyers. Low fares are filling up early, and revenue management is the only thing stopping dilution.
Applicable framework/metric: Revenue Management and Leakage Loop and Realization Rate.
Analysis: If low fares remain open too long, realization falls even when occupancy looks good. Tight fences, booking-curve closeouts, and weekly no-show updates keep the inventory for premium buyers.
Decision rule: If the booking curve is ahead of plan, close cheap fare classes. If realization is dropping, check leakage. If no-shows rise, allow limited overbooking.
Action: Close low fare classes earlier, monitor discounts by channel, and tie policy updates to the latest pickup data.

### Scenario 3: Retailer With Heavy Promotions
Situation: A retailer is running frequent markdowns and cannot tell whether they are profitable. Promotions are lifting traffic, but margin is under pressure.
Applicable framework/metric: Elasticity and Promotion Grid and Discount Leakage Rate.
Analysis: If a 10% price cut yields only a 2% quantity increase, elasticity is 0.2 and the promo is weak. If 12.5% of discounts are off-policy, leakage is high enough to hurt the P&L even before considering cannibalization. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: Deepen promotions only on elastic, high-margin SKUs. If leakage is above 10%, stop and fix governance. If the promo is just shifting the same buyers around, stop using it as growth.
Action: Rebuild the promo calendar SKU by SKU and audit the waterfall every week.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Segment the portfolio by elasticity, WTP, and trust sensitivity before setting price rules.
2. Decide whether each offer is cost-plus, value-based, skimming, penetration, or dynamic.
3. Build the approval waterfall so discounts cannot leak without trace.
4. Tie hotel, airline, or event pricing to the booking curve and no-show data.
5. Run A/B tests and competitor monitoring in controlled windows, not as constant chaos.
6. Review annual price increases, multi-year contracts, and working-capital effects together.
7. Audit fairness, transparency, and edge-case complaints before scaling any algorithmic pricing.
