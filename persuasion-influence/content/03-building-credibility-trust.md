# Building Credibility and Trust

## Overview
Credibility and trust are the foundation on which all persuasion rests. If your audience does not believe you are competent and honest, no technique will land. Building trust is a slow, deliberate process that combines expertise, reliability, and genuine concern for others. Once established, it makes every future persuasion attempt significantly easier.

---

## Why It Matters
In the workplace, your ability to influence hinges on your reputation. People evaluate your track record, consistency, and intentions before they agree to anything. A single broken promise can undo years of credibility, while steady follow-through builds a compounding advantage that opens doors no pitch deck ever could.

## Key Principles
- Demonstrate competence by sharing relevant knowledge and delivering results consistently
- Be transparent about limitations and uncertainties rather than overpromising
- Follow through on commitments, no matter how small, to build a track record of reliability
- Show genuine interest in others' goals and well-being, not just your own agenda

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Credibility** | The quality of being trusted and believed in, based on demonstrated expertise and character |
| **Trust** | A firm belief in the reliability, truth, and ability of someone, built over repeated interactions |
| **Goodwill** | The perception that a communicator has the audience's best interests at heart |
| **Competence** | The demonstrated ability to perform tasks effectively and deliver on promises |

## Use Case
A new team lead builds credibility in the first 90 days by listening to each team member, delivering two quick wins, and openly admitting what she does not yet know about the product.

## Scenario
> James joined a cross-functional committee where no one knew his work. Instead of leading with opinions, he spent the first three meetings asking thoughtful questions and volunteering to handle a data analysis nobody wanted. By month two, the committee actively sought his input on strategic decisions because he had proven he was both capable and dependable.

## Examples
- Sharing credit for a successful project with the entire team increases perceived goodwill and trustworthiness
- Admitting a mistake early and presenting a fix builds more credibility than hiding the error ever could

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Credibility Triangle
**Purpose:** Build persuasion on competence, honesty, and goodwill instead of personality alone.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
competence + honesty + goodwill -> credibility
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Competence: proof you can do the work.
Honesty: proof you tell the truth about limits and tradeoffs.
Goodwill: proof you care about the other side's goals.
Credibility: the combined effect that makes people believe your recommendations.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A new AI product lead builds credibility by explaining the model limits honestly, shipping two quick wins, and helping sales teams answer customer objections [verified from model knowledge, not source]. The decision is to prove capability and intent before asking for bigger influence.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When people are not rejecting the idea but are still unsure whether to trust the messenger.

### Trust Flywheel
**Purpose:** Show how trust compounds with repeated follow-through.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
commitment -> delivery -> confidence -> bigger commitment -> stronger trust
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Commitment size: the promise made to the audience.
Delivery quality: whether the promise is actually kept.
Confidence growth: whether the audience becomes more willing to rely on you.
Compounding effect: how one small success improves the next persuasion attempt.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting manager promises a client a clean dashboard by Friday, delivers it, and then gets approval for a larger analytics workstream [verified from model knowledge, not source]. The decision is to use small, reliable wins to unlock larger ones.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the relationship is still new and you need to build momentum.

### Transparency and Expectation Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide how much uncertainty to disclose and how to frame it.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                     Certainty
                 Low                      High
Transparency +----------------+------------------------+
Low          | Spin / surprise| False confidence       |
High         | Honest caution  | Clear and credible     |
             +----------------+------------------------+
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Transparency: how openly you share limits, risks, and unknowns.
Certainty: how much is actually known.
Expectation management: whether the audience is being set up for disappointment.
Trust effect: whether candor increases or decreases confidence.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product lead tells executives that the release is 80% ready, the remaining 20% is a known integration risk, and the date depends on that risk clearing [verified from model knowledge, not source]. The decision is to be precise about uncertainty instead of disguising it as certainty.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When overpromising would create a larger credibility problem later.

### First 90 Days Reputation Canvas
**Purpose:** Build a concrete early-plan for a new leader's credibility.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
listen -> learn -> quick wins -> visible reliability -> trust
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Listening: understanding the team and the context before declaring a plan.
Quick wins: small, visible actions that show competence.
Reliability: keeping promises and showing up consistently.
Trust transfer: the point where the team begins to assume good intent.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A new engineering leader spends the first month listening to release blockers, then ships one tooling fix and one process fix so the team can see early value [verified from model knowledge, not source]. The decision is to earn trust through action, not announcements.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a new leader needs to set tone without overreaching.

## 4. Formulas

The source is conceptual, so the formulas below are managerial tools expanded from model knowledge, not the source. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 1: Credibility Score
Formula: `credibility = competence x reliability x goodwill`
Variables:
`competence` = ability to deliver quality work
`reliability` = ability to keep commitments
`goodwill` = perceived concern for the audience's interests
Why this formula exists: It answers whether people will take your advice seriously.
How to interpret the output:
Low on any factor -> credibility drops sharply -> fix the weakest dimension first
Balanced and high -> strong influence -> use carefully and consistently
High competence with low goodwill -> technically respected but resisted
Worked example with numbers: If competence is 0.9, reliability is 0.8, and goodwill is 0.7, credibility is 0.50. Decision: improve transparency and follow-through before asking for a bigger ask. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 2: Trust Deposit
Formula: `trust deposit = promises kept - promises broken`
Variables:
`promises kept` = commitments delivered on time
`promises broken` = commitments missed or altered without explanation
Why this formula exists: It answers whether trust is accumulating or being spent.
How to interpret the output:
Positive -> trust is building -> keep compounding
Zero -> trust is flat -> deliver more consistently
Negative -> trust is eroding -> repair quickly
Worked example with numbers: If a manager keeps 12 promises and breaks 2, trust deposit is +10. Decision: protect that balance by not overcommitting on the next project. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 3: Transparency Gap
Formula: `transparency gap = what you know - what you disclosed`
Variables:
`what you know` = the information you already have
`what you disclosed` = the information the audience actually received
Why this formula exists: It answers whether candor is strong enough to sustain trust.
How to interpret the output:
Small gap -> high candor -> trust tends to rise
Moderate gap -> acceptable if explained -> watch for surprises
Large gap -> high risk of backlash -> disclose more
Worked example with numbers: If a release risk is known internally but not shared with a key customer, the transparency gap is large. Decision: disclose the risk early and state the mitigation plan. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Lead with opinion when nobody knows your work yet. | Lead with competence, listening, and useful follow-through. |
| Overpromise to sound confident. | State limits and uncertainties clearly. |
| Break small commitments and assume they do not matter. | Treat every commitment as a trust deposit or withdrawal. |
| Ask for a big influence ask before earning belief. | Use early wins to unlock larger requests. |
| Hide mistakes until they become visible to everyone. | Admit errors early and show the fix. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: New team lead in the first 90 days
Situation: A new team lead joins a cross-functional product group and needs the team to trust her quickly. The team is skeptical because prior leaders overpromised and underdelivered. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: First 90 Days Reputation Canvas + trust deposit.
Analysis: If the lead keeps 6 early commitments and misses none, the trust deposit is strongly positive. Two quick wins, especially around a recurring pain point, show competence and goodwill faster than a polished speech.
Decision rule: If trust is low, listen and deliver small wins. If trust is moderate, increase visibility and transparency. If trust is high, broaden the mandate.
Action: Schedule one-on-ones, deliver two visible fixes, and publish a weekly update on progress and tradeoffs.

### Scenario 2: Cross-functional committee credibility
Situation: A manager joins a strategic committee where no one knows his work. He needs influence but does not yet have a track record with the group. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Credibility Triangle + credibility score.
Analysis: If competence is strong but goodwill is not yet proven, the credibility score stays limited. Asking thoughtful questions, doing unglamorous analysis, and helping the group save time builds the missing dimensions.
Decision rule: If the audience does not know you, prove reliability first. If they know you but doubt intent, show goodwill. If they know your intent but not your skill, deliver visible quality.
Action: Contribute useful analysis, share credit, and avoid trying to dominate the room too early.

### Scenario 3: Owning a mistake early
Situation: A product manager discovers that a launch metric was reported incorrectly to a client. The team is worried that admitting the error will hurt trust. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Transparency and Expectation Matrix + transparency gap.
Analysis: If the issue is already internally known, hiding it widens the transparency gap and increases the future credibility hit. Early disclosure plus a fix usually preserves more trust than silence.
Decision rule: If the audience will find out anyway, tell them early. If the issue is minor and already corrected, disclose with context. If the issue changes the decision, escalate immediately.
Action: Explain what happened, what is being fixed, and what will prevent recurrence.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Build a reputation plan for the first 90 days before the first meeting.
2. Show competence through small, visible outcomes that solve real pain points.
3. Be explicit about limitations, assumptions, and unknowns.
4. Keep every promise you make, especially the small ones.
5. Ask what matters to the other person before pitching your own agenda.
6. Admit mistakes early and attach a fix to the admission.
7. Track trust as a cumulative asset, not a soft feeling.
