# Cialdini's Principles of Influence

## Overview
Robert Cialdini identified six core principles that explain why people say yes to requests. These principles — reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, and scarcity — operate across cultures and contexts. Understanding them helps you recognize influence attempts and apply ethical persuasion at work. They form the backbone of modern persuasion science.

---

## Why It Matters
Cialdini's principles are the most widely cited framework in persuasion research and practice. Whether you are pitching an idea, closing a deal, or rallying a team, at least one of these six levers is at play. Knowing the framework lets you design more compelling messages and spot when others are using these tactics on you.

## Key Principles
- Reciprocity means people feel obligated to return favors, so giving first creates influence
- Commitment and consistency drive people to align future actions with past promises or public statements
- Social proof shows that people look to others' behavior to decide what is correct, especially under uncertainty
- Scarcity increases perceived value — limited availability or exclusive access makes offers more attractive

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Reciprocity** | The social norm that compels people to repay what they have received from others |
| **Social Proof** | The tendency to view an action as correct because other people are doing it |
| **Authority** | The principle that people defer to experts or those who display symbols of competence and status |
| **Scarcity** | The perception that opportunities become more desirable when their availability is limited |

## Use Case
A product manager uses social proof by sharing adoption metrics from early teams to persuade skeptical departments to try a new workflow tool.

## Scenario
> Maria needed buy-in from the finance team for a new reporting dashboard. She started by helping the finance lead with a data cleanup task at no cost. When she later presented the dashboard proposal, the finance lead felt a genuine urge to reciprocate and championed the project in the next leadership meeting.

## Examples
- Offering a free trial before asking for a subscription leverages reciprocity
- Displaying customer logos on a sales deck uses authority and social proof to build confidence

### Influence Sequence Ladder [verified from model knowledge, not source]
**Purpose:** Choose the order of influence tactics instead of throwing all six levers at once.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
give value -> create commitment -> show peers -> add authority -> reinforce liking -> create urgency
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Value, meaning reciprocity starts the sequence.  
Component 2: Commitment, meaning a small public yes can open the next step.  
Component 3: Proof and credibility, meaning social proof and authority increase confidence.  
Component 4: Relationship and timing, meaning liking and scarcity close the gap to action.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A product leader first helps the finance team with a data cleanup task, then asks for a dashboard pilot, then shows that two peer teams have adopted it, then uses the data team's endorsement to accelerate sign-off. The ladder keeps the ask logical and ethical.

When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when one-shot persuasion is failing and you need a sequence.

## 4. Formulas

### Persuasion Effectiveness Index [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `PEI = desired action / audience exposure`

Variables:
`desired action` = the number of people who actually did what you asked.  
`audience exposure` = the number of people who saw the message.  

Why this formula exists: It answers whether the message is driving action or just attention.

How to interpret the output:
Value `< 0.10` -> weak influence -> adjust the lever or the audience.  
Value `0.10 to 0.30` -> decent -> refine the message and proof points.  
Value `> 0.30` -> strong -> scale the approach carefully and keep it ethical.

Worked example with numbers: [verified from model knowledge, not source] If 40 of 200 stakeholders approve a workflow tool, PEI = `0.20`. That suggests the message works for some audiences but needs stronger proof or a better lever.

### Reciprocity Return Rate [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `return rate = favorable response / first favor given`

Variables:
`favorable response` = helpful actions, sign-offs, referrals, or adoption after the first favor.  
`first favor given` = the initial value offered first.  

Why this formula exists: It answers whether giving first is actually leading to return behavior.

How to interpret the output:
Value `< 1` -> weak reciprocity -> value offer or audience fit may be off.  
Value `1 to 2` -> healthy -> the favor is opening doors.  
Value `> 2` -> very strong -> keep the relationship warm and avoid over-asking.

Worked example with numbers: [verified from model knowledge, not source] If a manager helps a finance lead clean up data and gets two project champions in return, the return rate is `2.0`. That is strong enough to justify the initial help.

### Scarcity Lift [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `scarcity lift = action rate with scarcity / action rate without scarcity`

Variables:
`action rate with scarcity` = conversion when time or availability is limited.  
`action rate without scarcity` = conversion without that limitation.  

Why this formula exists: It answers whether limited availability is actually motivating action or just creating pressure.

How to interpret the output:
Value `< 1` -> scarcity is hurting trust or fit.  
Value `1 to 1.2` -> modest benefit -> use sparingly.  
Value `> 1.2` -> strong lift -> scarcity is working and may justify a deadline.

Worked example with numbers: [verified from model knowledge, not source] If a pilot offer converts at `18%` with a deadline and `12%` without one, scarcity lift is `1.5`. That is a meaningful lift, but only if the deadline is real.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Throw every persuasion tactic at every audience. | Pick the lever that fits the audience's uncertainty and decision style. |
| Use scarcity when the real issue is trust. | Build social proof or authority first if credibility is missing. |
| Ask for a big commitment without any prior value. | Start with reciprocity and a small, useful favor. |
| Pretend manipulation is just strong persuasion. | Check the ethical persuasion risk before you push. |
| Use testimonials that are vague or non-credible. | Use real, relevant proof from peers or customers. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Workflow tool adoption across departments
Situation: A product manager wants skeptical departments to try a new workflow tool. The org is uncertain, and teams are waiting for proof that the tool will actually help.  
Applicable framework/metric: Influence Lever Selection Map; social proof.  
Analysis: [verified from model knowledge, not source] Early teams' adoption metrics show that three peer groups already use the tool. Showing those numbers and a short testimonial can shift the audience from uncertainty to action more effectively than a generic pitch.  
Decision rule: If uncertainty is high, lead with social proof or authority; if commitment is already public, use consistency; if people already like the champion, use liking.  
Action: Publish the early-team adoption metrics, invite a pilot group, and give them a low-risk implementation path.

### Scenario 2: Finance team buy-in for a dashboard
Situation: An analytics lead needs the finance team to approve a reporting dashboard. The finance lead is skeptical, but the analytics team can do a useful cleanup task first.  
Applicable framework/metric: Influence Sequence Ladder; reciprocity return rate.  
Analysis: [verified from model knowledge, not source] If the cleanup task creates two champions after one favor, the reciprocity return rate is `2.0`. That means the initial help has opened the door to a stronger ask.  
Decision rule: If reciprocity is weak, improve the initial favor; if commitment is already public, use consistency; if authority is needed, bring in a trusted finance peer.  
Action: Deliver the cleanup, then ask for a small dashboard pilot rather than a full rollout.

### Scenario 3: Limited-time launch offer
Situation: A consulting team is promoting a training workshop with a limited registration window. The team wants to know if scarcity is helping or just creating noise.  
Applicable framework/metric: Ethical Persuasion Risk Matrix; scarcity lift.  
Analysis: [verified from model knowledge, not source] If conversion rises from `12%` to `18%` when the deadline is real, scarcity lift is `1.5`. That is useful, but the team should avoid fake urgency or the tactic will backfire.  
Decision rule: If scarcity lift is below `1`, remove the deadline; if `1 to 1.2`, use it carefully; if above `1.2`, keep the urgency but stay truthful.  
Action: Publish the real deadline, explain what is limited, and only use urgency if the offer truly closes.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Diagnose whether the audience needs trust, proof, commitment, or urgency.
2. Start with reciprocity when you need the relationship to open.
3. Use commitment and consistency after a small public yes.
4. Add social proof and authority when uncertainty is the main barrier.
5. Use liking to strengthen collaboration, not to mask a weak proposal.
6. Use scarcity only when the limit is real and the offer is genuinely time-bound.
7. Review every tactic against the ethical persuasion risk before sending it.
