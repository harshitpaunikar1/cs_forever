# Administrative Decision-Making Practice

## Overview

Administrative decision-making is the day-to-day practice of choosing between options with incomplete information and limited time. It is different from one-off strategic choices, because it happens constantly at every level. Good practice means following a clear method, not relying on gut alone.

---

## Why It Matters

Most business value is created or lost in ordinary decisions, not in dramatic ones. A manager who makes sound routine choices builds a team that trusts her judgment. A manager who flips flops or avoids deciding creates chaos even with great strategy.

## Key Principles

- Define the real problem before generating options. Many bad decisions solve the wrong question.
- List at least three options. Binary choices usually hide better alternatives.
- Weigh options against clear criteria: cost, risk, time, impact, and fit.
- Accept "good enough" when perfect is too slow. Satisficing beats paralysis.
- Document the decision and the reason, so learning compounds over time.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Administrative Decision** | A routine managerial choice about operations, people, or resources. |
| **Bounded Rationality** | The idea that managers decide with limited information, time, and brainpower. |
| **Satisficing** | Picking the first option that meets the minimum acceptable standard. |
| **Decision Criteria** | The measurable tests used to compare options. |
| **Decision Log** | A written record of what was chosen, why, and what was expected. |

## Use Case

A branch manager at a regional bank must decide how to staff peak customer hours. She maps three options against service time, cost, and staff fatigue, then picks a split-shift model that trades a small cost increase for faster service.

## Scenario

> A restaurant chain could not decide whether to close its weakest store or rebrand it. The regional head created a one-page comparison of cost, brand risk, and time. The rebrand option clearly scored lower once actual data replaced opinion. The store closed, and the team redirected energy to the top five locations.

## Examples

- A logistics lead turns a weekly "what route" debate into a one-page decision template and cuts meeting time in half.
- A sales manager writes a short decision memo every time she overrides a policy, creating a history that protects her when audit questions arise.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Decision Score
Formula: `decision score = (impact + fit) - (cost + risk + time)`
Variables:
`impact` = business value created
`fit` = alignment with goals and constraints
`cost` = money or effort required
`risk` = downside exposure
`time` = delay or elapsed effort
Why this formula exists: It answers which option produces the best tradeoff under constraints.
How to interpret the output:
Negative score -> poor choice unless there is no alternative
Near zero -> acceptable but not compelling
Positive score -> strong candidate for action
Worked example with numbers: If a support automation option scores impact 8, fit 7, cost 4, risk 3, and time 2, the score is 6. Decision: move ahead, because the value outweighs the burden. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 2: Decision Confidence
Formula: `decision confidence = criteria met / total criteria`
Variables:
`criteria met` = number of decision criteria the option satisfies
`total criteria` = all criteria in the matrix
Why this formula exists: It answers how many of the key tests the option passes.
How to interpret the output:
Low ratio -> keep looking
Moderate ratio -> likely good enough, but test one weak spot
High ratio -> proceed
Worked example with numbers: If an option meets 4 of 5 criteria, confidence is 0.8. Decision: choose it if the remaining gap is not mission-critical. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

### Formula 3: Log Completeness
Formula: `log completeness = recorded reason + expected outcome + review date`
Variables:
`recorded reason` = why the choice was made
`expected outcome` = what success should look like
`review date` = when the decision should be revisited
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the decision can be learned from later.
How to interpret the output:
Missing one element -> partial traceability
Missing two -> weak governance
All three -> useful decision record
Worked example with numbers: A policy override is logged with rationale, expected service impact, and a 30-day review date. Decision: this is auditable and easy to revisit. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Start with the solution you already like. | Define the real problem first. |
| Compare only two options. | Force at least three options onto the table. |
| Treat criteria as vague preferences. | Write down and weight the criteria explicitly. |
| Wait for perfect information. | Satisfice when the minimum bar is met. |
| Make decisions you cannot explain later. | Log the reason and expected outcome. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Peak-hour staffing in a bank branch
Situation: A branch manager must staff customer peaks with limited headcount. The obvious option is more staffing, but cost, fatigue, and service speed all matter. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Criteria Matrix and Decision Score.
Analysis: If the split-shift model gives high service impact and acceptable fit while staying within cost limits, it can beat the default full-shift option. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If the score is positive and the risk is manageable, choose the option. If scores are close, favor the one with better fit and lower fatigue. If all scores are low, revisit the problem definition.
Action: Compare three staffing patterns in a one-page matrix and pick the highest-scoring option.

### Scenario 2: AI feature launch tradeoff
Situation: A product team is deciding whether to launch an AI feature now, delay it, or reduce scope. Leadership is split because speed matters, but quality risk is also real. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Decision Funnel and Decision Confidence.
Analysis: If the chosen option clears most criteria but only barely on reliability, it may still be acceptable if the threshold is met and the remaining risk is contained. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If confidence is above 80%, proceed. If it is between 60% and 80%, run a narrow pilot. If it is below 60%, keep options open.
Action: Document the tradeoff, launch a narrow pilot, and log the expected rollback condition.

### Scenario 3: Store close or rebrand decision
Situation: A consulting-style regional leader must decide whether to close a weak store or rebrand it. One path is emotionally easier, but the data may point elsewhere. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Applicable framework/metric: Satisficing Threshold Map and Log Completeness.
Analysis: If one option clearly beats the threshold on cost, risk, and impact, waiting for a perfect answer adds delay without value. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If an option clears the threshold, accept it. If none do, keep iterating. If the decision will matter later, log the rationale now.
Action: Build a one-page decision memo, choose the threshold-clearing option, and set a review date.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. State the problem in one sentence before listing options.
2. Require at least three options for any non-trivial choice.
3. Write the criteria and weights before comparing alternatives.
4. Choose the first option that clears the minimum bar when time is limited.
5. Log the decision, the reason, and the expected result.
6. Review the outcome later and adjust the criteria if needed.
