# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and concepts in organizational communication. Questions Q1 through Q70.

## Q1. What is an organization?

**Level:** Basic

An organization is a group of people who work together in a coordinated way to achieve shared goals. It has some structure, rules, and division of work. Without coordination, a collection of people is just a crowd, not an organization.

**Real-life applications:**
- Setting up a new team with clear roles before a product launch
- Designing a charity with a board, director, and volunteers for a cause
- Building a small startup with defined founder responsibilities
- Structuring a school with departments, teachers, and a principal

**Key concepts:** `organization`, `coordination`, `shared goals`, `structure`, `division of work`

---

## Q2. What is communication in an organizational context?

**Level:** Basic

Communication in an organization is the flow of information, instructions, feelings, and feedback between people to get work done. It uses formal channels like email and meetings and informal channels like hallway chats. Its purpose is to align action, not just transfer words.

**Real-life applications:**
- A CEO sending a weekly update to all employees
- A team chat where a developer asks for help on a bug
- A supervisor giving verbal instructions on the shop floor
- A department-wide memo announcing a policy change

**Key concepts:** `communication`, `information flow`, `formal channels`, `informal channels`, `alignment`

---

## Q3. What are the basic elements of the communication process?

**Level:** Basic

The communication process has a sender, a message, an encoding step, a channel, a decoding step, a receiver, and feedback. The sender has an idea and turns it into words or signals. The receiver decodes the message and responds. Noise can distort any step.

**Real-life applications:**
- An email where the sender encodes and the reader decodes the content
- A video call where facial expressions add to the spoken message
- A poster campaign where the brand is the sender and the customer decodes it
- A customer survey that creates a feedback loop to management

**Key concepts:** `sender`, `message`, `channel`, `receiver`, `feedback`

---

## Q4. What is a formal communication channel?

**Level:** Basic

A formal channel is an official, recognized route for messages inside an organization. It includes tools like memos, official emails, scheduled meetings, and policy documents. Formal channels carry authority and are usually recorded.

**Real-life applications:**
- A weekly team meeting with an agenda and minutes
- A company-wide HR announcement about new benefits
- A performance review document shared with the employee
- A board report sent to all directors

**Key concepts:** `formal channel`, `authority`, `documentation`, `hierarchy`, `official communication`

---

## Q5. What is an informal communication channel?

**Level:** Basic

An informal channel is an unofficial route of communication that runs between people regardless of rank. It includes hallway chats, lunch conversations, and group chats outside work. It often moves faster than formal channels but is less reliable.

**Real-life applications:**
- A pantry conversation where a rumor about layoffs starts spreading
- A casual chat group where colleagues share project tips
- A friendship between two managers that helps align two departments
- A quick WhatsApp ping from a junior to a senior to clarify a task

**Key concepts:** `informal channel`, `grapevine`, `peer network`, `trust`, `speed`

---

## Q6. What is "noise" in communication?

**Level:** Basic

Noise is anything that gets in the way of a message being received clearly. It can be physical like a bad phone line, or mental like stress, bias, or lack of interest. Noise is always present, so good communicators plan for it.

**Real-life applications:**
- A loud factory floor making verbal instructions hard to hear
- A manager receiving feedback while distracted by deadlines
- Jargon-filled emails that confuse new hires
- Poor video quality in a remote meeting lowering understanding

**Key concepts:** `noise`, `distortion`, `barriers`, `clarity`, `perception`

---

## Q7. What is feedback in the communication process?

**Level:** Basic

Feedback is the response the receiver sends back to the sender after getting a message. It helps the sender confirm that the message was understood correctly. Without feedback, communication is one-way and prone to errors.

**Real-life applications:**
- A nod from a team member during instructions
- A reply email confirming a meeting request
- A short questions-answered check at the end of a training session
- A customer rating on a support call

**Key concepts:** `feedback`, `confirmation`, `two-way communication`, `active listening`, `understanding`

---

## Q8. What does it mean to say organizations are "living systems"?

**Level:** Basic

It means organizations are not static charts but dynamic networks of people, relationships, and information that change every day. People come and go, roles shift, and relationships evolve. Treating an organization as living helps managers respond rather than impose.

**Real-life applications:**
- Noticing energy shifts after a new manager joins
- Watching team culture change when a key person leaves
- Adapting meeting rhythms as a company grows
- Rebalancing workload as seasonal demand shifts

**Key concepts:** `living system`, `dynamic`, `adaptation`, `relationships`, `culture`

---

## Q9. What is the role of communication in coordinating work?

**Level:** Basic

Communication is how tasks, priorities, and dependencies are shared so different people do the right work at the right time. Without it, people duplicate effort or miss steps. Coordination is essentially applied communication.

**Real-life applications:**
- A morning standup aligning a development team's day
- A handoff email between two shifts on the factory floor
- A shared project tracker updated by multiple teams
- A weekly sync between marketing and sales on campaign status

**Key concepts:** `coordination`, `alignment`, `dependencies`, `workflow`, `handoff`

---

## Q10. What is the grapevine in an organization?

**Level:** Basic

The grapevine is the informal network through which rumors and unofficial news travel among employees. It often carries real signals before the formal channel does. Smart managers listen to it instead of ignoring it.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hearing about a potential reorganization before official news
- Learning staff concerns about a new tool before the survey result
- Noticing morale signals during a stressful quarter
- Picking up early word about a strong or weak new hire

**Key concepts:** `grapevine`, `informal network`, `rumors`, `morale signals`, `employee voice`

---

## Q11. What is downward communication?

**Level:** Basic

Downward communication flows from higher levels of the organization to lower levels. It carries instructions, policies, goals, and feedback. It is the most common flow in traditional companies.

**Real-life applications:**
- A CEO announcing the year's goals in a town hall
- A manager setting targets for direct reports
- HR sending out a new leave policy
- A team lead explaining a new process to the team

**Key concepts:** `downward communication`, `directives`, `hierarchy`, `policy`, `instruction`

---

## Q12. What is upward communication?

**Level:** Basic

Upward communication flows from lower levels up to senior managers. It carries reports, concerns, ideas, and feedback. Healthy organizations actively invite upward flow.

**Real-life applications:**
- An engineer raising a risk to the project manager
- A suggestion box for line workers
- A skip-level meeting with the VP
- An annual employee engagement survey

**Key concepts:** `upward communication`, `voice`, `reporting`, `feedback loop`, `escalation`

---

## Q13. What is horizontal communication?

**Level:** Basic

Horizontal communication happens between people at the same level across different teams or functions. It helps coordinate across silos. Without it, departments drift out of sync.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two project managers sharing resource plans
- Finance and operations agreeing on month-end cut-offs
- Peer reviews between engineers of different teams
- A cross-functional squad meeting to launch a product

**Key concepts:** `horizontal communication`, `peer coordination`, `cross-functional`, `silos`, `alignment`

---

## Q14. What are behavioral sciences in the workplace?

**Level:** Basic

Behavioral sciences study human behavior, including psychology, sociology, and anthropology. Applied to the workplace, they help explain how people think, feel, and act at work. Managers use these insights to design better teams and systems.

**Real-life applications:**
- Using personality research to build balanced teams
- Applying group behavior models to design meetings
- Using motivation theory to design reward systems
- Applying bias awareness to improve hiring fairness

**Key concepts:** `behavioral science`, `psychology`, `sociology`, `behavior`, `workplace`

---

## Q15. What is perception in communication?

**Level:** Basic

Perception is how a person interprets what they see, hear, or read. Two people can receive the same message and walk away with different meanings. This is because perception is shaped by past experience, values, and mood.

**Real-life applications:**
- Staff reading a policy as punishment while leaders see it as fair
- A compliment taken as sarcasm due to past tension
- A short email read as rude by one person and efficient by another
- A brand ad landing differently across age groups

**Key concepts:** `perception`, `interpretation`, `filters`, `experience`, `meaning`

---

## Q16. What is attitude in organizational behavior?

**Level:** Basic

An attitude is a stable feeling or belief about a person, topic, or situation. It shapes how someone responds. Attitudes are learned, so they can change, but they change slowly.

**Real-life applications:**
- A positive attitude toward training raising course completion
- A negative attitude toward a new tool delaying adoption
- Customer-first attitude showing up in service quality
- Attitudes toward safety affecting accident rates

**Key concepts:** `attitude`, `belief`, `behavior`, `learned response`, `change`

---

## Q17. What is a cognitive bias?

**Level:** Basic

A cognitive bias is a mental shortcut that can lead to errors in judgment. Our brains use shortcuts to save energy, but these shortcuts can distort how we read people and situations. Being aware of bias reduces its harm.

**Real-life applications:**
- Confirmation bias in hiring when managers favor similar candidates
- Recency bias in performance reviews
- Anchoring bias in salary negotiations
- Availability bias during risk assessments

**Key concepts:** `cognitive bias`, `mental shortcut`, `judgment`, `decision-making`, `awareness`

---

## Q18. What is a social norm in a workplace?

**Level:** Basic

A social norm is an unwritten rule of behavior that a group expects its members to follow. It might say things like "we respond to emails within a day" or "we speak up in meetings." Norms are often stronger than written policy.

**Real-life applications:**
- Dress code norms that differ between finance and tech firms
- Meeting punctuality norms shaped by the leader's habits
- Email tone norms in different cultures
- Norms about working late in start-ups versus large corporates

**Key concepts:** `social norm`, `unwritten rule`, `group behavior`, `culture`, `expectation`

---

## Q19. What does "diagnosis of human problems" mean in an organization?

**Level:** Basic

It means investigating people-related issues at work to find the real cause, not just the symptom. A manager acts like a doctor asking questions before giving medicine. The goal is to treat the root cause, not guess at a fix.

**Real-life applications:**
- Investigating why one team has higher attrition than others
- Diagnosing a sudden drop in quality scores
- Exploring why a policy is not being followed
- Looking into rising conflicts in a department

**Key concepts:** `diagnosis`, `root cause`, `symptom`, `people problem`, `investigation`

---

## Q20. What is the difference between a symptom and a root cause?

**Level:** Basic

A symptom is the visible sign of a problem, like absenteeism or errors. A root cause is the deeper reason behind the symptom. Treating the symptom alone usually means the problem returns.

**Real-life applications:**
- High attrition as a symptom of poor management
- Missed deadlines as a symptom of unclear priorities
- Customer complaints as a symptom of a broken process
- Low morale as a symptom of unfair pay

**Key concepts:** `symptom`, `root cause`, `surface sign`, `underlying driver`, `problem solving`

---

## Q21. What is administrative decision-making?

**Level:** Basic

Administrative decision-making is the ongoing practice of making routine managerial choices about people, operations, and resources. Unlike strategic decisions, these happen every day. Done well, they keep the business running smoothly.

**Real-life applications:**
- Approving a staff leave request
- Deciding a weekly production schedule
- Choosing a vendor for small office supplies
- Allocating a contractor to a project

**Key concepts:** `administrative decision`, `routine`, `operations`, `management`, `resource allocation`

---

## Q22. What is bounded rationality?

**Level:** Basic

Bounded rationality is the idea that managers make decisions with limited information, limited time, and limited brainpower. They cannot evaluate every option, so they settle for a good enough choice. Herbert Simon introduced the term.

**Real-life applications:**
- Choosing the first suitable candidate instead of interviewing twenty
- Picking an office location from three nearby options, not all in the city
- Approving a project plan without reading every appendix
- Settling on a vendor based on two references

**Key concepts:** `bounded rationality`, `limits`, `satisficing`, `decision-making`, `Herbert Simon`

---

## Q23. What is satisficing?

**Level:** Basic

Satisficing means picking the first option that meets the minimum acceptable standard, instead of searching for the perfect option. It saves time and brainpower. Most real-world decisions use this approach.

**Real-life applications:**
- Hiring the first candidate that clears the bar
- Choosing the first supplier that meets price and quality thresholds
- Taking the first meeting slot that works for everyone
- Selecting a policy that solves the main problem, even if not ideal

**Key concepts:** `satisficing`, `good enough`, `efficiency`, `decision speed`, `pragmatic choice`

---

## Q24. What is personality in an organizational setting?

**Level:** Basic

Personality is a stable pattern of how a person thinks, feels, and behaves. In a workplace, it affects how someone communicates, handles pressure, and fits into a team. Personality is fairly consistent across situations.

**Real-life applications:**
- An introvert succeeding in focused analysis roles
- An extrovert thriving in sales and networking
- A detail-oriented person excelling in audit work
- A high-openness person enjoying creative problem-solving

**Key concepts:** `personality`, `trait`, `stable pattern`, `behavior`, `fit`

---

## Q25. What are values in the workplace?

**Level:** Basic

Values are deep beliefs about what is right and important. They guide long-term choices more than short-term rewards. When personal values match organizational values, engagement is higher.

**Real-life applications:**
- An honesty value making someone resist fudging reports
- A family value shaping a manager's stance on work-life balance
- A craft value driving relentless quality work
- A fairness value pushing someone to call out bias

**Key concepts:** `values`, `belief`, `principle`, `guidance`, `alignment`

---

## Q26. What is ability in the context of work?

**Level:** Basic

Ability is the current capacity to perform a task. It is built from knowledge, skill, and practice. Unlike personality, ability can be improved with training and experience.

**Real-life applications:**
- Typing speed improving with practice
- Financial modeling skill gained through a course
- Negotiation ability developed over deals
- Coding ability strengthened through mentorship

**Key concepts:** `ability`, `skill`, `knowledge`, `capacity`, `training`

---

## Q27. What is emotional intelligence?

**Level:** Basic

Emotional intelligence is the ability to notice and manage one's own emotions and the emotions of others. It has four parts: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, and relationship management. It helps managers lead difficult conversations.

**Real-life applications:**
- A manager staying calm when a client shouts
- A team lead sensing a member's burnout before it hits
- Giving tough feedback without damaging trust
- Reading the room during a tense meeting

**Key concepts:** `emotional intelligence`, `self-awareness`, `empathy`, `regulation`, `relationship`

---

## Q28. What is locus of control?

**Level:** Basic

Locus of control is the belief about how much you control outcomes in your life. People with an internal locus believe they shape their outcomes through effort. People with an external locus believe luck or fate is in charge.

**Real-life applications:**
- An internal-locus seller working hard after a lost deal
- An external-locus employee blaming the economy for missed targets
- A founder with internal locus pushing through failures
- Coaching an external-locus person toward small wins

**Key concepts:** `locus of control`, `internal`, `external`, `agency`, `motivation`

---

## Q29. What is motivation at work?

**Level:** Basic

Motivation is the internal drive that gets a person started, keeps them going, and pushes them to put effort into work. It comes from inside (interest, meaning) and outside (pay, praise). Both matter, but inside drive lasts longer.

**Real-life applications:**
- A designer working late on a project she loves
- A salesperson energized by a quarterly bonus
- A new hire motivated by learning and growth
- A teacher driven by student progress

**Key concepts:** `motivation`, `drive`, `effort`, `engagement`, `performance`

---

## Q30. What is the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation?

**Level:** Basic

Intrinsic motivation comes from the satisfaction of the work itself. Extrinsic motivation comes from external rewards like money, titles, or recognition. Strong teams rely on both, but intrinsic drive carries them through tough times.

**Real-life applications:**
- A coder who enjoys solving problems versus one chasing bonuses
- A volunteer working for a cause, not a paycheck
- A sales rep working hard for both commission and pride in the craft
- A teacher staying in education for meaning, not salary

**Key concepts:** `intrinsic`, `extrinsic`, `reward`, `meaning`, `engagement`

---

## Q31. What is leadership?

**Level:** Basic

Leadership is the practice of influencing and steering a group of people toward a shared goal. It is not the same as management, which is about running processes. Leaders set direction and energize people.

**Real-life applications:**
- A captain rallying a struggling team
- A founder painting a vision of the company's future
- A senior engineer guiding juniors through a hard project
- A department head aligning three teams on a common outcome

**Key concepts:** `leadership`, `influence`, `direction`, `vision`, `inspiration`

---

## Q32. What is a leadership style?

**Level:** Basic

Leadership style is the pattern a person uses to influence others. Common styles are directive, participative, coaching, and delegating. Good leaders shift style to match the situation and the person.

**Real-life applications:**
- Directive style in a crisis requiring speed
- Participative style in a design workshop
- Coaching style with a high-potential junior
- Delegating style with an experienced senior

**Key concepts:** `leadership style`, `directive`, `participative`, `coaching`, `situational`

---

## Q33. What is engagement at work?

**Level:** Basic

Engagement is the emotional commitment a person has to their job and organization. Engaged employees give discretionary effort. Disengaged employees do only what is asked.

**Real-life applications:**
- A high-engagement support agent going the extra mile for a customer
- A disengaged employee meeting bare minimums and watching the clock
- Strong engagement shown in positive survey scores
- Engagement dropping after a leadership change

**Key concepts:** `engagement`, `commitment`, `discretionary effort`, `survey`, `morale`

---

## Q34. What is a work group?

**Level:** Basic

A work group is a set of people who interact regularly and depend on each other to complete tasks. It is different from a crowd or a department because of the shared tasks and interdependence. Teams are a special kind of tight work group.

**Real-life applications:**
- A product squad of engineers, designers, and a product manager
- A shift team at a hospital emergency room
- A project team pulled together for six months
- A sales pod serving one region

**Key concepts:** `work group`, `team`, `interdependence`, `interaction`, `task`

---

## Q35. What are group norms?

**Level:** Basic

Group norms are unwritten rules about how members of a team should behave. They cover things like how quickly to reply to messages, who speaks first, or how disagreements are handled. Norms form quickly and then are hard to change.

**Real-life applications:**
- A norm of never interrupting in meetings
- A norm of working after hours during launches
- A norm about celebrating birthdays in the office
- A norm of honest feedback over sugar-coated responses

**Key concepts:** `group norms`, `unwritten rules`, `behavior`, `team culture`, `expectation`

---

## Q36. What is group cohesion?

**Level:** Basic

Group cohesion is the emotional glue that makes members want to stay with the group and contribute. High cohesion usually improves performance and loyalty. Too much cohesion, though, can cause groupthink.

**Real-life applications:**
- A startup team staying through tough funding times
- Long-serving sports teams winning with chemistry
- A project team preferring to work late together
- Close-knit shift teams covering for each other

**Key concepts:** `cohesion`, `loyalty`, `bond`, `teamwork`, `morale`

---

## Q37. What is groupthink?

**Level:** Basic

Groupthink happens when a group values harmony so much that members stop voicing real concerns. It leads to poor decisions because no one challenges bad ideas. It is a common failure in tight, loyal groups.

**Real-life applications:**
- A board failing to question a CEO's bold plan
- A design team ignoring a dissenting usability test
- A launch team pushing ahead despite clear red flags
- A board unanimously approving a doomed acquisition

**Key concepts:** `groupthink`, `harmony`, `dissent`, `decision failure`, `conformity`

---

## Q38. What is psychological safety?

**Level:** Basic

Psychological safety is the shared belief that it is safe to speak up, ask questions, admit mistakes, and take small risks. Teams with psychological safety learn faster and perform better. It is the most studied predictor of team performance.

**Real-life applications:**
- A junior asking what seems like a basic question without fear
- A nurse speaking up to a doctor about a risk
- A new hire admitting she does not understand a process
- An engineer flagging a design flaw before release

**Key concepts:** `psychological safety`, `speak up`, `risk taking`, `learning`, `trust`

---

## Q39. What are the stages of group development?

**Level:** Basic

Groups usually pass through four stages: forming, storming, norming, and performing. In forming, members feel each other out. In storming, they disagree. In norming, they find rules. In performing, they deliver real work.

**Real-life applications:**
- A new project team navigating early friction
- A cross-functional task force finding its rhythm
- A merged team sorting through conflicting norms
- A scrum team maturing over sprints

**Key concepts:** `forming`, `storming`, `norming`, `performing`, `team development`

---

## Q40. What are inter-group relations?

**Level:** Basic

Inter-group relations are the patterns of cooperation, conflict, and communication between different teams inside the same organization. Good relations speed up work. Bad ones cause silos and missed targets.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sales and operations working together on delivery promises
- Engineering and marketing aligning on launch dates
- Finance and HR coordinating on payroll cycles
- Research and regulatory teams working through approvals

**Key concepts:** `inter-group`, `cooperation`, `conflict`, `silos`, `alignment`

---

## Q41. What is role clarity?

**Level:** Basic

Role clarity is a shared understanding of what each person is responsible for. Without it, people either duplicate work or assume someone else will do the task. Clear roles reduce fights and speed up delivery.

**Real-life applications:**
- A RACI chart defining project responsibilities
- A job description updated after a team restructure
- A new hire onboarding with a one-page role summary
- Clear escalation paths in an incident response plan

**Key concepts:** `role clarity`, `responsibility`, `ownership`, `duplication`, `accountability`

---

## Q42. What is role conflict?

**Level:** Basic

Role conflict happens when a person faces mismatched expectations from different sources. A manager might be told to cut costs and improve service at the same time. Unresolved role conflict causes stress and mistakes.

**Real-life applications:**
- A quality engineer pressured to ship fast and stop defects
- A first-line manager torn between worker and company interests
- A compliance officer caught between speed and safety
- A teacher asked to cover syllabus and offer deep learning

**Key concepts:** `role conflict`, `mismatched expectations`, `stress`, `trade-off`, `clarity`

---

## Q43. What is RACI?

**Level:** Basic

RACI is a simple tool that clarifies who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed for each task. Responsible does the work. Accountable owns the outcome. Consulted gives input. Informed is kept in the loop. It prevents confusion on who decides.

**Real-life applications:**
- A RACI for a product launch across teams
- A RACI in a hiring process
- A RACI for incident response duties
- A RACI for compliance reviews

**Key concepts:** `RACI`, `responsible`, `accountable`, `consulted`, `informed`

---

## Q44. What is a socio-technical system?

**Level:** Basic

A socio-technical system is the joint arrangement of people, tasks, and technology in a workplace. It treats the human side and the technical side as two halves that must be designed together. Ignoring either side causes failure.

**Real-life applications:**
- A new CRM rollout redesigned with salespeople's input
- A factory cell combining machines and trained operators
- A hospital records system built with nurse workflows
- A call center where scripts and systems are tuned together

**Key concepts:** `socio-technical`, `joint optimization`, `people`, `technology`, `design`

---

## Q45. What is joint optimization?

**Level:** Basic

Joint optimization is the principle of designing both the human side and the technical side of a system together for the best overall result. Optimizing one alone usually hurts the other. The best outcomes come from tuning both at once.

**Real-life applications:**
- Balancing automation with meaningful work for operators
- Designing software with user workflows in mind
- Setting up a call center for both speed and empathy
- Arranging a clinic so workflow and patient experience both improve

**Key concepts:** `joint optimization`, `balance`, `design`, `human factors`, `system performance`

---

## Q46. What is a self-managed team?

**Level:** Basic

A self-managed team is a group that controls its own work methods, scheduling, and improvements. Instead of a boss giving orders, the team decides how to meet its goals. It works best when the team is skilled and trusted.

**Real-life applications:**
- A software scrum team choosing its own sprint work
- A production cell deciding its own shift roles
- A research group setting its own experiment priorities
- A consulting pod planning its engagement approach

**Key concepts:** `self-managed team`, `autonomy`, `empowerment`, `trust`, `accountability`

---

## Q47. What is work design?

**Level:** Basic

Work design is the structured choice of tasks, tools, and interactions that make up a job. Good work design balances efficiency with motivation. Bad work design creates burnout or boredom.

**Real-life applications:**
- Redesigning a call center job to add variety
- Rotating assembly line roles to reduce strain
- Combining tasks to give a clerk a full service case
- Splitting a bloated manager role into two clearer ones

**Key concepts:** `work design`, `task`, `motivation`, `efficiency`, `job satisfaction`

---

## Q48. What is autonomy in the workplace?

**Level:** Basic

Autonomy is the freedom a person has to choose how to do their job. High autonomy usually increases motivation and ownership. Low autonomy can frustrate skilled workers.

**Real-life applications:**
- Engineers choosing their own tools
- Sales reps planning their own daily routes
- Researchers picking methods for a project
- Teachers designing their own lesson plans

**Key concepts:** `autonomy`, `freedom`, `choice`, `motivation`, `ownership`

---

## Q49. What is organizational structure?

**Level:** Basic

Organizational structure is the formal arrangement of roles, reporting lines, and decision authority. It shows who reports to whom and who can decide what. Structure is the skeleton of the organization.

**Real-life applications:**
- A traditional hierarchy of CEO, VPs, directors, managers
- A flat start-up with the founder and a small team
- A matrix structure in a consulting firm
- A product-based structure in a tech company

**Key concepts:** `organizational structure`, `hierarchy`, `reporting`, `authority`, `roles`

---

## Q50. What is span of control?

**Level:** Basic

Span of control is the number of people a manager directly supervises. A narrow span means closer supervision. A wide span means more independence for direct reports. The right span depends on complexity and team maturity.

**Real-life applications:**
- A factory supervisor handling twenty line workers
- A CEO with five direct reports
- A call center team leader with ten agents
- A research head managing three senior scientists

**Key concepts:** `span of control`, `supervision`, `hierarchy`, `team size`, `management`

---

## Q51. What is centralization?

**Level:** Basic

Centralization means decision authority is held at the top of the organization. Senior leaders make most of the calls, and lower levels execute. It fits stable industries where consistency matters most.

**Real-life applications:**
- A bank centralizing all lending decisions
- A retail chain centralizing buying for consistency
- A hospital centralizing drug formulary choices
- A government department with strict top-down rules

**Key concepts:** `centralization`, `authority`, `top-down`, `consistency`, `control`

---

## Q52. What is decentralization?

**Level:** Basic

Decentralization means decision authority is pushed down to local teams or units. Local managers have the power to decide within clear limits. It fits fast-changing markets and diverse customer needs.

**Real-life applications:**
- A global firm letting country heads set local prices
- A retail chain letting store managers order seasonal stock
- A tech firm letting product teams own their roadmaps
- A hospital letting ward teams manage shift planning

**Key concepts:** `decentralization`, `empowerment`, `local decision`, `speed`, `flexibility`

---

## Q53. What is a functional structure?

**Level:** Basic

A functional structure groups people by their specialty, such as finance, sales, operations, or HR. Each function has its own reporting line. It is the most common structure in traditional companies.

**Real-life applications:**
- A manufacturing firm with production, sales, and finance heads
- A bank with separate departments for retail, corporate, and treasury
- A media firm with editorial, advertising, and operations
- A university with deans of each faculty

**Key concepts:** `functional structure`, `specialty`, `department`, `silos`, `traditional`

---

## Q54. What is change management?

**Level:** Basic

Change management is the structured approach to moving people and processes from a current state to a future one. It focuses on the human side of change. Without it, even strong strategies fail in execution.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company rolling out a new ERP system
- A hospital adopting a new electronic records platform
- A merger combining two cultures
- A department moving from office to hybrid work

**Key concepts:** `change management`, `transition`, `adoption`, `process`, `culture`

---

## Q55. What is resistance to change?

**Level:** Basic

Resistance to change is the pushback people show against a new way of working. It can be emotional (fear of loss) or practical (new skills needed). Resistance is normal and should be planned for.

**Real-life applications:**
- Staff ignoring a new reporting tool
- Managers quietly working around a new policy
- Workers protesting a shift in production methods
- Long-serving employees resisting cultural change after a merger

**Key concepts:** `resistance`, `fear`, `loss`, `adoption`, `change curve`

## Q56. What is a change agent?

## 1. Jargon Buster

Ten foundational terms every practitioner must own before attempting the 70 Qs:

**Communication Process**
The end-to-end sequence by which a sender encodes a message, transmits it through a chosen channel, and a receiver decodes it and (ideally) sends feedback. The process is only complete when meaning is shared — not merely when words are delivered. In async digital work, the gap between transmission and shared meaning is the single largest source of project failure.

**Formal Channel**
An officially sanctioned pathway for organizational information — org charts, memos, reporting lines, sprint review meetings, email threads marked for record, ticketing systems. Formal channels carry authority and accountability; they are auditable and documented. Their weakness is latency and the perception of bureaucracy, which causes people to route around them.

**Informal Channel**
Any communication pathway that exists outside the official structure — Slack side-threads, hallway conversations, shared lunch tables, off-the-record calls. Informal channels are faster and more emotionally honest than formal ones. They are not inherently bad; smart leaders use them intentionally to sense-check formal messaging and accelerate trust.

**Grapevine**
The self-organizing informal rumor network inside any organization. The grapevine is not random — research shows it is about 80% accurate on factual matters but highly distorted on intent, causality, and timing. In IT/AI environments, the grapevine activates most aggressively during restructuring, layoffs, or a product pivot. Leaders who ignore it cede narrative control.

**Noise**
Any factor — physical, semantic, psychological, or cultural — that degrades the fidelity of a message between encoding and decoding. In digital-first teams, noise includes: Zoom audio lag, ambiguous ticket descriptions, acronym overload, notification fatigue, and cross-cultural idiom mismatch. Noise is always present; the question is whether it is above or below the threshold of meaning loss.

**Intrinsic Motivation**
Drive sourced from the work itself — curiosity, mastery, autonomy, purpose. Herzberg classified this as a true motivator (vs. hygiene factor). For knowledge workers and AI practitioners, intrinsic motivation is the primary fuel; removing it (through micromanagement, meaningless tasks, or blocked learning) degrades output faster than any salary cut.

**Span of Control**
The number of direct reports a single manager effectively oversees. Classical theory recommends 5-9 for knowledge work. Wider spans demand stronger communication infrastructure (documentation, async norms, shared decision frameworks). Narrower spans enable richer 1:1 communication but increase organizational layers and slow information flow.

**RACI**
Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed — a decision-rights matrix that pre-empts communication breakdown by making explicit who does what, who owns the outcome, who must be looped in before action, and who only needs a notification. In Agile/product environments RACI is often replaced by DRI (Directly Responsible Individual), but the underlying communication logic is identical.

**Psychological Safety**
Amy Edmondson's term for the shared belief that one can speak up — with a question, a mistake, a dissent — without being punished or humiliated. Psychological safety is a prerequisite for upward communication to be honest. Without it, upward channels carry only good news; leaders become informationally blind to risk.

**Change Agent**
An individual (internal or external) who actively facilitates organizational change — translating the why, surfacing resistance, modeling new behaviors, and stabilizing after transition. In Lewin's model, the change agent drives unfreezing. In practice, every PM and tech lead is a part-time change agent whether they are titled as one or not.

## 2. Frameworks & Mental Models

Four meta-frameworks that organize the conceptual terrain of the 70 basic Qs:

**Shannon-Weaver Communication Model (1949)**
Originally an engineering model of signal transmission, now the dominant mental model for organizational communication. Components: Sender (encodes) → Message → Channel → Receiver (decodes) → Feedback loop. Noise intervenes at every stage. The model's critical insight for practitioners is that encoding and decoding are separate cognitive acts — a message sent is not a message understood. In practice: the PM who writes a detailed spec has encoded; the engineer who reads it has decoded. If the internal models do not match, there is communication failure regardless of how well the spec was written.

Application layer: Before any critical communication, ask — what assumptions am I encoding that the receiver does not share? That gap is where noise lives.

**Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs (5 Levels)**
Physiological → Safety → Belonging → Esteem → Self-Actualization. In organizational communication, Maslow maps to: (1) basic working conditions/compensation, (2) job security and psychological safety, (3) team inclusion and social belonging, (4) recognition and status, (5) growth, mastery, and meaningful work. Communication strategies must be calibrated to where people actually are, not where leaders assume they should be. A team facing layoff uncertainty cannot meaningfully engage with self-actualization messaging about the product vision.

Practitioner note: In AI/tech orgs, most ICs operate at level 3-4 by default. Engagement interventions that skip straight to purpose (level 5) without addressing belonging or recognition fail because they misdiagnose the level.

**Tuckman's 4 Stages of Group Development**
Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing (Adjourning added later). Each stage has a characteristic communication pattern: Forming = polite, surface-level, high uncertainty; Storming = conflict, competing agendas, emotional communication; Norming = collaborative norms emerge, feedback becomes safe; Performing = autonomous, high-trust, efficient communication.

The practitioner's diagnostic use: if a team's communication is breaking down, identify the Tuckman stage first. A team in Storming needs conflict surfacing tools, not better documentation templates. A team in Forming needs clarity of purpose and role, not autonomy.

**Lewin's 3-Stage Change Model**
Unfreeze → Change → Refreeze. Communication is the primary mechanism at each stage: Unfreeze = communicate the burning platform and the why; Change = provide information, training, and psychological support during transition; Refreeze = reinforce new norms through recognition, process documentation, and updated communication channels. Most change communication failures happen because organizations skip Unfreezing — they announce the change without ever establishing why the status quo is no longer acceptable. The result is passive or active resistance that leaders misread as laziness or incompetence.

## 3. Formulas / Thresholds / Decision Rules

**Channel Selection Rule — Richness vs. Complexity**
Decision logic: As message complexity increases and as relational ambiguity increases, channel richness must increase proportionally.

- Low complexity + low ambiguity → asynchronous text (email, ticket comment, Slack message)
- Medium complexity + medium ambiguity → synchronous voice or video without screen share
- High complexity + high ambiguity → synchronous video with shared screen, whiteboard, and explicit follow-up documentation
- Emotionally charged message (feedback, termination, escalation) → always richest available channel, never async text

Anti-rule: Using a lean channel for a rich message is one of the most common and costly communication errors in remote-first organizations.

**Span of Control Range: 5-9 Direct Reports**
Below 5: over-managed, communication is micromanaged, autonomy suppressed, organizational layers multiply. Above 9: under-managed, communication becomes shallow, 1:1 quality degrades, informal channels carry load that formal channels should. The 5-9 range assumes knowledge workers with moderate interdependence. For highly autonomous senior ICs, spans up to 12 may be manageable if async communication norms are strong.

**Motivation Diagnostic Decision Tree**
Step 1: Is the person meeting basic hygiene thresholds? (Compensation fair? Working conditions acceptable? Job secure?) If NO → fix hygiene first; no motivator intervention will work. If YES → proceed to Step 2.
Step 2: Is there recognition and role clarity? If NO → address esteem layer. If YES → proceed to Step 3.
Step 3: Is the work itself meaningful, growth-oriented, and autonomous? If NO → this is an intrinsic motivation problem; redesign the work or the role. If YES → motivation problem may be interpersonal or systemic, not individual.

**Communication Loss Per Hierarchy Layer Rule**
Research-derived heuristic: in a typical organization, approximately 20-25% of information fidelity is lost at each hierarchical layer. In a 5-layer hierarchy, a message from the CEO reaches a front-line IC with as little as 30% of its original fidelity intact. Implication: for critical strategic communication, skip-level communication, town halls, and direct written artifacts (recorded videos, written principles) are not optional extras — they are fidelity-preservation mechanisms.

## 4. Do / Don't

**DO:**

1. Match channel richness to message complexity before every significant communication.
2. Explicitly name your assumptions when encoding technical or strategic messages for cross-functional audiences.
3. Confirm understanding via paraphrase or follow-up action, not via "does everyone understand?" (which yields false positives).
4. Use the grapevine as a sensing tool — monitor informal channels to understand what the formal narrative is failing to address.
5. Establish RACI or equivalent decision-rights clarity at the start of every project or squad.
6. Calibrate motivational interventions to the actual Maslow level of the individual, not the aspirational level.
7. Create psychological safety explicitly at team formation — name it, model it, and protect it when it is tested.
8. Communicate the "why" before the "what" and "how" in any change initiative.
9. Use span of control as a diagnostic — if managers consistently have more than 9 reports, the communication infrastructure is under-resourced.
10. Close the feedback loop explicitly: acknowledge receipt, confirm interpretation, and report back on action taken.
11. Treat Tuckman stage diagnosis as a prerequisite to communication intervention design.
12. Document informal agreements made in informal channels — convert them to formal record within 24 hours.

**DON'T:**

1. Don't use email or Slack for emotionally complex messages, performance issues, or conflict resolution — the channel is too lean.
2. Don't assume downward communication has been understood because it has been sent.
3. Don't suppress grapevine activity — it accelerates in information vacuums; fill vacuums with honest formal communication instead.
4. Don't apply a single communication style across all hierarchy levels — senior leaders and junior ICs have different context and decoding frameworks.
5. Don't skip the Unfreeze stage of change communication — announcing change without establishing the burning platform creates resistance.
6. Don't conflate hygiene factors (salary, environment) with motivators (achievement, growth) — fixing one does not fix the other.
7. Don't allow upward communication to be filtered to only good news — this is an organizational pathology that accelerates failure.
8. Don't use RACI as a bureaucratic formality — if no one can name the A (Accountable) without checking a document, the matrix has not been operationalized.
9. Don't ignore noise sources in distributed teams — technical noise (lag, drop-outs), semantic noise (jargon), and cultural noise (idiom) all degrade meaning.
10. Don't conflate a Storming team with a dysfunctional team — Storming is normal and healthy; suppressing it drives conflict underground.
11. Don't assume flat org structures eliminate communication problems — they trade vertical noise for horizontal coordination overhead.
12. Don't use one-way broadcast communication (all-hands announcements, mass emails) as a substitute for dialogue during high-ambiguity periods.

## 5. Metric-Driven Scenarios with Anti-Examples

**Scenario 1: Noise Failure in Remote Standup**

Context: A distributed engineering squad of 8 runs a daily standup over video. Sprint velocity has dropped 18% over six weeks. The engineering manager attributes it to scope creep.

Metric signals: Standup attendance is 100%, but post-standup clarification messages in Slack average 14 per session. Three blockers identified in standups went unresolved for more than 48 hours despite being verbally acknowledged.

Diagnosis: The channel (video standup, 15 minutes, 8 people) is generating noise faster than it is resolving it. Semantic noise — members using sprint jargon inconsistently — is causing apparent agreement (head nods) that masks real misalignment. The feedback loop (Slack follow-up messages) is confirming that decoding is failing systematically.

Correct intervention: Introduce a structured standup format with written pre-standup updates (async), limit live discussion to blockers only, and establish a shared glossary for the three most-ambiguous recurring terms. Measure: target fewer than 4 post-standup clarification messages per session within two weeks.

Anti-example: The manager responds by extending the standup to 30 minutes and requiring more detailed verbal updates. This increases transmission volume but does not address the noise source. Clarification messages increase to 19 per session. Velocity drops a further 6%.

---

**Scenario 2: Motivation Diagnosis for Disengaged Senior Engineer**

Context: A senior ML engineer with 4 years at a Series B AI company has shown a measurable 30% reduction in code commit frequency, a pattern of declining sprint story point completion, and three consecutive quarters of "meets expectations" in a culture where this IC previously received "exceeds." Exit interview risk score (internal metric) flags them as high-flight-risk.

Metric signals: Compensation benchmarking shows this engineer is at the 62nd percentile for their role and market. Team NPS survey shows a 7/10 for "I feel recognized for my contributions." Learning and development budget usage: 0% in the last two quarters.

Motivation Diagnostic: Step 1 — hygiene threshold. Compensation is acceptable (not deficient). Step 2 — recognition. Score of 7/10 is borderline; investigate. Step 3 — intrinsic motivation. Zero L&D usage is a significant signal; the engineer may be under-challenged or see no growth path.

Correct intervention: Manager initiates a structured 1:1 to surface growth aspiration explicitly. Engineer discloses they want to move into applied research but see no pathway. Manager proposes a 20% allocation to an internal research project with a conference submission target. Commit frequency recovers to baseline within 6 weeks.

Anti-example: Manager responds by increasing the engineer's performance bonus eligibility. Commit frequency does not change. Manager escalates to HR, who begins a performance improvement process. Engineer resigns within 60 days. The hygiene lever was pulled for an intrinsic motivation problem. Herzberg's prediction confirmed.

---

**Scenario 3: Change Rollout with No Why Explanation**

Context: A 200-person product organization migrates from Jira to Linear over a 3-week rollout. The announcement is a Slack message from the Head of Engineering: "We are moving to Linear starting Monday. Training sessions are scheduled below."

Metric signals: Training session attendance is 61% (target was 90%). Two weeks post-migration, Linear usage is at 44% of tickets vs. the target of 85%. Slack messages referencing "the old way" or "Jira" average 23 per day. Three senior PMs are using Linear for formal logging and Jira for actual workflow tracking.

Diagnosis: Lewin's Unfreeze stage was skipped entirely. No burning platform was communicated; no why was given. Employees experienced the change as arbitrary and managerial, not necessary and rational. The resistance is passive (low adoption) rather than vocal, making it harder to detect until metrics surface it.

Correct intervention (remediation): All-hands async video from the Head of Engineering explaining: why Jira's cost and query limitations were causing real workflow friction (specific examples), what Linear solves specifically, and what the 90-day success metric looks like. Follow-up: designated Linear champions in each squad to provide peer-level informal support. Measure: adoption rate target reset to 85% within 4 weeks of remediation communication.

Anti-example: Engineering leadership responds by making Linear usage mandatory and disabling Jira access. Adoption metrics reach 92% within one week. However, ticket quality metrics drop sharply — engineers are logging minimally to comply, not to communicate. The compliance metric was achieved; the communication goal was not.

## 6. Practitioner Playbook

Ten specific applications of organizational communication fundamentals in a typical PM/Tech Lead workday:

1. **Morning standup facilitation:** Before speaking, diagnose Tuckman stage. A team in Storming needs structured turn-taking and explicit conflict surfacing — not open discussion that allows dominant voices to suppress quieter ones. A Performing team needs minimal facilitation and maximum autonomy.

2. **Ticket writing:** Apply Shannon-Weaver encoding discipline. For every ticket, ask: what assumptions does the assignee need to share for them to decode this correctly? Write those assumptions into the acceptance criteria, not into verbal handoffs.

3. **Slack message routing decisions:** Apply channel richness decision rule before sending. If you are about to send a message that you suspect will generate more than two clarification replies, upgrade to a synchronous channel. Count the expected back-and-forth as a noise indicator.

4. **1:1 meetings:** Run a silent Maslow diagnostic for each direct report before the meeting. What level are they operating at today based on recent signals? Design your agenda to address that level — do not open with product vision if they are sitting in job security anxiety.

5. **RACI review at sprint kickoff:** For each epic starting this sprint, can every team member name the Accountable without looking it up? If not, RACI is not operationalized. Spend 5 minutes verbally naming the A for the top three ambiguous decisions this sprint is likely to generate.

6. **All-hands or town hall preparation:** For any change being announced, draft the why in one sentence before drafting the what. If you cannot write the why in one sentence, you are not ready to communicate the change. Test the why sentence on one person outside the decision-making group before broadcasting.

7. **Grapevine monitoring:** Identify two informal connectors on the team — people who are central in the informal communication network (they tend to be the ones everyone goes to for "what's really going on"). Before any significant announcement, informally brief these connectors 24-48 hours ahead. This does not undermine formal channels; it uses informal channels to amplify accurate messaging.

8. **Conflict mediation:** When two team members are in conflict, diagnose before intervening. Is this a role clarity conflict (RACI ambiguity), a communication style conflict (channel or noise mismatch), or an interest conflict (competing goals)? The intervention differs completely. Role clarity conflicts are solved with documentation; style conflicts with channel adjustment; interest conflicts with negotiation.

9. **Post-mortem facilitation:** Psychological safety is the precondition for an honest post-mortem. If your last post-mortem produced only surface-level findings, the problem was not analytical capability — it was safety. Open the next post-mortem by naming a mistake you (the facilitator) made this sprint. Modeling vulnerability is the fastest way to shift safety norms.

10. **End-of-day communication audit:** Before closing, review the messages you sent today. Identify any that used a lean channel for a rich message, any that lacked an explicit feedback loop, and any that assumed shared context that may not exist. This 5-minute audit compounds into dramatically better communication hygiene over 90 days.

## 7. Content Critique

**Are these the right 70 basic questions?**

The 70 basic Qs cover the canonical syllabus of organizational communication with reasonable breadth. For a generalist MBA or management student, the coverage is appropriate and complete. For IT, AI product, and consulting practitioners, however, there are meaningful gaps and some areas of overrepresentation.

**Gaps for IT/AI Practitioners:**

- Async-first communication norms are absent. In distributed engineering teams, the default channel is async text — but none of the 70 Qs address how noise, feedback loops, or channel selection operate differently in async contexts. This is not a minor omission; async communication is the primary mode for most AI/tech practitioners.

- Documentation as communication is not addressed. For engineering teams, code comments, ADRs (Architecture Decision Records), and README files are formal communication artifacts — but the basic Qs treat "formal channel" as synonymous with managerial hierarchy, which is a legacy frame.

- Cross-cultural communication noise is underrepresented. Global AI teams routinely experience communication breakdown at the cultural-semantic layer — idiom mismatch, directness norms, hierarchy expectations — none of which appear in the 70 Qs.

- AI-mediated communication is absent. Teams increasingly use AI tools (summarizers, meeting transcribers, drafting assistants) as intermediary communication channels. The noise and fidelity implications of AI-mediated communication are not addressed anywhere in the basic framework.

- Psychological safety operationalization is thin. The concept appears (Q-series touching Herzberg and motivation), but how to build, measure, and sustain psychological safety in high-performance technical teams is not addressed practically.

**Overrepresented Areas:**

- Classical hierarchy models (downward/upward/horizontal) receive disproportionate attention relative to their practical relevance in flat, Agile, or matrixed organizations. The distinction matters conceptually but is less actionable for practitioners than network communication patterns.

- Maslow receives significant coverage, but the empirical limitations of Maslow (non-linear progression, cultural variability, lack of strong empirical validation) are not surfaced. Practitioners applying Maslow naively can misdiagnose motivation problems.

- Tuckman's stages are well-covered but the model's limitation (teams rarely move linearly, and can regress) is not examined. A team that re-Storms after a membership change is not covered.

**Verdict:** The 70 Qs are a sound foundation for generalists. For IT/AI/Product practitioners, they should be supplemented with: async communication norms, documentation-as-communication, AI-mediated communication channel analysis, and applied psychological safety measurement.
