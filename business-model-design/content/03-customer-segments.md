# Customer Segments

## Overview

Customer segments are the distinct groups of people or organizations a business aims to serve. Each segment has different needs, behaviors, and willingness to pay, which means a single product or message rarely works for everyone. Identifying and prioritizing segments lets a company focus its resources on the groups that matter most to its growth and profitability.

---

## Why It Matters

Trying to serve everyone means serving no one well. Clear segmentation drives sharper marketing, better product design, and smarter pricing. When a company knows exactly who its best customers are, it can allocate budget to acquire more of them and stop wasting money on segments that drain resources without returning profit.

## Key Principles

- Segment by behavior and needs, not just demographics
- Each segment must be large enough to justify a tailored approach
- Prioritize segments by profitability, growth potential, and strategic fit
- Revisit segments regularly because markets evolve
- Validate segments with data, not assumptions

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Mass Market** | A segment approach that targets the broadest possible audience with no meaningful differentiation |
| **Niche Market** | A narrow, specialized segment with very specific needs and preferences |
| **Multi-Sided Market** | A model that serves two or more interdependent customer groups simultaneously |
| **Early Adopter** | A customer who is willing to try new products before the mainstream, often tolerating imperfections for innovation |

## Use Case

An online education platform analyzes enrollment data and discovers three distinct segments: career changers seeking certificates, college students supplementing coursework, and corporate teams buying bulk licenses. It creates separate landing pages, pricing tiers, and email sequences for each group, boosting overall conversion by 35%.

## Scenario

> A craft-beer brewery sold to anyone who walked through its taproom door. After analyzing point-of-sale data, it found that 70% of revenue came from two segments: weekend tourists buying flights and local regulars buying growlers. The brewery launched a loyalty program for regulars and partnered with tour operators for tourists, increasing monthly revenue by 25% without opening a new location.

## Examples

- A project management tool creates a free tier for freelancers and a premium tier for enterprise teams after discovering these two segments have completely different feature priorities
- An insurance company separates young renters from homeowners over 40, tailoring policy bundles and marketing channels to each group's risk profile and buying habits

## 4. Formulas

Interpretation bands below are decision guidance synthesized from the source example and common business practice [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Revenue concentration in top segments
Formula: `revenue share of top segments = revenue from selected segments / total revenue`
Variables:
Revenue from selected segments = combined revenue from the target segments
Total revenue = all revenue across the business
Why this formula exists: It shows whether a small number of segments drive most of the business.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.50 → revenue is diffuse → keep testing segment focus
Value 0.50–0.80 → revenue is concentrated enough to prioritize → tailor offers and channels
Value > 0.80 → the business is highly dependent on a few segments → protect against concentration risk
Worked example with numbers: The source says a brewery found that 70% of revenue came from weekend tourists and local regulars. Using the formula, 0.70 means those two segments should receive tailored loyalty and partnership strategies.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Target everyone with one generic message | Segment by behavior and needs first |
| Pick segments only from demographics | Validate segments with data and observed behavior |
| Chase a large market that does not pay well | Prioritize profitability, growth potential, and strategic fit |
| Ignore the fact that segments buy differently | Build separate offers, pricing, or channels where needed |
| Leave segments unchanged as the market shifts | Revisit and re-score segments regularly |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

The decision rules below are operational heuristics layered on top of the source example [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Scenario 1: Online education platform with three buyer groups
Situation: A product team sees career changers, college students, and corporate teams in enrollment data. The generic funnel is underperforming because each segment responds to different messages and price points.
Applicable framework/metric: Revenue concentration in top segments
Analysis: If corporate teams contribute 0.45 of revenue and career changers plus students add 0.25 and 0.20, the team should design separate offers rather than one blended page.
Decision rule: If one segment exceeds 0.50 of revenue, tailor hard. If no segment exceeds 0.20, keep testing. If the top two segments exceed 0.70 combined, prioritize them.
Action: Build separate landing pages, pricing tiers, and email sequences for each segment.

### Scenario 2: Product management tool choosing between free and premium offers
Situation: A PM team at a project management tool serves freelancers and enterprise teams. The feature roadmap is split because freelancers want simplicity while enterprises want controls and reporting.
Applicable framework/metric: Feature priority by segment
Analysis: If enterprise requests cluster around governance and freelancers mostly use basic task tracking, a one-tier roadmap will keep missing one group.
Decision rule: If priorities diverge sharply, separate the offer. If they overlap moderately, use one core product with segment-specific packaging. If they overlap heavily, keep one standard offer.
Action: Keep a free tier for freelancers and a premium tier for enterprise teams.

### Scenario 3: Consulting review of a retail segment mix
Situation: A consulting team advises an insurance company that has been targeting everyone over 40 with the same offer. POS-style transaction and claim data show clear differences in buying habits and risk profile.
Applicable framework/metric: Segment fit and profitability
Analysis: If younger renters have lower policy size but faster growth and homeowners have higher premium value but higher claims, the company should not treat them as one segment.
Decision rule: If risk and buying habits differ materially, split the segment. If differences are modest, keep one segment with tiered offers. If data is weak, gather more evidence before redesigning.
Action: Tailor bundles and channels to each group, then re-check profitability after launch.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Pull segment data from transactions, usage, and customer interviews.
2. Group customers by behavior and needs before looking at demographics.
3. Score each segment for profitability, growth potential, and strategic fit.
4. Build one offer, landing page, or pricing tier for each priority segment.
5. Test the segment assumptions with real conversion and retention data.
6. Remove low-value segments that drain budget without improving profit.
7. Revisit the segment map on a regular review cadence.
