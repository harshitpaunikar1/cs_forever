# Disrupting Existing Models

## Overview

Disrupting existing business models means entering a market with a fundamentally different approach that incumbents cannot easily copy without cannibalizing their own profits. Disruption often starts at the low end or in overlooked segments, offering a simpler or cheaper alternative that gradually improves until it displaces established players. The key is not just having better technology but having a different economic logic.

---

## Why It Matters

Incumbents are trapped by their own success — their cost structures, customer expectations, and internal incentives make it painful to adopt a disruptive model even when they see it coming. Understanding disruption helps managers both attack vulnerable industries and defend against challengers. Companies that ignore disruptive signals end up like Blockbuster, Kodak, or traditional taxi operators — overtaken by rivals they initially dismissed.

## Key Principles

- Disruption is about the business model, not just the product or technology
- Incumbents often ignore disruptors because the initial market looks small or unprofitable
- Start with customers who are over-served or non-consumers of the current offering
- Build a cost structure that is profitable at the lower price point, not just temporarily subsidized
- Speed of iteration matters more than perfection at launch

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Disruptive Innovation** | An innovation that creates a new market or enters at the bottom of an existing one and eventually displaces established competitors |
| **Sustaining Innovation** | Improvements to existing products that serve current customers better without changing the business model |
| **Incumbent's Dilemma** | The conflict where adopting a new model would undermine the company's existing profitable business |
| **Low-End Disruption** | Targeting the least profitable customers of incumbents with a good-enough product at a much lower price |

## Use Case

A budget airline enters a market dominated by full-service carriers. By stripping out meals, assigned seats, and lounge access, it offers fares 60% lower. Business travelers stay with incumbents, but price-sensitive leisure travelers switch in droves. Within five years, the budget airline captures 30% of domestic routes.

## Scenario

> A traditional textbook publisher charged $150 per college textbook. An edtech startup offered interactive digital textbooks for $30/semester via subscription. Professors adopted the platform because it included built-in quizzes and analytics. Within three years, the startup had 2 million student subscribers, and the publisher's print sales dropped 40% on titles where the digital alternative existed.

## Examples

- Dollar Shave Club disrupted Gillette by shipping basic razors via subscription at a fraction of the retail price, bypassing the expensive shelf-space model entirely
- Netflix disrupted Blockbuster first with mail-order DVDs (no late fees, no stores) and then again with streaming, making the physical rental model obsolete

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Disruption Wedge Map [verified from model knowledge, not source]
**Purpose:** Identify the first segment and business model that can open a market wedge.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                 Customer Need
            Low complexity     High complexity
Over-served     easy wedge        premium upgrade later
Non-consumer    entry wedge       too hard for first launch
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Customer Need - how much capability the segment truly requires.
Component 2: Complexity - how much process, service, or premium performance is needed.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting analytics startup starts with over-served SMB clients who want simple KPI dashboards, not full enterprise BI. It later adds deeper forecasting for larger clients once the low-cost model has traction.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when deciding whether the first offer should be stripped-down, self-serve, or highly serviced.

### Incumbent Response Matrix [verified from model knowledge, not source]
**Purpose:** Forecast how an incumbent may respond and whether the entrant can survive the response.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    Incumbent Pain
                 Low                High
Low Threat     ignore            watch closely
High Threat    copy if safe      resist or delay
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Threat - how much the new model threatens existing profits.
Component 2: Pain - how hard it is for the incumbent to copy without self-harm.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI support tool sold to small businesses is easy for large software vendors to ignore at first, but once it starts winning enterprise workflow seats, incumbents may copy the feature set while keeping the old pricing model intact.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when the team is underestimating how quickly a large player can imitate the feature but not the economics.

## 4. Formulas

### Relative Price
Formula: `Relative Price = Entrant Price ÷ Incumbent Price`
Variables:
Entrant Price = the price charged by the new model.
Incumbent Price = the incumbent's comparable price.
Why this formula exists: It answers how much cheaper the entrant is than the status quo.
How to interpret the output:
Value > 0.8 → little price disruption → differentiation must come from convenience or service
Value 0.3–0.8 → meaningful wedge → can attract over-served users
Value < 0.3 → aggressive low-end entry → must prove cost structure works
Worked example with numbers: If a digital product costs $30 per month versus an incumbent's $150 per month, relative price is 0.2. That is a strong low-end wedge if the product still solves the core job.

### Cost Structure Ratio [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Cost Structure Ratio = Entrant Unit Cost ÷ Incumbent Unit Cost`
Variables:
Entrant Unit Cost = cost to serve one customer or transaction.
Incumbent Unit Cost = comparable incumbent cost.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the entrant can survive at the lower price point.
How to interpret the output:
Value > 1.0 → not disruptive on cost → rethink the model
Value 0.5–1.0 → possible but fragile → improve automation and scope
Value < 0.5 → strong structural advantage → scale the wedge
Worked example with numbers: If an AI workflow platform serves a customer for $8/month while the incumbent support-heavy model costs $20/month, the ratio is 0.4. That gives room to underprice the market and still survive.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Sell a premium feature and call it disruption | Change the business model and economics |
| Start with the most demanding customers | Start with over-served users or non-consumers |
| Depend on temporary subsidies with no cost advantage | Build a cost structure that works at the lower price |
| Assume incumbents will react immediately | Model when they can ignore, copy, or fight back |
| Chase perfection before launch | Iterate quickly and improve the offer in-market |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI product enters through the low end  
Situation: An AI contract-review tool cannot beat enterprise legal suites on depth, but small law firms and in-house teams are over-served by the incumbents. They need speed, not every advanced clause workflow.  
Applicable framework/metric: Disruption Wedge Map.  
Analysis: The startup launches at $49 per user/month versus the incumbent's $250 per user/month. Relative price is 0.196, and the first version automates only the top 20% of repeat reviews.  
Decision rule: If the first segment is over-served and the product solves the core job cheaply, enter there first; if the user needs premium compliance on day one, do not start there.  
Action: Ship a focused review workflow, then expand into heavier legal operations after adoption proves the wedge.

Scenario 2: Consulting product displaces manual reporting  
Situation: A consulting firm sells weekly performance packs built by analysts. Clients tolerate the service but do not value the manual polish enough to justify the cost.  
Applicable framework/metric: Cost Structure Ratio.  
Analysis: Manual reporting costs $18 per account per week; automated reporting costs $5. The ratio is 0.28, so the product can underprice the legacy model and still make margin.  
Decision rule: If the cost ratio is below 0.5, the model can likely support a disruption wedge; if it is above 1.0, it is not a real disruptive offer.  
Action: Automate the standard pack, keep human review only for exceptions, and use the savings to lower price.

Scenario 3: Product team watches incumbent response  
Situation: A cloud productivity startup is winning small teams with a simpler collaboration tool. A larger incumbent begins copying a subset of features, but it keeps the old enterprise pricing and procurement flow.  
Applicable framework/metric: Incumbent Response Matrix.  
Analysis: The entrant has 4,000 active teams, 18% month-over-month growth, and high retention among small users. The incumbent can copy features, but the new pricing and workflow are still materially different.  
Decision rule: If the incumbent can copy the feature but not the economics, keep moving; if it can copy both quickly, widen the wedge or specialize further.  
Action: Double down on self-serve onboarding, lower price friction, and speed of iteration before the incumbent learns the new model.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Identify over-served users, non-consumers, or least-profitable segments.
2. Define the good-enough product that solves the core job at a lower cost.
3. Build a cost structure that is viable at the lower price point.
4. Launch with fast iteration instead of broad feature depth.
5. Measure price ratio, cost ratio, and adoption in the first segment.
6. Watch incumbent behavior and update the wedge if they copy features.
7. Expand upward only after the low-end model proves it can scale.
