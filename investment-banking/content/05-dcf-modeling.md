# DCF Modeling

## Overview

A Discounted Cash Flow model estimates what a business is worth today by projecting its future free cash flows and discounting them back to the present using a rate that reflects the risk of those cash flows. It is the most fundamental valuation method in investment banking because it values a company based on its own ability to generate cash rather than relying on what other companies trade at. The output is an intrinsic value per share.

---

## Why It Matters

Every major deal in investment banking uses a DCF as one of its core valuation tools. When a bank advises on an acquisition, issues a fairness opinion, or prices an IPO, the DCF provides an anchor valuation based on fundamentals. Getting the assumptions wrong by even a few percentage points on growth or discount rate can swing the valuation by billions of dollars, directly affecting deal outcomes and client wealth.

## Key Principles

- Free cash flow to the firm equals operating cash flow minus capital expenditures, representing cash available to all capital providers
- The discount rate is usually the weighted average cost of capital, blending the cost of equity and after-tax cost of debt
- Terminal value typically accounts for 60-80% of total DCF value, making growth and exit multiple assumptions critical
- Sensitivity analysis tests how the valuation changes when key assumptions like growth rate or WACC shift up or down

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Free Cash Flow** | Cash generated by operations minus capital expenditures, available to debt and equity holders |
| **WACC** | Weighted Average Cost of Capital, the blended discount rate reflecting the cost of all funding sources |
| **Terminal Value** | The estimated value of the business beyond the explicit forecast period, usually calculated as a perpetuity or exit multiple |
| **Sensitivity Analysis** | A technique that tests how changes in key assumptions affect the final valuation output |

## Use Case

An investment bank advising on a $5 billion acquisition builds a DCF model projecting the target company's free cash flows for the next ten years. The model uses a 9% WACC and a 2.5% terminal growth rate to arrive at an enterprise value range of $4.6 to $5.8 billion, giving the acquirer confidence that the offered price is fair.

## Scenario

> A private equity firm considered buying a specialty chemicals company for $3 billion. Its analysts built a DCF with 7% revenue growth, 22% EBITDA margins, and a 10% WACC. The base case yielded $2.8 billion. However, sensitivity analysis showed that if margins improved to 25% through operational efficiencies, the value jumped to $3.5 billion. The firm proceeded with the deal, targeting margin expansion as the key value creation lever.

## Examples

- JPMorgan building a DCF for a tech company IPO that showed intrinsic value of $45-$55 per share, which anchored the pricing discussion during the roadshow
- An analyst discovering through sensitivity analysis that a retail target's DCF value dropped 30% if same-store sales growth fell from 3% to 1%, flagging a critical risk for the acquirer

## 1. Topic Snapshot
DCF values a business by projecting future free cash flows and discounting them back to today using a rate that reflects risk.
For an IT, AI, Product, or Consulting leader, it is the cleanest way to test whether a deal price, build-vs-buy choice, or internal investment case is justified by cash, not just market mood.
The source is short but the decision is real: is the business worth what the seller says, and which assumptions have to be true for that answer to hold?

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### DCF Build Stack
**Purpose:** Show how the model converts operations into value.

```text
Revenue -> margin -> FCFF -> discount rate -> present value -> EV -> equity bridge
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: operating drivers, which include revenue growth, margins, capex, and working capital.
Component 2: discounting, which translates future cash into present value.
Component 3: bridge, which converts enterprise value into equity value.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS target with high ARR growth but heavy SBC should be reviewed through FCFF rather than headline revenue alone, because the real question is whether growth produces cash after reinvestment.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is debating valuation without agreeing on the driver tree.

### Terminal Value Selector
**Purpose:** Choose a terminal value method without drifting into wishful thinking.

```text
Is the business stable? -> use Gordon
Are market comps reliable? -> cross-check exit multiple
If neither is stable, extend explicit forecast or fade
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: stability, which tells you whether perpetuity assumptions are reasonable.
Component 2: market anchoring, which checks the result against comps.
Component 3: fade logic, which gradually moves from high growth to steady state.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI platform with no mature terminal state should not use a very aggressive perpetual growth rate; it should be modeled with a fade and an implied multiple cross-check.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Right before finalizing the DCF output.

### Sensitivity Grid and Football Field
**Purpose:** Make uncertainty visible and compare methods side by side.

```text
DCF range | comps range | precedent range | chosen bid / value
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: WACC, which usually has the biggest impact.
Component 2: terminal growth, which often drives a large share of value.
Component 3: valuation triangulation, which keeps the DCF honest.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting M&A team shows DCF, trading comps, and precedents together, then explains why the DCF is below the comps range because the target is still under-earning its potential.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When senior stakeholders want one clean number but the economics are still noisy.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source]; they are practical DCF mechanics for banking decisions.

### Formula 1: Enterprise Value
Formula: `EV = Σ(FCFF_t / (1 + WACC)^t) + TV / (1 + WACC)^n`

Why this formula exists: It converts future cash into a present-day enterprise value.

How to interpret the output:
Higher EV -> stronger cash generation or lower discount rate
Lower EV -> weaker economics or higher risk

Worked example: If explicit FCFF PV is 410 and PV of TV is 1,137, EV is 1,547.

### Formula 2: Gordon Growth Terminal Value
Formula: `TV = FCFF_(n+1) / (WACC - g)`

Why this formula exists: It values a stable business as a perpetuity that grows modestly.

How to interpret the output:
WACC - g too small -> unstable / inflated TV
Reasonable spread -> more defensible result

Worked example: If FCFF next year is 175 × 1.03 and WACC is 12% with g at 3%, TV is about 2,003.

### Formula 3: Equity Value Bridge
Formula: `Equity Value = EV - Net Debt - Minority Interest + Cash`

Why this formula exists: It shows what equity holders actually own after the balance sheet is considered.

How to interpret the output:
Positive bridge -> equity value below enterprise value due to debt
Large cash balance -> equity value can exceed simple EV expectations

Worked example: EV 1,547 minus net debt 120 gives equity value of 1,427 before minority and other adjustments.

### Formula 4: Implied Growth Check
Formula: `Implied g = WACC - (FCFF_(n+1) / TV)`

Why this formula exists: It tests whether the terminal assumption is secretly too optimistic.

How to interpret the output:
Implied g near nominal GDP -> reasonable
Implied g above long-run GDP -> likely aggressive

Worked example: If implied g comes out at 4.5% in a mature market, the reviewer should ask whether that is a base case or a bull case.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Match FCFF with cost of equity or FCFE with WACC | Keep the numerator and discount rate aligned |
| Treat terminal value as a small line item | Recognize that TV often drives most of the valuation |
| Use an aggressive terminal growth rate without a check | Cap g near long-run nominal GDP and cross-check the implied multiple |
| Present a point estimate with no range | Show a sensitivity grid and a football field |
| Ignore SBC in tech-heavy companies | Treat dilution as part of real valuation economics |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: SaaS acquisition
Situation: An investment bank is valuing a SaaS target with strong ARR growth, sticky customers, and a large share of value sitting in the terminal year.
Applicable framework/metric: DCF Build Stack and Terminal Value Selector.
Analysis: The base DCF says the target is worth less than the seller's asking price, but the comps suggest buyers are paying for growth optionality. The DCF is still useful because it isolates whether cash generation supports the story.
Decision rule: "If the DCF is below comps, decide whether the gap is growth optionality or overpricing."
Action: Use DCF as the anchor, show the football field, and negotiate around the assumptions that move the value most.

### Scenario 2: Build vs buy for an AI platform
Situation: A product organization is deciding whether to build an internal AI inference stack or buy a vendor solution.
Applicable framework/metric: Sensitivity Grid and Equity Value Bridge.
Analysis: The internal build has positive NPV if adoption hits the planned level and operating savings show up on time. If adoption falls short, the NPV flips quickly because the upfront investment is real.
Decision rule: "If the adoption assumption is the biggest swing factor, gate the spend with a pilot milestone."
Action: Run a pilot, tie the next tranche to usage thresholds, and review the IRR against the internal hurdle rate.

### Scenario 3: Terminal value abuse in a consulting diligence memo
Situation: A consulting team sees a DCF where terminal growth is set too close to the economy's long-run growth rate.
Applicable framework/metric: Implied Growth Check.
Analysis: The TV becomes inflated, and the model is doing most of the work instead of the operations. A quick implied-growth check shows the model is effectively assuming perpetual overperformance.
Decision rule: "If g is too close to WACC, the valuation is unstable and should be stressed."
Action: Reduce g, extend the explicit forecast if needed, and present a lower, more defensible range.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Define the valuation purpose: bid, fairness opinion, internal project, or build-vs-buy.
2. Build the operating drivers first so the forecast is tied to real economics.
3. Set WACC using a target capital structure and a current beta check.
4. Choose a terminal method and cross-check it with an implied multiple or implied growth rate.
5. Bridge from EV to equity with a dated net-debt schedule.
6. Stress-test the model with a WACC × g grid and a tornado chart.
7. Present DCF, trading comps, and precedents together in a football field.
8. Document assumptions, sources, and reviewer sign-offs before the model is used externally.
