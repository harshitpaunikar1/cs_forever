# Role of Investment Banks

## Overview

Investment banks act as intermediaries between companies that need capital and investors who have it. They help businesses raise money by issuing stocks or bonds, advise on mergers and acquisitions, and facilitate large financial transactions. Unlike commercial banks that take deposits and make loans, investment banks focus on capital markets, deal-making, and advisory services.

---

## Why It Matters

Without investment banks, companies would struggle to access the capital markets efficiently. These institutions reduce friction in fundraising, provide expert valuation guidance, and connect buyers with sellers in complex transactions. When a startup wants to go public or a Fortune 500 company wants to acquire a competitor, investment banks make the deal happen faster, cheaper, and with less risk.

## Key Principles

- Investment banks earn revenue through advisory fees, underwriting spreads, and trading commissions
- The Chinese wall separates advisory (private information) from trading (public markets) to prevent conflicts of interest
- Bulge bracket banks handle the largest global deals while boutique firms specialize in specific industries or deal types
- Relationship banking drives deal flow because trust and track record determine which bank gets hired

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Bulge Bracket** | The largest global investment banks such as Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and Morgan Stanley |
| **Boutique Bank** | A smaller advisory firm specializing in specific industries or deal sizes |
| **Underwriting** | The process of guaranteeing the sale of a new securities issue by purchasing it from the issuer |
| **Chinese Wall** | An information barrier within a bank that prevents conflicts of interest between departments |

## Use Case

A mid-size technology company generating $200 million in annual revenue wants to raise $500 million through an IPO. The company hires an investment bank to structure the offering, set the price range, market the shares to institutional investors, and manage the listing on the stock exchange.

## Scenario

> A healthcare company needed $300 million to fund a new drug pipeline. It hired a bulge bracket bank to underwrite a convertible bond offering. The bank priced the bonds at a 2% coupon with a 30% conversion premium, attracting institutional investors who wanted downside protection with equity upside. The offering was oversubscribed 3x, and the company secured its funding in under six weeks.

## Examples

- Goldman Sachs advising Arm Holdings on its $54 billion IPO, managing the roadshow and pricing the shares for maximum investor demand
- Evercore, a boutique bank, advising Dell Technologies on its $24 billion return-to-public-markets transaction because of its deep tech sector expertise

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Deal Flow Ladder
**Purpose:** Show how a company moves from financing need to executed transaction.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Need capital or liquidity
   -> select bank
      -> mandate
         -> diligence / valuation
            -> roadshow or buyer process
               -> pricing / signing / closing
```

Axes / Components explained:
Need capital or liquidity: the reason the process starts.
Select bank: choose the advisor with the right reach and expertise.
Mandate: the bank’s formal authority to run the process.
Diligence / valuation: pricing and materials preparation.
Roadshow or buyer process: investor marketing or strategic buyer outreach.
Pricing / signing / closing: the transaction outcome.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company preparing an IPO needs a bank that can manage both investor education and comparables analysis. The ladder helps the leadership team see that the bank is not just a salesperson; it is the process owner for pricing and execution.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team asks “why do we need an investment bank at all?”

### Bank Selection Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether to hire a bulge bracket, boutique, or specialist adviser.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    TRANSACTION COMPLEXITY
                Low                          High
CLIENT NEED
Speed / reach   Boutique / regional bank   Bulge bracket
Sector depth    Boutique specialist        Elite boutique / focused advisor
Multi-product    Regional coverage bank     Bulge bracket + syndicate support
```

Axes / Components explained:
Transaction complexity: size, geography, and product mix.
Speed / reach: broad distribution and market access.
Sector depth: understanding the industry’s economics.
Multi-product: ability to coordinate equity, debt, and M&A.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A mid-cap AI software firm selling a minority stake may prefer a boutique with deep software knowledge, while a cross-border telecom acquisition may require a bulge bracket for global distribution and complex financing.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When deciding between a specialist advisor and a global firm.

### Conflict-Control Matrix
**Purpose:** Manage the tension between advisory work and market activities.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                   CONFLICT RISK
               Low                           High
INFORMATION SENSITIVITY
Public data     Standard controls            Routine Chinese wall
Private data    Restricted access            Strong Chinese wall + monitoring
Deal-sensitive  Tight ring-fencing           Highest compliance scrutiny
```

Axes / Components explained:
Conflict risk: likelihood that one desk benefits unfairly from another’s information.
Information sensitivity: whether the data is public, private, or price-sensitive.
Chinese wall: formal barrier to protect client confidentiality.
Monitoring: compliance checks, logs, and approvals.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: If a bank advises a software company on an acquisition while also trading its stock, the advisory team’s information must not leak into the trading desk. The conflict matrix is the compliance lens for that risk.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When legal, compliance, or the board asks how private information is being protected.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Underwriting Fee
Formula: `Underwriting fee = Issue size × fee spread`

Variables:
Issue size = total amount raised
fee spread = percentage charged by the bank

Why this formula exists: It shows how banks monetize capital raising.

How to interpret the output:
Value higher than expected -> the bank may be charging for scarcity or complexity
Value in line with market -> normal execution economics
Value too low -> the issuer may be pushing for tighter economics

Worked example with numbers: If a company raises $500M and the underwriting spread is 2%, the bank earns $10M. That fee must cover marketing, diligence, syndicate management, and execution risk.

### Formula 2: Oversubscription Ratio
Formula: `Oversubscription ratio = Orders received / Issue size`

Variables:
Orders received = total demand from investors
Issue size = shares or bonds offered

Why this formula exists: It measures how strong the book is before pricing.

How to interpret the output:
Value above 1.0 -> excess demand -> pricing power
Value near 1.0 -> balanced demand -> steady pricing
Value below 1.0 -> weak demand -> pricing risk

Worked example with numbers: If a bond issue of $300M receives $900M of orders, oversubscription is 3.0x. That gives the bank room to tighten pricing or allocate selectively.

### Formula 3: Interest Coverage
Formula: `Interest coverage = EBITDA / Interest expense`

Variables:
EBITDA = earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization
Interest expense = annual debt cost

Why this formula exists: It helps DCM bankers judge whether debt is supportable.

How to interpret the output:
Value above 3x -> healthy debt capacity
Value 2x-3x -> watch closely
Value below 2x -> debt may be too risky

Worked example with numbers: If EBITDA is $120M and interest expense is $30M, coverage is 4x. That supports a debt capital markets conversation more comfortably than a 1.5x profile would.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Don't hire a bank only because it is famous. | Do match bank capabilities to the actual transaction type and complexity. |
| Don't confuse underwriting with pure advice. | Do separate advisory, distribution, and balance-sheet risk. |
| Don't ignore conflicts between research, trading, and advisory. | Do insist on clean Chinese-wall controls and documented compliance. |
| Don't price a new issue without checking demand. | Do use bookbuilding and oversubscription to refine pricing. |
| Don't assume one bank can do everything equally well. | Do split roles when sector depth, distribution, or execution needs differ. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: IPO for a B2B SaaS Company
**Situation:** A software company wants to raise $400M in an IPO and needs market education, analyst support, and strong institutional distribution.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Deal Flow Ladder + Bank Selection Matrix.

**Analysis:** A bulge bracket bank may be useful because the offer needs broad distribution and strong ECM execution. If the issue is oversubscribed 2.5x during bookbuilding, the company can price near the top of the range.

**Decision rule:** If global demand and analyst coverage matter more than fee minimization, choose a bulge bracket. If the deal is narrower and sector-specific, a boutique may be enough.

**Action:** Run a dual-track pitch process and choose the bank that can both price the deal and defend it in the market.

### Scenario 2: Debt Financing for a Growth Company
**Situation:** A growth company wants to issue convertible notes to fund expansion without diluting shareholders immediately.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Debt Capital Markets lens + Interest Coverage.

**Analysis:** If coverage is comfortably above 3x, the company can likely support the new debt. The bank’s role is to structure coupon, tenor, and conversion premium so the instrument is attractive to institutional buyers.

**Decision rule:** If interest coverage is below 2x, reduce leverage or shorten the deal size. If demand is weak, reprice or add conversion upside.

**Action:** Prepare a debt investor deck, test the coupon range, and set the conversion terms after early order feedback.

### Scenario 3: Sell-Side M&A for a Mid-Market Firm
**Situation:** A mid-market product company wants to sell itself to a strategic buyer.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Conflict-Control Matrix + Underwriting/Advisory separation.

**Analysis:** The bank should run a clean sale process, manage confidential information carefully, and prevent leakage to trading or research teams. A strong process can increase the final premium by broadening the buyer set and reducing information asymmetry.

**Decision rule:** If the buyer universe is fragmented, use a bank with sector coverage and process discipline. If the company is highly niche, focus on boutique expertise and buyer targeting.

**Action:** Launch a controlled teaser process, set confidentiality rules, and run staged outreach to strategic buyers.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Define the transaction goal first: capital raise, liquidity, sale, or liability management.
2. Match the bank type to the need: distribution-heavy, sector-heavy, or process-heavy.
3. Ask for a concrete execution plan, not just a pitch about relationships.
4. Check conflicts, walling procedures, and role separation before signing a mandate.
5. Use bookbuilding or buyer feedback to refine valuation rather than anchoring on one number.
6. Track fees, timing, and execution quality against the bank’s promise.
7. Reassess the bank choice if the process changes from one product type to another.
