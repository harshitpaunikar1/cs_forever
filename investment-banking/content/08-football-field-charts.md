# Football Field Charts

## Overview

A football field chart is a horizontal bar chart that displays valuation ranges from multiple methodologies side by side. Each bar represents a different approach such as DCF, trading comps, precedent transactions, and LBO analysis. The chart makes it easy to see where the ranges overlap, which methods agree, and where the proposed deal price falls relative to the full spectrum of values.

---

## Why It Matters

Boards and executives rarely trust a single valuation number. They want to see how different methods compare and where consensus forms. The football field chart is the standard visual in investment banking pitch books and fairness opinions because it communicates complex valuation analysis in one clear picture. The overlap zone typically becomes the negotiation range for deal pricing.

## Key Principles

- Each bar should show the low, median, and high values from that valuation methodology
- The chart works best when it includes at least three different valuation approaches for triangulation
- The current share price or proposed deal price is marked as a reference line so stakeholders can see where it falls
- Wider bars indicate more uncertainty in a methodology while narrow bars suggest tighter consensus

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Football Field Chart** | A horizontal bar chart showing valuation ranges from multiple methodologies on the same scale |
| **Valuation Range** | The low-to-high spread of implied values produced by a single valuation methodology |
| **Triangulation** | Using multiple independent valuation methods to converge on a reasonable price range |
| **Reference Price** | The current market price or proposed deal price marked on the chart for comparison |

## Use Case

A bank preparing a fairness opinion for a $10 billion merger creates a football field chart with four bars: DCF ($8.5-$11.2B), trading comps ($9.0-$10.5B), precedent transactions ($9.8-$12.0B), and LBO analysis ($8.0-$9.5B). The overlap zone of $9.8-$10.5B brackets the proposed $10 billion price, supporting the fairness conclusion.

## Scenario

> During a contested takeover, the target company's board received a $45 per share bid. The bank's football field chart showed the DCF range at $42-$52, comps at $40-$48, and precedent transactions at $46-$55. The board used the chart to argue that $45 was at the low end of the fair range and successfully negotiated a bump to $50, which fell in the overlap zone of all three methods.

## Examples

- A pitch book for a sell-side mandate showing five valuation bars where the overlap between DCF and precedent transactions at $22-$26 per share became the recommended asking price range
- An analyst presenting a football field chart to a PE fund's investment committee where the LBO bar was entirely below the seller's asking price, visually demonstrating the deal would not meet the fund's return threshold

## 1. Topic Snapshot
A football field chart is the standard investment-banking visual for comparing valuation ranges from multiple methods on one axis. For IT, AI, product, and consulting leaders, it matters because it shows where DCF, trading comps, precedent transactions, and LBO output agree, and where a proposed price sits inside or outside the credible range.

The decision it supports is whether a proposed deal price is defensible, aggressive, or out of bounds. It is also the quickest way to communicate uncertainty without hiding the underlying methods.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Valuation Triangulation Stack
**Purpose:** Show why multiple methods are used together.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
DCF -> intrinsic lens
trading comps -> market lens
precedent transactions -> control lens
LBO -> buyer return lens
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
DCF: what the business is worth based on cash generation.
Trading comps: what the market is paying for similar public companies.
Precedent transactions: what buyers paid in actual deals.
LBO: what a financial buyer can afford and still earn a return.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud platform is valued at $10 billion on DCF, $9.2 billion on comps, $10.8 billion on precedents, and $8.9 billion on LBO. The overlap around $9.2-$10.0 billion becomes the negotiation zone.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone asks why the bank does not use one "true" valuation number.

### Football Field Build Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide how to turn method outputs into a usable chart.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
method output -> low / median / high -> align scale -> plot bars -> add reference price
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Low, median, high: the valuation spread for each method.
Common scale: required so the chart is comparable.
Reference line: the current or proposed price.
Overlap zone: the range where methods agree.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A sell-side pitch book plots DCF, comps, and precedent transactions on the same scale and marks the proposed ask price as a line to show whether it sits inside the overlap zone.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team needs a board-ready visual rather than a spreadsheet.

### Negotiation Range Matrix
**Purpose:** Translate the overlap zone into a negotiation strategy.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
below overlap -> bargain to improve
inside overlap -> defend and close
above overlap -> justify with special factors
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Below overlap: likely too cheap for sellers.
Inside overlap: usually defensible.
Above overlap: needs a strong story or buyer-specific logic.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A $45 bid in a range where the overlap zone is $46-$50 is a starting point for negotiation, not a final answer.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the board asks whether to push back on an offer.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: `Valuation range = [low, median, high] across each method`
Variables:
Low = conservative case for each method
Median = central case
High = optimistic case
Why this formula exists: It gives the chart its structure and communicates uncertainty.
How to interpret the output:
Wide range -> high uncertainty
Narrow range -> stronger consensus
Overlapping ranges -> stronger negotiation anchor
Worked example with numbers: DCF = $8.5-$11.2B, comps = $9.0-$10.5B, precedents = $9.8-$12.0B, LBO = $8.0-$9.5B.

Formula: `Overlap zone = intersection of credible valuation ranges`
Variables:
Credible range = each method's accepted low/high
Intersection = values supported by multiple methods
Why this formula exists: It answers where the price is most defensible.
How to interpret the output:
No overlap -> methods disagree sharply
Small overlap -> price needs careful justification
Large overlap -> negotiation comfort zone
Worked example with numbers: The overlap of comps and precedent transactions is $9.8-$10.5B.

Formula: `Reference gap = Proposed price - Midpoint of overlap zone`
Variables:
Proposed price = bid or ask price under review
Midpoint of overlap zone = center of the agreed range
Why this formula exists: It shows how far the ask is from the consensus.
How to interpret the output:
Negative gap -> price is below the middle
Near zero -> price is aligned with consensus
Large positive gap -> price may be too aggressive
Worked example with numbers: If the overlap midpoint is $10.15B and the price is $10.0B, the gap is -$150M.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Use a football field with one method only. | Show at least three valuation methods for triangulation. |
| Hide a weak method by changing the scale. | Keep one common scale across the entire chart. |
| Present the chart without a reference price. | Mark the current or proposed price clearly. |
| Treat the overlap zone as certainty. | Explain that it is a negotiation range, not a guarantee. |
| Leave out the assumptions behind each bar. | Explain what drives the low, median, and high values. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Fairness opinion for a merger
Situation: A board needs a fairness opinion for a merger and wants to know whether the bid price is acceptable.
Applicable framework/metric: Valuation Triangulation Stack.
Analysis: If DCF is $8.5-$11.2B, comps are $9.0-$10.5B, and precedents are $9.8-$12.0B, the overlap zone brackets $9.8-$10.5B. A $10.0B bid sits comfortably inside that range.
Decision rule: If the bid is inside the overlap and the assumptions are sound, it is defensible. If it is below the overlap, push for more. If it is above, verify strategic justification.
Action: Use the chart in the board memo and explain which method is driving the range.

### Scenario 2: Private equity buyer testing return constraints
Situation: A PE fund wants to buy a business but needs to know if the entry price works.
Applicable framework/metric: LBO and Negotiation Range Matrix.
Analysis: If the LBO bar sits entirely below the seller's ask, the deal cannot clear the fund's return hurdle. If the ask is within the overlap zone, the fund can negotiate from a position of evidence.
Decision rule: Proceed only if the price fits both the valuation range and the return model.
Action: Rework the leverage, the exit multiple, or the price before bidding.

### Scenario 3: Sell-side pitch book for a software company
Situation: An advisor is marketing a software company and needs a clean, fast visual for the board.
Applicable framework/metric: Football Field Build Matrix.
Analysis: Plot DCF, comps, precedents, and LBO on the same scale, then show a reference price line. If one method is far outside the others, explain the outlier or remove it if the logic is weak.
Decision rule: Keep the chart simple enough for a board packet but precise enough to show the negotiation range.
Action: Add the assumptions slide directly after the chart so the team can see what drives the spread.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Gather valuation outputs from DCF, trading comps, precedent transactions, and LBO.
2. Standardize all outputs to the same units and scale.
3. Extract low, median, and high values from each method.
4. Plot the bars and add a reference price line.
5. Identify the overlap zone and label the negotiation range.
6. Write the assumptions behind each bar in a footnote or appendix.
7. Use the chart as a conversation tool, not a substitute for judgment.
