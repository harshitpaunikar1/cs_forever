# Equity Capital Markets

## Overview

Equity Capital Markets, or ECM, is the division within an investment bank that helps companies raise money by selling ownership stakes. ECM covers IPOs, follow-on offerings, rights issues, block trades, and convertible securities. The team sits between the advisory bankers who win the client relationship and the sales and trading desks that distribute the shares to investors.

---

## Why It Matters

ECM transactions put real money into companies that need it to grow, acquire, or restructure. The equity markets are the primary engine for funding innovation and expansion across the global economy. For investment banks, ECM is a major revenue driver through underwriting fees that typically range from 2-7% of the total offering size. Execution quality in ECM directly affects the bank's reputation and future deal flow.

## Key Principles

- Timing the market matters enormously because equity offerings perform better in bull markets when investor appetite is strong
- Follow-on offerings must be priced at a discount to the current market price to attract buyers, typically 3-7%
- Accelerated bookbuilds allow companies to raise equity overnight, reducing market exposure risk
- Block trades let large shareholders sell significant positions quickly with minimal market disruption

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Follow-on Offering** | A sale of additional shares by an already public company to raise new capital |
| **Rights Issue** | An offering that gives existing shareholders the right to buy new shares at a discount before they are offered to the public |
| **Block Trade** | The sale of a large block of shares by a single holder, typically executed overnight by an investment bank |
| **Convertible Bond** | A debt instrument that can be converted into equity shares at a predetermined price |

## Use Case

A publicly traded airline needs $3 billion to modernize its fleet after a period of depressed travel demand. Its bankers recommend a follow-on offering rather than more debt because the balance sheet is already leveraged. The bank prices the offering at a 5% discount to the closing price, runs an overnight accelerated bookbuild, and places the shares with 40 institutional investors by morning.

## Scenario

> A large private equity firm wanted to exit 20% of its stake in a recently IPO'd fintech company worth $15 billion. The bank executed an overnight block trade, offering the $3 billion share block at a 4% discount to the closing price. By 7 AM, the book was covered 2.5 times over. The stock dropped only 2% at market open, well within expectations, and recovered within a week.

## Examples

- Tesla raising $5 billion through at-the-market equity offerings in 2020, selling shares gradually into the open market to fund factory expansion without a traditional offering discount
- A mining company using a rights issue to raise $2 billion for a new project, giving existing shareholders priority so they could maintain their ownership percentage

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Equity Capital Markets (ECM) is the part of investment banking that turns a companys equity story into fresh capital, shareholder liquidity, or both. It covers IPOs, follow-on offerings, rights issues, block trades, convertibles, and accelerated bookbuilds.

For IT, AI, product, and consulting leaders, ECM is the discipline that answers three questions:
What should be sold, to whom, and at what price without breaking the market signal?

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### ECM Instrument Selection Matrix
**Purpose:** Choose the right equity channel for the companys objective.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Need new cash?     Yes -> primary issue path
Need only liquidity? Yes -> secondary / block path
Need to protect control? -> rights issue or selective placement
Need speed? -> ABB or block trade
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Need for new cash: whether the company treasury should receive proceeds.
Need for liquidity: whether the main goal is shareholder exit.
Need for speed: whether the market window is short.
Control sensitivity: whether dilution must be minimized.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A mid-growth SaaS platform with predictable ARR may use a follow-on to fund product expansion, while its founder may prefer a secondary sale later if the goal is partial liquidity rather than operating cash.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When finance, founders, and the bank are arguing about whether the deal is a financing or an exit.

### Pricing and Demand Curve
**Purpose:** Show how price affects demand and aftermarket quality.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
lower price -> broader demand -> higher certainty
higher price -> thinner demand -> higher execution risk
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Price: the offer level the market must accept.
Demand: the amount of capital willing to buy.
Coverage: how many times the deal is subscribed.
Aftermarket risk: probability of weak trading or poor sentiment.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI infrastructure company can push pricing only if long-only demand stays intact; if the book becomes hedge-fund heavy, the price is probably too ambitious.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the book is open but the allocation team is unsure whether to tighten or widen the range.

### ECM Execution Loop
**Purpose:** Map the operational sequence from idea to settlement.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
strategy -> investor targeting -> bookbuild -> pricing -> allocation -> settlement -> aftermarket monitoring
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Strategy: why the deal exists.
Targeting: who should buy.
Bookbuild: how orders are collected.
Pricing and allocation: where the final decision is made.
Aftermarket monitoring: whether the stock behaves as expected.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting-backed software company may pre-wire cornerstone investors, run a compressed bookbuild, and then watch trading on day one to see whether the story survived contact with the market.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team needs a step-by-step operating model instead of a generic capital markets overview.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Gross Proceeds
Formula: `Gross proceeds = Offer price x Primary shares sold`

Why this formula exists: It shows how much cash the company raises before fees.
How to interpret the output:
High proceeds -> more funding, more dilution
Low proceeds -> less dilution, but possibly underfunded
Worked example with numbers: Offer price = 20 and primary shares sold = 50 million, so gross proceeds = $1.0 billion.

### Formula 2: Dilution
Formula: `Dilution % = 1 - (Pre-issue shares / Post-issue shares)`

Why this formula exists: It shows how much existing ownership shrinks after issuance.
How to interpret the output:
Higher dilution -> stronger capital raise, weaker ownership retention
Lower dilution -> more control preserved
Worked example with numbers: 100 million pre-issue shares and 120 million post-issue shares gives dilution of 16.7%.

### Formula 3: Greenshoe Capacity
Formula: `Greenshoe shares = Base deal size x 15%`

Why this formula exists: It quantifies the usual stabilization buffer.
How to interpret the output:
Used greenshoe -> demand or stabilization support was needed
Unused greenshoe -> base deal was sufficient
Worked example with numbers: Base deal size = 80 million shares, so greenshoe capacity = 12 million shares.

## 5. Do vs Dont
| Dont | Do |
|------|----|
| Confuse fresh capital with shareholder liquidity | Separate primary issuance from secondary selling |
| Price the deal only from internal optimism | Use order quality and market feedback to set price |
| Ignore dilution math | Measure ownership impact before launch |
| Treat every buyer as equally valuable | Favor durable long-only demand when stability matters |
| Use a rights issue without checking subscription mechanics | Model take-up, discount, and control implications first |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Growth SaaS follow-on
Situation: A public SaaS company needs $300 million for AI product expansion.
Applicable framework/metric: ECM Instrument Selection Matrix, gross proceeds, dilution.
Analysis: A follow-on makes sense if the company can tolerate moderate dilution and the book is broad enough to absorb the issue without a large discount.
Decision rule: If dilution stays below a board-approved threshold and book coverage is above 2x, proceed.
Action: Prepare a use-of-proceeds case and test demand with anchor investors before launch.

### Scenario 2: Founder liquidity block trade
Situation: The founder of a listed fintech wants to sell 5% of the company without a public overhang.
Applicable framework/metric: Pricing and Demand Curve, block trade, free float.
Analysis: A block trade is better than a slow drip sale because it clears size overnight and limits market noise.
Decision rule: If the discount required for execution is acceptable and the market can absorb the free-float change, execute the block.
Action: Pre-sound institutions, set the discount, and run an accelerated bookbuild overnight.

### Scenario 3: Capital raise with ownership protection
Situation: A family-controlled industrials company wants capital but does not want control to fall below a critical threshold.
Applicable framework/metric: Rights issue, dilution, take-up rate.
Analysis: A rights issue lets existing shareholders maintain proportionate ownership if they subscribe, which is usually better than a straight follow-on for control-sensitive issuers.
Decision rule: If expected take-up is high and the discount is not punitive, a rights issue is the cleanest path.
Action: Communicate subscription mechanics early and secure anchor support from major holders.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Define whether the goal is capital, liquidity, or both.
2. Quantify dilution, discount, and expected proceeds before speaking to investors.
3. Pick the ECM format that fits speed, control, and market appetite.
4. Build an investor list that matches the desired shareholder base.
5. Stress test the book for weak demand, pricing pressure, and allocation trade-offs.
6. Decide in advance how much stabilization support the bank is allowed to use.
7. Monitor aftermarket trading and capture lessons for the next issue.
