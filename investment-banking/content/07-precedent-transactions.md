# Precedent Transactions

## Overview

Precedent transaction analysis values a company by looking at the prices paid in past acquisitions of similar businesses. The analyst identifies relevant completed deals, calculates the acquisition multiples, and applies them to the target's financials. This method captures the control premium that buyers historically pay above trading values, making it especially useful for M&A advisory work.

---

## Why It Matters

When a company is being sold, the board needs to know what similar businesses actually sold for, not just what they trade at in the public market. Precedent transactions provide that benchmark. They also help acquirers avoid overpaying by showing the range of multiples paid in comparable deals. Courts and regulators rely on precedent analysis when evaluating whether a transaction price is fair to shareholders.

## Key Principles

- Precedent multiples are typically higher than trading comps because they include a control premium of 20-40%
- Deal selection should prioritize recency, similar size, same industry, and comparable market conditions
- Older transactions may be less relevant because market conditions, interest rates, and industry dynamics change over time
- Synergy expectations embedded in past deal prices can inflate multiples beyond what a financial buyer would pay

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Control Premium** | The additional price a buyer pays above the market trading price to gain a controlling stake in a company |
| **Precedent Transaction** | A previously completed acquisition used as a benchmark for valuing a current deal |
| **Transaction Multiple** | The acquisition price divided by a financial metric like EBITDA, used to compare deal valuations |
| **Synergy Premium** | The portion of the acquisition price justified by expected cost savings or revenue gains from combining businesses |

## Use Case

A bank advising a pharmaceutical company on selling its consumer health division identifies twelve comparable consumer health acquisitions from the past five years. The deals ranged from 12x to 18x EBITDA with a median of 15x. Applying 15x to the division's $800 million EBITDA suggests a sale price around $12 billion.

## Scenario

> A mid-market investment bank was advising a logistics company on its sale. The team found eight comparable logistics acquisitions over the prior three years, with EV/EBITDA multiples ranging from 9x to 13x. Two recent deals by strategic acquirers paid 12-13x due to expected route synergies. The bank argued the target deserved the upper range because it had similar synergy potential, and the eventual sale closed at 12.5x EBITDA.

## Examples

- Bankers advising on the Activision Blizzard sale to Microsoft citing prior gaming acquisitions like Zynga and Bethesda to justify the $95 per share price as consistent with industry precedent
- A sell-side advisor excluding a distressed competitor's forced sale at 6x EBITDA from the precedent set because the circumstances were not representative of fair market conditions

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Precedent transactions value a business by looking at what buyers actually paid for similar companies in past deals. For IT, AI, product, and consulting leaders, the point is to translate market history into a control-value estimate that can support a sale, fairness review, or acquisition negotiation.

The decision it supports is whether a target is priced fairly relative to real transaction history, not just trading multiples. It is especially useful when control premium, synergy premium, and market conditions matter.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Comparable Deal Screen
**Purpose:** Decide which precedent transactions belong in the set.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
recency -> industry fit -> size fit -> market fit -> include / exclude
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Recency: how close the deal is to current conditions.
Industry fit: whether the target and precedent operate in similar businesses.
Size fit: whether scale and margin profile are comparable.
Market fit: whether interest rates and sentiment are similar enough to compare.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A bank valuing a SaaS sale excludes a five-year-old distressed hardware deal even if both are "technology" because the multiples are not comparable on economics or market conditions.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team is mixing too many deals into the comp set.

### Multiple Bridge
**Purpose:** Show how to move from trading value to transaction value.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
trading multiple + control premium + synergy premium = transaction multiple
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Trading multiple: what public markets pay.
Control premium: extra value for control.
Synergy premium: extra value for buyer-specific benefits.
Transaction multiple: the paid price benchmark.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A data-platform company trades at 10x EBITDA, but comparable strategic acquisitions closed at 12x to 14x because buyers could add cross-sell and cost synergies.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone asks why deal multiples exceed public market multiples.

### Precedent Valuation Range
**Purpose:** Turn a group of deals into a valuation band.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
deal multiples -> clean set -> median / quartiles -> implied enterprise value
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Clean set: comparable and non-distressed transactions only.
Median: middle value of the precedent set.
Range: lower and upper valuation limits.
Implied EV: multiple x target financial metric.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: Twelve consumer-health deals range from 12x to 18x EBITDA; the 15x median implies an enterprise value of about $12 billion on $800 million EBITDA.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the board needs a defensible price range rather than a single number.

### Synergy Adjustment Check
**Purpose:** Decide how much of the multiple is standalone value versus buyer-specific value.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
standalone value -> synergy estimate -> adjusted purchase price
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Standalone value: what the business is worth on its own.
Synergy estimate: value from combining businesses.
Adjusted price: what a strategic buyer may pay.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A logistics buyer can pay more for route density synergies, so the bank keeps the strategic premium separate from the base operating multiple.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a buyer claims the target is worth more because of their own synergy case.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: `Implied enterprise value = Transaction multiple x Target EBITDA`
Variables:
Transaction multiple = selected precedent EV/EBITDA multiple
Target EBITDA = target company's EBITDA
Why this formula exists: It translates market history into a price estimate.
How to interpret the output:
Value below seller expectations -> likely too low or comps are weak
Value near midpoint -> usually the base case
Value above seller expectations -> may depend on synergy or scarcity
Worked example with numbers: 15x EBITDA on $800 million EBITDA implies $12 billion enterprise value.

Formula: `Control premium = (Transaction price - Trading value) / Trading value`
Variables:
Transaction price = price paid in the precedent deal
Trading value = public market value or trading-equivalent value
Why this formula exists: It shows how much extra buyers paid for control.
How to interpret the output:
Value below 20% -> low control premium
Value 20%-40% -> typical range cited in many deals
Value above 40% -> strategic or scarcity-driven pricing
Worked example with numbers: If trading value is $100 and deal price is $130, control premium = 30%.

Formula: `Synergy premium = Transaction price - Standalone value`
Variables:
Transaction price = price in the precedent deal
Standalone value = value of the target without buyer-specific synergies
Why this formula exists: It separates normal value from buyer-specific value creation.
How to interpret the output:
Value near zero -> deal mostly reflected standalone economics
Value positive and large -> strategic buyer paid for integration upside
Worked example with numbers: If standalone value is $10 billion and purchase price is $11.5 billion, synergy premium = $1.5 billion.

Formula: `Selected valuation band = [P25 multiple x EBITDA, P75 multiple x EBITDA]`
Variables:
P25 multiple = lower quartile of selected deals
P75 multiple = upper quartile of selected deals
Why this formula exists: It gives a defensible lower and upper bound.
How to interpret the output:
Narrow band -> stable comp set
Wide band -> deal set is noisy or market conditions differ
Worked example with numbers: If multiples are 12x to 18x and EBITDA is $800 million, the band is $9.6 billion to $14.4 billion.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Mix distressed and normal deals in the same set. | Screen for recency, size, industry, and market fit. |
| Compare a public trading multiple directly to a control deal without adjustment. | Use a control premium bridge. |
| Let one outlier set the valuation. | Anchor on median and range, then explain exceptions. |
| Treat synergy as guaranteed. | Separate standalone value from buyer-specific synergy value. |
| Ignore market conditions at the time of the deal. | Adjust for rate and sentiment differences before drawing conclusions. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
### Scenario 1: Selling a consumer business
Situation: A board is evaluating a sale of a consumer division and needs a valuation anchored in completed deals, not just analyst estimates.
Applicable framework/metric: Precedent Valuation Range.
Analysis: If comparable deals range from 12x to 18x EBITDA and the target has $800 million EBITDA, the implied range is $9.6 billion to $14.4 billion. A median at 15x gives a $12 billion anchor.
Decision rule: If the target is above the range, the seller may be overreaching. If it is below the lower quartile, the comp set is probably too weak or too old.
Action: Present a range, identify the best comparables, and explain why the median is the most defensible point.

### Scenario 2: Strategic buyer paying more than trading comps
Situation: A strategic acquirer wants to buy a logistics platform and claims the target is worth much more than public multiples imply.
Applicable framework/metric: Multiple Bridge and Synergy Adjustment Check.
Analysis: If trading comps are 9x EBITDA but precedent strategic deals are 12x to 13x, the difference likely comes from control and synergy premiums. If the strategic buyer can prove route synergies worth $300 million NPV, a higher multiple is justifiable.
Decision rule: If synergies are real and measurable, justify a premium. If they are speculative, stay closer to the base multiple.
Action: Separate standalone value from synergy value in the board memo and deal model.

### Scenario 3: Excluding a forced sale
Situation: The advisor finds a low-multiple transaction that would pull the valuation down.
Applicable framework/metric: Comparable Deal Screen.
Analysis: If the deal was a distressed sale closed in a weak market and is far older than the rest of the set, it should be excluded or at least flagged. A 6x EBITDA forced sale is not comparable to a fair-market strategic deal at 12x.
Decision rule: Exclude distressed transactions unless the target is also distressed. Keep only deals that match the current context.
Action: Document the exclusion rationale and keep the valuation set clean.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Define the target company and the financial metric you will use, usually EBITDA.
2. Build a candidate list of precedent transactions by industry, size, and recency.
3. Remove distressed, outdated, and non-comparable deals.
4. Calculate transaction multiples and summarize the median and range.
5. Separate control premium from synergy premium where possible.
6. Translate the valuation range into enterprise value and equity value.
7. Explain the deal set and the exclusions clearly in the board or client memo.
