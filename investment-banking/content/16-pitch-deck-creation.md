# Pitch Deck Creation

## Overview

A pitch deck in investment banking is a presentation used to win advisory mandates, propose deal strategies, or market securities to investors. It combines financial analysis, market data, valuation work, and strategic recommendations into a polished document. Pitch decks are the primary tool bankers use to demonstrate expertise, build credibility, and persuade clients to hire them for transactions.

---

## Why It Matters

The pitch deck is often the first and most important impression a bank makes on a potential client. A compelling deck wins mandates worth millions in fees. A weak one loses the deal to a competitor. Beyond winning business, pitch decks also serve as the analytical backbone of live transactions, guiding board discussions, investor meetings, and negotiation strategies. The quality of the deck reflects the quality of the bank's thinking.

## Key Principles

- Every pitch deck should tell a clear story with a logical flow: situation, analysis, recommendation, and next steps
- Data-driven pages with original analysis differentiate a bank from competitors who rely on generic market slides
- The executive summary on page one or two must capture the key message in under 30 seconds of reading
- Visual consistency with clean formatting, aligned charts, and minimal text per slide signals professionalism

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Pitch Book** | A comprehensive presentation used to win advisory mandates or present deal recommendations to clients |
| **Credentials Page** | A section showcasing the bank's relevant deal experience and track record in the client's industry |
| **Situation Overview** | The opening section that describes the client's current position, market context, and strategic options |
| **CIM** | Confidential Information Memorandum, a detailed document sent to potential buyers describing the target company for sale |

## Use Case

A bank competing for a sell-side mandate prepares a 40-page pitch deck. It includes a market overview showing consolidation trends in the industry, a preliminary valuation using comps and DCF, a list of 30 likely acquirers ranked by strategic fit and financial capacity, a proposed timeline, and a credentials section highlighting five similar deals the bank recently closed.

## Scenario

> Three banks pitched for the right to advise a consumer brands company on a $2 billion divestiture. Two banks presented generic market slides and standard valuation pages. The winning bank included proprietary buyer feedback from informal conversations with six likely acquirers, showing specific interest levels and indicative valuation ranges. This original intelligence convinced the client that the bank could run a more effective process and command a higher price.

## Examples

- Goldman Sachs winning a mega-merger mandate by presenting a pitch deck that included a detailed synergy analysis showing $1.5 billion in cost savings, quantified with bottom-up estimates by business line
- A boutique bank differentiating its pitch by including a football field chart with five valuation methodologies and a recommended price range, while competitors only showed two methods

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Mandate Narrative Spine
**Purpose:** Force the deck to move from context to conclusion without meandering.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Situation -> Analysis -> Recommendation -> Next Steps
```

Axes / Components explained:
Situation: what changed, why now, and what the client is trying to solve.
Analysis: market data, valuation, and deal logic that support the recommendation.
Recommendation: the bank’s proposed route, valuation stance, or buyer list.
Next Steps: the immediate process, timeline, and decision needed from the client.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company wants a sell-side process. The deck starts with growth deceleration, moves into comps and DCF, recommends a targeted buyer universe, and closes with a process timetable. That structure helps the CEO decide whether the bank can run the sale, not just comment on it.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the room has good analysis but no clean story.

### Audience-Need Matrix
**Purpose:** Match slide emphasis to the decision-maker in the room.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                   CLIENT NEED
            Speed                         Depth
AUDIENCE
CEO      concise answer            strategic options and risk
CFO      valuation rigor            funding and downside cases
Board    decision support           governance and tradeoffs
Investors upside narrative          evidence and comparables
```

Axes / Components explained:
Audience: who is reading and what they care about.
Need: whether they need speed, detail, or evidence.
Concise answer: a short conclusion that can be absorbed fast.
Strategic options and risk: the longer context needed for approval.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A PE-backed product company’s CFO wants DCF support and downside cases, while the CEO wants the recommendation in one slide. The matrix prevents the deck from over-serving one audience and under-serving the rest.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the pitch has too much detail for the wrong audience.

### Evidence Ladder
**Purpose:** Rank slide content from generic proof to proprietary proof.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Generic market claim
-> Peer benchmark
-> Bank analysis
-> Proprietary buyer feedback
-> Decision-ready recommendation
```

Axes / Components explained:
Generic market claim: baseline context everyone can find.
Peer benchmark: public comps and market norms.
Bank analysis: valuation, buyer ranking, and scenario work.
Proprietary buyer feedback: original market intelligence.
Decision-ready recommendation: the slide that earns action.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A bank pitching a software carve-out includes public tech multiples, then adds a target list ranked by strategic fit and financing capacity, and finally cites buyer feedback from six informal calls. That progression is what makes the pitch feel differentiated.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the deck sounds smart but still feels generic.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Deck Win Score
Formula: `Deck win score = (clarity + credibility + originality + relevance) / 4`

Variables:
clarity = how easily the client sees the conclusion
credibility = how well the evidence supports the ask
originality = whether the deck includes non-generic insight
relevance = fit to the client’s transaction and audience

Why this formula exists: It converts a subjective pitch into a reviewable quality check.

How to interpret the output:
Value below 3.5 -> the deck is likely too generic
Value 3.5-4.5 -> workable but still vulnerable to a better pitch
Value above 4.5 -> strong chance of winning the meeting

Worked example with numbers: If clarity is 5, credibility is 4, originality is 5, and relevance is 4, the deck win score is 4.5. That tells the team the story is strong enough to take into a live pitch.

### Formula 2: Slide Impact Score
Formula: `Slide impact score = decision importance x evidence strength / text density`

Variables:
decision importance = how close the slide is to the client’s yes/no choice
evidence strength = quality of the data or analysis on the slide
text density = amount of reading required to understand the point

Why this formula exists: It keeps bankers from burying the key insight under wall-of-text slides.

How to interpret the output:
Value below 1 -> too much text, too little decision value
Value 1-3 -> acceptable support slide
Value above 3 -> high-leverage page that should stay near the front

Worked example with numbers: A recommendation slide with importance 5, evidence strength 4, and text density 5 gives a score of 4.0. That is a front-of-deck page, not an appendix page.

### Formula 3: Buyer Priority Score
Formula: `Buyer priority score = strategic fit + financial capacity + execution probability`

Variables:
strategic fit = how well the buyer matches the asset
financial capacity = whether the buyer can fund the deal
execution probability = likelihood the buyer can close

Why this formula exists: It helps the pitch book rank likely buyers instead of dumping a long list.

How to interpret the output:
Value below 6 -> low-priority buyer
Value 6-8 -> credible mid-tier target
Value above 8 -> top-call buyer for the process

Worked example with numbers: A strategic buyer scores 4 on fit, 3 on capacity, and 2 on execution probability for a total of 9. That buyer goes into the first call list.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Don't open with background that delays the conclusion. | Do lead with the executive summary and the recommendation. |
| Don't use generic market slides copied from other pitches. | Do add original analysis and proprietary buyer feedback. |
| Don't bury valuation in a dense appendix. | Do surface comps, DCF, and the football field chart early. |
| Don't rank buyers by name recognition alone. | Do rank them by strategic fit and financial capacity. |
| Don't write for every audience at once. | Do tune the story to the CEO, CFO, board, or investor. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: SaaS Sell-Side Mandate
**Situation:** A SaaS company with slowing growth wants to explore a sale, and the CEO is choosing between three banks that all claim to have strong software coverage. The winning pitch must show more than slide polish; it needs a buyer map, valuation discipline, and a believable process.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Mandate Narrative Spine + Buyer Priority Score.

**Analysis:** The deck ranks 30 possible acquirers. Ten score above 8, twelve score between 6 and 8, and the rest are weak fits. The bank also shows a DCF range that lines up with comps instead of pushing an unrealistic top-of-range headline.

**Decision rule:** If at least 10 buyers score above 8 and the valuation range is defendable, push for the mandate. If the buyer universe is thin or the valuation is vague, simplify the process or keep pitching.

**Action:** Build a short list, prepare buyer-specific positioning, and have the CEO summary ready for the first board call.

### Scenario 2: Consumer Divestiture Pitch
**Situation:** A consumer brands company is considering a $2 billion divestiture and wants the bank that can prove it understands buyer appetite, not just market multiples. Two banks show similar comps, but one adds informal buyer feedback from six likely acquirers.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Evidence Ladder + Deck Win Score.

**Analysis:** The generic deck stays at the market-claim level. The better deck climbs the ladder by showing direct interest levels, indicative valuation ranges, and a focused process timeline. That raises credibility and originality materially.

**Decision rule:** If the deck win score is above 4.5 and at least half the target buyers show real interest, move ahead. If the score is below 3.5, ask for a rewrite before the next meeting.

**Action:** Turn buyer feedback into a process slide and a negotiated timeline for outreach.

### Scenario 3: Mega-Merger Advisory Pitch
**Situation:** A bank is pitching a mega-merger and wants to stand out against larger competitors. One competitor has a famous brand, but the smaller bank has a sharper synergy analysis and a cleaner football field chart.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Slide Impact Score + Audience-Need Matrix.

**Analysis:** The board does not need thirty dense slides. It needs the strategic logic, the synergy case, and a clean range of valuation outcomes. When the deck is short, sharp, and evidence-heavy, it looks more credible.

**Decision rule:** If the main recommendation slides have impact scores above 3 and the board can absorb the story in under 30 minutes, the pitch is usable. If not, compress the narrative and move detail to the appendix.

**Action:** Rework the opening pages, remove redundancy, and keep only the evidence that changes the decision.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Write the executive summary first so the deck has a conclusion before it has decoration.
2. Build the story in the order the client will decide: situation, analysis, recommendation, next steps.
3. Pull comps, DCF, and any buyer list into the body of the deck, not just the appendix.
4. Add proprietary buyer feedback or other original insight where the mandate is competitive.
5. Use a credentials page that proves relevance, not just bank size.
6. Tune slide density to the room: CEO, CFO, board, or investor.
7. Finish with a clean ask that makes the next decision obvious.
