# Investor Positioning Strategies

## Overview

Investor positioning is how investment banks and companies craft the narrative and materials to attract the right investors at the right price. It involves identifying which investor types are the best fit, building an equity story that resonates with their criteria, and managing communication before, during, and after a transaction. The goal is to build a stable, long-term shareholder base that supports the stock price.

---

## Why It Matters

A company can have strong fundamentals but still see its stock underperform if it attracts the wrong investors. Short-term traders create volatility, while long-only institutional funds provide stability. Effective positioning ensures that the investors who buy during an IPO or follow-on offering actually understand and believe in the business, reducing post-deal selling pressure and supporting the valuation over time.

## Key Principles

- The equity story translates complex financials into a simple, compelling investment thesis that resonates with target investors
- Investor targeting segments the market into categories like long-only funds, hedge funds, sovereign wealth funds, and retail investors
- Anchor investors who commit early provide credibility and attract other participants
- Post-deal investor relations maintains the relationship and prevents information gaps that cause sell-offs

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Equity Story** | The narrative that explains why a company is a good investment including its growth drivers and competitive advantages |
| **Anchor Investor** | A large institutional investor that commits to a significant allocation early in the offering process |
| **Investor Targeting** | The process of identifying and prioritizing the most suitable investors for a particular offering |
| **Cornerstone Investment** | A pre-agreed investment by a strategic or institutional investor disclosed in the offering prospectus |

## Use Case

A Southeast Asian ride-hailing company preparing for a US listing works with its bankers to identify 200 target accounts. They segment investors into growth-focused tech funds, emerging market specialists, and transportation sector funds, then customize the roadshow pitch for each group to highlight the metrics that matter most to them.

## Scenario

> A Brazilian payments company planned a $2 billion follow-on offering. Its bankers analyzed the existing shareholder register and found that 40% of holders were short-term hedge funds from the IPO. They targeted 50 long-only funds with strong fintech track records, secured three anchor investors for 25% of the deal, and structured a one-day accelerated bookbuild. The new investor mix reduced stock volatility by 35% over the following quarter.

## Examples

- Spotify choosing a direct listing instead of a traditional IPO to let existing shareholders sell directly to the public, targeting sophisticated investors who already understood the subscription model
- SoftBank's Vision Fund acting as a cornerstone investor in multiple tech IPOs, providing instant credibility that attracted smaller institutional investors to fill out the order book

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Equity Story Pyramid
**Purpose:** Turn a complicated company into a narrative that investors can underwrite.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Market problem
   -> company solution
      -> growth drivers
         -> proof points
            -> valuation case
```

Axes / Components explained:
Market problem: the pain the company solves.
Company solution: the product or platform.
Growth drivers: what makes the business scale.
Proof points: metrics that make the story believable.
Valuation case: why the current price is justified.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A cloud AI company pitches investors by leading with customer pain, then showing renewal rates, expansion revenue, and operating leverage. The pyramid helps the team see that metrics matter only after the story is understandable.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the market is asking “why this company, why now?”

### Investor Segmentation Grid
**Purpose:** Match the offer to the capital profile of each investor type.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    TIME HORIZON
                Short                         Long
RISK APPETITE
High          Hedge funds / fast money     Growth funds / specialists
Low           Event-driven allocators      Long-only anchor base
```

Axes / Components explained:
Time horizon: how long the investor is likely to hold.
Risk appetite: how much volatility the investor can tolerate.
Fast money: can support demand but can also exit quickly.
Anchor base: creates post-deal stability.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product-led SaaS IPO should not over-index on short-term traders if the goal is stable ownership after listing. The grid makes that tradeoff visible before the roadshow starts.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When deciding whether the order book should be built around growth funds, anchors, or opportunistic traders.

### Post-Deal Support Loop
**Purpose:** Keep the shareholder base informed after pricing so the stock does not lose support.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Deal closed
   -> disclosures
      -> investor calls
         -> analyst coverage
            -> refreshed conviction
               -> stable ownership
```

Axes / Components explained:
Disclosures: updates that reduce uncertainty.
Investor calls: direct communication with holders.
Analyst coverage: third-party validation.
Conviction: continued willingness to hold the stock.
Stable ownership: lower volatility and better liquidity.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A public software company that explains churn, retention, and AI roadmap changes clearly after listing is more likely to keep long-only holders than a company that goes silent after the roadshow.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When post-IPO performance is weak and the team thinks the issue is only pricing.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Anchor Coverage
Formula: `Anchor coverage = Anchor commitments / Deal size`

Variables:
Anchor commitments = amount committed by anchor investors
Deal size = total issue size

Why this formula exists: It shows how much of the transaction is stabilized before opening to the wider market.

How to interpret the output:
Value above 0.25 -> solid pre-book support
Value around 0.10-0.25 -> moderate support
Value below 0.10 -> weak anchoring

Worked example with numbers: If anchors commit $150M on a $600M deal, coverage is 25%. That usually helps the bank market the deal with more confidence.

### Formula 2: Long-Only Share
Formula: `Long-only share = Long-only allocation / Total allocation`

Variables:
Long-only allocation = shares sold to long-duration investors
Total allocation = all shares allocated

Why this formula exists: It measures how stable the shareholder base will likely be.

How to interpret the output:
Value above 0.60 -> stable base
Value 0.40-0.60 -> mixed base
Value below 0.40 -> volatile base

Worked example with numbers: If 70M of 100M shares go to long-only funds, long-only share is 70%. That is usually preferable for a company trying to minimize post-deal selling pressure.

### Formula 3: Story-to-Price Fit
Formula: `Story-to-price fit = Demand quality × pricing confidence`

Variables:
Demand quality = percentage of orders from aligned investors
pricing confidence = strength of demand at the target range

Why this formula exists: It is a practical check on whether the narrative and the valuation are aligned.

How to interpret the output:
Value high -> the market understands and supports the price
Value medium -> keep refining the message
Value low -> change investor mix or price range

Worked example with numbers: If a deal has 80% aligned orders and strong demand at the top of the range, the fit is strong. If most orders come from hedge funds only at a discount, the fit is weak.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Don't pitch every investor the same story. | Do tailor the message to the investor type and holding period. |
| Don't chase hot demand from short-term traders if you need stable ownership. | Do prioritize long-only and anchor capital when post-deal support matters. |
| Don't rely only on the first-day price pop. | Do measure the quality and durability of the shareholder base. |
| Don't assume the roadshow ends at pricing. | Do maintain IR and analyst communication after the deal closes. |
| Don't let the book fill with mismatched capital. | Do segment and prioritize investors before allocating shares. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: IPO for a Consumer SaaS Company
**Situation:** A subscription software company is preparing for an IPO and wants a stable base of long-term holders.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Equity Story Pyramid + Investor Segmentation Grid.

**Analysis:** The company should highlight recurring revenue, retention, and expansion metrics to long-only growth funds, not just headline growth to fast-money accounts. If the book is 3x covered with 65% long-only interest, the shareholder base is likely supportable.

**Decision rule:** If long-only share is above 60% and valuation demand holds at the top of range, keep the price range firm. If the book is skewed to hedge funds, widen targeting or soften the range.

**Action:** Rework the roadshow narrative and rebalance the book before pricing.

### Scenario 2: Follow-On Offering After Share Volatility
**Situation:** A listed fintech company wants to raise more equity, but the stock has been volatile since listing.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Post-Deal Support Loop + Long-Only Share.

**Analysis:** The problem may not be the business itself, but the shareholder mix. If short-term holders dominate, any new issue is likely to face selling pressure after close.

**Decision rule:** If the new issue can anchor at least 20% of the book and shift ownership toward long-only accounts, proceed. Otherwise, delay and reset the narrative.

**Action:** Build a targeted list of sector specialists and long-only funds, then use investor relations to reinforce the story after the issue.

### Scenario 3: Direct Listing for a Well-Known Platform
**Situation:** A mature software platform is considering a direct listing rather than a traditional IPO.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Investor Segmentation Grid + Story-to-Price Fit.

**Analysis:** A direct listing depends on investors already understanding the business. If the company has a strong existing audience and high story clarity, it can reduce the need for heavy roadshow work.

**Decision rule:** If the story is already familiar and demand quality is high, a direct listing can work. If the company needs education and stabilization, a traditional IPO with anchors is safer.

**Action:** Compare investor familiarity, pricing confidence, and desired shareholder mix before choosing the route.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Define the ideal shareholder base before you start the roadshow.
2. Convert the company’s numbers into a concise, credible equity story.
3. Segment investors by horizon, size, and risk appetite, then tailor the pitch.
4. Use anchors or cornerstones to stabilize demand when the market needs confidence.
5. Track order quality, not just order quantity, during bookbuilding.
6. Plan post-deal investor relations before pricing, not after.
7. Reassess the investor mix after close and use IR to sustain conviction.
