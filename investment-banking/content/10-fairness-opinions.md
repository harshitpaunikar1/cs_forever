# Fairness Opinions

## Overview

A fairness opinion is a formal letter from an investment bank stating whether the price in a proposed transaction is fair to shareholders from a financial point of view. The bank conducts independent valuation analysis using multiple methods and presents its conclusion to the board of directors. It is not a recommendation to approve the deal but rather a financial assessment that helps directors fulfill their fiduciary duty.

---

## Why It Matters

Boards of directors face legal liability if they approve a deal at an unfair price. A fairness opinion from a reputable bank provides legal protection by demonstrating that the board sought independent financial advice. In lawsuits challenging M&A transactions, courts look at whether a fairness opinion was obtained and how thorough the analysis was. Without one, directors are far more vulnerable to shareholder litigation.

## Key Principles

- The fairness opinion addresses financial fairness only, not whether the deal is strategically wise or operationally sound
- The bank performs multiple valuation analyses including DCF, comps, precedent transactions, and potentially LBO analysis
- The opinion letter discloses key assumptions, limitations, and the scope of the review
- Conflicts of interest must be disclosed, especially if the bank also serves as deal advisor and earns a success fee

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Fairness Opinion** | A formal assessment by an investment bank on whether a transaction price is fair from a financial standpoint |
| **Fiduciary Duty** | The legal obligation of board members to act in the best interests of shareholders |
| **Solvency Opinion** | A related analysis that confirms a company will remain solvent after completing a leveraged transaction |
| **Stapled Opinion** | A fairness opinion provided by the same bank that is also acting as the deal advisor, raising potential conflict concerns |

## Use Case

A public company's board receives an unsolicited $80 per share takeover bid. Before voting, the board engages an independent bank to render a fairness opinion. The bank's analysis shows the fair value range is $75-$95 per share. The opinion states the $80 offer is fair from a financial point of view, though at the lower end of the range, giving the board the information needed to negotiate or accept.

## Scenario

> A REIT received a $6.5 billion take-private offer from a private equity consortium. The board engaged two independent banks to deliver fairness opinions. Both banks conducted DCF, NAV, and precedent analyses. One bank found the price fair at a 15% premium to NAV. The second bank flagged that recent comparable REIT privatizations paid 20-25% premiums, suggesting the price was below market. The board used the dual opinions to negotiate the price up to $7.1 billion.

## Examples

- Lazard delivering a fairness opinion to Twitter's board on Elon Musk's $54.20 per share offer, concluding the price was fair despite the stock trading well below that level at the time
- A board rejecting a hostile bid after its fairness opinion showed the offer fell 20% below the low end of the DCF range, providing the legal basis to refuse without shareholder vote

## 1. Topic Snapshot
A fairness opinion is a financial assessment of whether a proposed transaction price is fair to shareholders from a financial point of view.
For IT, AI, product, and consulting leaders, the practical value is board protection and decision discipline. The opinion does not say a deal is strategically good; it says the price is defensible given the valuation evidence and the disclosed assumptions.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Fairness Scope Matrix
**Purpose:** Separate what a fairness opinion does from what it does not do.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    Opinion scope
            In scope                  Out of scope
Financial   deal price fairness      strategic fit, synergies
Legal       disclosure support       legal advice, tax advice
Governance  board record support     management judgment replacement
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: financial fairness, meaning whether price is reasonable from a valuation perspective.
Component 2: strategic fit, meaning whether the deal is smart for the business.
Component 3: legal scope, meaning what the bank is and is not opining on.
Component 4: governance value, meaning how the opinion supports board process.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A software company may have a fairness opinion saying an acquisition price is fair, but the board still needs to decide whether integrating the target is strategically sensible. The opinion supports price judgment, not product roadmap judgment.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone asks the fairness opinion to answer a non-financial question.

### Opinion Process Ladder
**Purpose:** Show the workflow from mandate to opinion letter.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
mandate -> diligence -> valuation -> sensitivities -> board review -> opinion letter
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: mandate, meaning the bank is formally engaged.
Component 2: diligence, meaning review of financials and transaction terms.
Component 3: valuation, meaning DCF, comps, precedents, and other methods.
Component 4: sensitivities, meaning checking how the conclusion changes under assumptions.
Component 5: board review, meaning directors receive and interrogate the analysis.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A take-private of a SaaS company should not be summarized with one valuation number. The process ladder forces the bank to test multiple methods and disclose the assumptions behind them.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the board wants the opinion quickly without understanding the method stack.

### Conflict Screen Matrix
**Purpose:** Evaluate whether the opinion is credibly independent.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                 Conflict risk
              Low                     High
Role overlap   advisory only          advisory + success fee
Fee dependence standard fee           large contingent incentive
Disclosure     clear and full         weak or delayed disclosure
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: role overlap, meaning whether the bank is also advising the transaction.
Component 2: fee dependence, meaning how much the bank’s compensation depends on closing.
Component 3: disclosure, meaning how open the opinion is about relationships and limits.
Component 4: independence, meaning the board can rely on the work without obvious bias.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: If a bank is both running the sale process and delivering the fairness opinion, the board should scrutinize the fee structure and independence language carefully. In a consulting-led tech transaction, process credibility matters as much as the math.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the board asks whether the opinion is truly independent.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below are practical decision tools [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Offer Premium [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Offer Premium = (Offer Price - Unaffected Price) / Unaffected Price`

Why this formula exists: It measures how much more the buyer is paying than the market did before the deal.
How to interpret the output:
Low premium -> board may question fairness if control changes hands
Moderate premium -> often acceptable in negotiated deals
High premium -> may reflect competition, control value, or strategic scarcity
Worked example with numbers: If unaffected price is $80 and the offer is $96, premium = 20%. Decision: compare that premium to precedent deals and valuation ranges.

### Formula 2: Valuation Range Spread [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Range Spread = High Value - Low Value`

Why this formula exists: It shows how much uncertainty sits inside the valuation work.
How to interpret the output:
Narrow spread -> more certainty or fewer assumptions
Wide spread -> more sensitivity and more board judgment
Worked example with numbers: If DCF suggests $74 to $92 per share, the range spread is $18. Decision: the board should focus on where the offer sits within that range and why.

### Formula 3: Solvency Cushion [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Solvency Cushion = Cash + Liquid Assets - Near-Term Obligations`

Why this formula exists: It supports the related solvency check in leveraged or recapitalization transactions.
How to interpret the output:
Positive cushion -> less distress risk
Thin or negative cushion -> higher post-close risk
Worked example with numbers: If a company has $500M in liquid resources and $420M in near-term obligations, cushion is $80M. Decision: the post-deal balance sheet needs more review.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat fairness as strategic approval | Treat it as financial fairness only |
| Ignore conflicts because the letter is formal | Disclose role overlap and fee incentives clearly |
| Use one valuation method only | Check DCF, comps, and precedents together |
| Hide uncertainty in the assumptions | Show sensitivities and range width to the board |
| Assume the opinion eliminates litigation risk | Use the opinion to strengthen process, not replace judgment |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Hostile Bid for a Public Software Company
Situation: A bidder offers $72 per share for a software company trading at $58.
Applicable framework/metric: Fairness Scope Matrix, Offer Premium.
Analysis: If unaffected price is $58, the offer premium is about 24%. That may be fair if DCF and comps support it, but the board must still consider process quality and any strategic alternatives.
Decision rule: If the offer sits inside or above the midpoint of the valuation range, the opinion can support fairness; if it falls below the low end, the board should push back.
Action: Build a range from multiple methods and document why the board accepts or rejects the bid.

### Scenario 2: Take-Private of a Consulting-Heavy Services Firm
Situation: A private equity buyer wants to take a public consulting company private with a leverage-heavy structure.
Applicable framework/metric: Opinion Process Ladder, Solvency Cushion.
Analysis: The fairness opinion covers the price to shareholders. The solvency analysis checks whether the deal leaves the business with enough liquidity and balance-sheet room.
Decision rule: If leverage materially weakens the post-close cushion, the transaction needs restructuring or a smaller debt load.
Action: Run valuation, solvency, and debt capacity checks before the board vote.

### Scenario 3: Bank Acting as Advisor and Opinion Provider
Situation: The same bank is advising the seller, earning a success fee, and issuing the fairness opinion.
Applicable framework/metric: Conflict Screen Matrix.
Analysis: This stapled structure is common but sensitive. The board should inspect how independent the analysis is, how the fee depends on closing, and whether a second opinion is warranted.
Decision rule: If conflict risk is high, add a second independent banker or tighten disclosure.
Action: Document the conflict, compare opinions if needed, and preserve a clear board record.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Define whether the opinion is needed for a merger, take-private, recap, or related transaction.
2. Collect the relevant financials, transaction terms, and board questions.
3. Run DCF, comps, and precedent analyses with consistent assumptions.
4. Check whether the bank has conflicts or fee incentives that require disclosure.
5. Prepare a board memo that explains the range, not just the headline conclusion.
6. Ask whether solvency or post-close leverage needs separate analysis.
7. Deliver the opinion only after the board has enough information to use it responsibly.
