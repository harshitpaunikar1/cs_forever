# Capital Markets Overview

## Overview

Capital markets are where buyers and sellers trade financial securities like stocks, bonds, and derivatives. They split into two main parts: the primary market, where new securities are issued for the first time, and the secondary market, where existing securities change hands between investors. These markets channel savings into productive investments across the economy.

---

## Why It Matters

Capital markets determine the cost of funding for every business and government. When capital markets function well, companies get cheaper financing, investors earn fair returns, and the economy grows. When they break down, as in 2008, credit freezes and businesses collapse. Understanding how these markets work is essential for anyone raising capital, investing money, or managing financial risk.

## Key Principles

- Primary markets create new securities while secondary markets provide liquidity for existing ones
- Equity markets offer ownership stakes with unlimited upside while debt markets offer fixed returns with priority in bankruptcy
- Market efficiency means prices reflect all available information, making it hard to consistently beat the market
- Regulation protects investors and maintains confidence through disclosure requirements and trading rules

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Primary Market** | The market where new securities are issued and sold to investors for the first time |
| **Secondary Market** | The market where previously issued securities are bought and sold between investors |
| **Equity Market** | The portion of capital markets where company ownership shares are traded |
| **Fixed Income Market** | The portion of capital markets where debt instruments like bonds are traded |

## Use Case

A growing retail chain needs $100 million to open 50 new stores. It evaluates whether to issue new shares in the equity market, diluting existing owners but avoiding mandatory payments, or to issue bonds in the debt market, preserving ownership but committing to interest payments. The capital markets give it both options.

## Scenario

> A European automaker wanted to fund its electric vehicle transition without diluting shareholders. It tapped the debt capital markets with a $2 billion green bond offering, attracting ESG-focused institutional investors. The bonds priced at a yield 15 basis points below comparable non-green issues, saving the company $3 million annually in interest costs while signaling its environmental commitment.

## Examples

- Apple issuing $5.5 billion in bonds despite having $200 billion in cash because debt financing was cheaper than repatriating overseas cash
- The New York Stock Exchange processing billions of dollars in daily secondary market trades, giving investors the confidence to buy stocks knowing they can sell them easily

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Capital markets are the funding system that lets companies and governments turn savings into capital through equity and debt.
For IT, AI, product, and consulting leaders, the practical question is not “what is a capital market?” It is “which market gives us the cheapest, safest, and most strategic way to raise money or provide liquidity?”

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Capital Market Choice Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether equity or debt is the better funding instrument.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    Need fixed repayment?
                Yes                         No
Need control   Debt preferred            Equity preferred
Need flexibility  Debt with caution     Equity or hybrid
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: control, meaning how much ownership the issuer wants to keep.
Component 2: repayment burden, meaning whether cash flows can support interest and principal.
Component 3: flexibility, meaning whether the firm needs freedom to invest without covenants.
Component 4: risk tolerance, meaning how much leverage the balance sheet can absorb.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company with steady recurring revenue may use debt for modest growth capital because repayment is predictable. A pre-product AI startup should usually avoid heavy debt and use equity or a strategic partner instead.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When finance and strategy teams disagree on whether dilution or repayment is the bigger problem.

### Primary vs Secondary Market Flow
**Purpose:** Clarify where money goes and who benefits.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Primary issue -> issuer receives cash -> investors receive new securities
Secondary trade -> seller receives cash -> issuer does not receive new cash
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: issuance, meaning new capital entering the company.
Component 2: liquidity, meaning existing holders can sell.
Component 3: price discovery, meaning public trading updates valuation continuously.
Component 4: signaling, meaning the market reads investor demand as a quality signal.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A growth-stage product company may raise a primary round to fund expansion, while early employees sell a small portion of shares in a secondary transaction to reduce concentration risk and improve retention.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone thinks every share sale helps the company’s treasury.

### Capital Markets Lifecycle
**Purpose:** Show how funding needs map to market channels.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Private capital -> private placements -> IPO / bond issuance -> public trading -> refinancing / secondary liquidity
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: private funding, meaning angels, VCs, private credit, or strategic investors.
Component 2: public issuance, meaning IPOs or bond deals.
Component 3: liquidity, meaning the ability to exit or rebalance positions.
Component 4: transparency, meaning disclosure and governance requirements rise as markets open.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting-backed tech company may start with private equity, then use a public listing or bond market once it has scale, audited reporting, and a repeatable operating model.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When planning the company’s transition from private growth to public capital.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below are practical finance tools [verified from model knowledge, not source].

### Formula 1: Net Issue Proceeds [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `Net Issue Proceeds = Gross Proceeds - underwriting fees - legal fees - listing costs`

Why this formula exists: It tells issuers how much cash they actually get from a financing.
How to interpret the output:
Lower fees -> better capital efficiency
Higher fees -> more expensive financing
Worked example with numbers: If a company raises $100M and spends $4M on fees, net proceeds are $96M. Decision: compare that cash to the actual investment plan before choosing issuance size.

### Formula 2: Debt Service Coverage Ratio [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `DSCR = Operating Cash Flow / Debt Service`

Why this formula exists: It shows whether the borrower can pay debt comfortably.
How to interpret the output:
Below 1.0 -> insufficient cash generation
1.0 to 1.5 -> tight coverage
Above 1.5 -> generally safer for lenders
Worked example with numbers: If operating cash flow is $18M and annual debt service is $12M, DSCR = 1.5. Decision: debt may be acceptable if the business is stable.

### Formula 3: After-Tax Cost of Debt [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Formula: `After-Tax Cost of Debt = Interest Rate × (1 - Tax Rate)`

Why this formula exists: It compares debt to equity on a true cost basis.
How to interpret the output:
Lower after-tax cost -> debt is cheaper than it first appears
Higher after-tax cost -> leverage is less attractive
Worked example with numbers: If the coupon is 8% and the tax rate is 25%, after-tax cost of debt is 6%. Decision: debt may beat equity if default risk stays manageable.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat all funding sources as interchangeable | Match the source to cash-flow stability and control needs |
| Assume secondary trading funds the company | Distinguish treasury money from shareholder liquidity |
| Ignore issuance fees | Use net proceeds, not gross proceeds, in planning |
| Compare debt and equity without risk context | Compare repayment burden, dilution, and flexibility together |
| Chase the cheapest rate without governance review | Check covenants, disclosure, and refinancing risk |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: Growing Company Choosing Between Equity and Debt
Situation: An IT services firm with stable recurring revenue needs $30M to open new offices and expand delivery capacity.
Applicable framework/metric: Capital Market Choice Matrix, DSCR, cost of capital.
Analysis: If cash flows are predictable and DSCR stays healthy after borrowing, debt can preserve ownership. If revenues are volatile, equity is safer.
Decision rule: If DSCR remains above 1.5 post-deal, debt is viable; if not, use equity or a hybrid.
Action: Build a financing model that includes downside cases before approaching banks or investors.

### Scenario 2: Startup Using the Public Market as a Liquidity Tool
Situation: A product company is approaching IPO readiness and wants both capital and credibility.
Applicable framework/metric: Capital Markets Lifecycle, secondary market, liquidity.
Analysis: The IPO is not just about cash. It creates a trading market, establishes a public valuation, and gives early holders liquidity.
Decision rule: If the company has audit-ready reporting, strong governance, and a repeatable growth story, public issuance becomes realistic.
Action: Prepare a disclosure package, test investor demand, and decide how much secondary liquidity to allow.

### Scenario 3: Green Bond Versus Standard Bond
Situation: A consulting-led infrastructure business wants to fund energy-efficient data centers.
Applicable framework/metric: Primary vs Secondary Market Flow, spread, green bond pricing.
Analysis: A labeled green bond can widen the investor base and sometimes improve pricing if the use of proceeds is credible.
Decision rule: If the project has a defensible sustainability story and reporting discipline, green issuance can create strategic value.
Action: Define eligible projects, set reporting rules, and compare all-in cost against a standard bond.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Decide whether the capital need is for growth, liquidity, or refinancing.
2. Separate equity, debt, and hybrid options by control and cash-flow impact.
3. Estimate net proceeds after fees and underwriting costs.
4. Stress-test debt service under conservative revenue assumptions.
5. Check the market’s appetite for the issuer story and timing.
6. Compare primary issuance with secondary liquidity if stakeholders need exits.
7. Document the disclosure and governance requirements before launch.
8. Choose the instrument that best fits the company’s stage and risk profile.
