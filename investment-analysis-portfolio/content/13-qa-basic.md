# Basic Questions — Q1 to Q70

## Q1. What is investment analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Investment analysis is the process of evaluating a financial asset to determine its suitability for a particular investor or portfolio. It involves examining an asset's potential return, associated risks, and alignment with the investor's financial goals. The analysis may include reviewing financial statements, market conditions, and economic indicators.

**Real-life applications:**
- A financial advisor evaluating mutual funds for a client's retirement account
- An individual comparing savings accounts and certificates of deposit for short-term goals
- A corporate treasurer deciding where to park excess company cash
- A student analyzing stock picks for an investment club competition

**Key concepts:** `investment analysis`, `financial assets`, `risk assessment`, `return evaluation`, `investor suitability`

---

## Q2. What are the main asset classes available to investors?

**Level:** Basic

The main asset classes are equities (stocks), fixed income (bonds), cash and cash equivalents, and alternative investments such as real estate, commodities, and hedge funds. Each class has distinct risk and return characteristics. Investors typically combine multiple asset classes to build diversified portfolios that match their goals.

**Real-life applications:**
- A pension fund allocating capital across stocks, bonds, and real estate
- An individual choosing between a stock fund and a bond fund in a 401(k) plan
- A wealth manager recommending commodity exposure during periods of inflation
- A young investor putting most savings into equities for long-term growth

**Key concepts:** `asset class`, `equities`, `fixed income`, `alternative investments`, `diversification`

---

## Q3. What is the difference between fundamental and technical analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Fundamental analysis evaluates a security by examining financial statements, industry conditions, and economic factors to determine its intrinsic value. Technical analysis focuses on historical price and volume patterns to predict future price movements. Many investors use a combination of both approaches to make more informed decisions.

**Real-life applications:**
- An equity analyst reading a company's annual report to estimate fair value
- A day trader studying candlestick charts to time market entries
- A portfolio manager using earnings estimates alongside momentum indicators
- A hedge fund combining quantitative models with balance sheet analysis

**Key concepts:** `fundamental analysis`, `technical analysis`, `intrinsic value`, `price patterns`, `volume analysis`

---

## Q4. What is a stock?

**Level:** Basic

A stock represents a share of ownership in a company. When you buy a stock, you become a partial owner entitled to a portion of the company's profits and assets. Stock prices fluctuate based on company performance, market conditions, and investor sentiment.

**Real-life applications:**
- Purchasing shares of a technology company through a brokerage account
- Receiving dividend payments from a utility stock held in a retirement portfolio
- Voting on corporate matters at an annual shareholder meeting
- Monitoring daily price changes of a stock on a financial news website

**Key concepts:** `stock`, `equity ownership`, `shareholder`, `dividends`, `market price`

---

## Q5. What is a bond?

**Level:** Basic

A bond is a debt instrument in which an investor lends money to an entity, typically a corporation or government, for a defined period at a fixed or variable interest rate. The issuer pays periodic interest called coupons and returns the principal at maturity. Bonds are generally considered less risky than stocks but offer lower potential returns.

**Real-life applications:**
- A retiree buying government bonds for predictable income
- A city issuing municipal bonds to finance infrastructure projects
- A corporation selling bonds to raise capital for expansion
- An investor buying treasury bonds as a safe haven during market turmoil

**Key concepts:** `bond`, `coupon`, `maturity`, `principal`, `fixed income`

---

## Q6. What is the risk-free rate?

**Level:** Basic

The risk-free rate is the theoretical return on an investment that carries zero risk of default. In practice, the yield on short-term government treasury bills is used as a proxy because government default is considered extremely unlikely. The risk-free rate serves as a baseline for evaluating all other investments.

**Real-life applications:**
- Using the 3-month Treasury bill rate as the risk-free rate in CAPM calculations
- Comparing a corporate bond's yield to the risk-free rate to determine the credit spread
- Setting a minimum required return for a capital budgeting project
- Benchmarking a money market fund's return against the risk-free rate

**Key concepts:** `risk-free rate`, `treasury bills`, `default risk`, `benchmark`, `minimum return`

---

## Q7. What is liquidity in the context of investments?

**Level:** Basic

Liquidity refers to how quickly and easily an asset can be converted to cash without significantly affecting its price. Highly liquid assets like publicly traded stocks can be sold almost instantly, while illiquid assets like real estate or private equity may take weeks or months to sell. Investors typically demand higher returns for holding illiquid investments.

**Real-life applications:**
- Selling shares of a large-cap stock on a stock exchange within seconds
- Waiting months to find a buyer for a commercial property
- A hedge fund imposing a lock-up period that restricts investor withdrawals
- Keeping an emergency fund in a money market account for immediate access

**Key concepts:** `liquidity`, `liquid assets`, `illiquid assets`, `bid-ask spread`, `liquidity premium`

---

## Q8. What is the time value of money?

**Level:** Basic

The time value of money is the principle that a dollar received today is worth more than a dollar received in the future because today's dollar can be invested to earn a return. This concept is fundamental to finance and underlies the valuation of virtually every financial instrument. It explains why borrowers pay interest and why investors expect returns.

**Real-life applications:**
- Choosing between receiving a lump sum now or installments over time
- Calculating how much to save monthly to reach a future financial goal
- Pricing a bond by discounting its future coupon payments to the present
- Determining the net present value of a business investment

**Key concepts:** `time value of money`, `present value`, `future value`, `compounding`, `discounting`

---

## Q9. What is compounding?

**Level:** Basic

Compounding is the process by which an investment generates earnings that are reinvested to generate their own earnings over time. In other words, you earn returns not just on your original investment but also on the accumulated returns from prior periods. Albert Einstein reportedly called compounding the eighth wonder of the world because of its powerful effect on wealth accumulation.

**Real-life applications:**
- A savings account where interest is added to the balance and earns additional interest
- Reinvesting stock dividends to purchase additional shares that generate their own dividends
- A retirement account growing exponentially over a 30-year career
- Comparing annual versus monthly compounding rates on a certificate of deposit

**Key concepts:** `compounding`, `compound interest`, `reinvestment`, `exponential growth`, `compounding frequency`

---

## Q10. What is present value?

**Level:** Basic

Present value is the current worth of a future sum of money or stream of cash flows, discounted at an appropriate interest rate. It answers the question of how much a future payment is worth in today's terms. The higher the discount rate or the longer the time until payment, the lower the present value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Calculating how much a future inheritance is worth today for financial planning
- Determining the fair price of a bond based on its future coupon and principal payments
- Evaluating whether a company's projected cash flows justify its current stock price
- Comparing two business projects with different cash flow timelines

**Key concepts:** `present value`, `discount rate`, `future cash flows`, `discounting`, `net present value`

---

## Q11. What is future value?

**Level:** Basic

Future value is the amount that a current sum of money will grow to over a specified period at a given rate of return. It shows how much an investment made today will be worth at a point in the future, assuming a particular growth rate and compounding frequency. Future value calculations help investors set savings targets and project investment outcomes.

**Real-life applications:**
- Estimating how much a 10,000 dollar investment will be worth in 20 years at 7 percent annual return
- Projecting the balance of a retirement account at the expected retirement date
- Calculating the total cost of a loan including accumulated interest
- Determining how inflation will erode the purchasing power of savings over time

**Key concepts:** `future value`, `compounding`, `growth rate`, `investment horizon`, `time period`

---

## Q12. What is an annuity?

**Level:** Basic

An annuity is a series of equal payments made at regular intervals over a specified period. Annuities can be ordinary, where payments occur at the end of each period, or annuities due, where payments occur at the beginning. They are commonly used in retirement planning, loan repayment, and insurance products.

**Real-life applications:**
- Monthly mortgage payments that remain the same over a 30-year loan
- A retiree receiving fixed monthly payments from a purchased annuity contract
- Equal quarterly dividend payments from a preferred stock
- A car loan with fixed monthly installments

**Key concepts:** `annuity`, `ordinary annuity`, `annuity due`, `periodic payments`, `present value of annuity`

---

## Q13. What is the difference between simple interest and compound interest?

**Level:** Basic

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal amount, while compound interest is calculated on the principal plus any previously accumulated interest. Over time, compound interest produces significantly higher returns because earnings themselves generate additional earnings. Most real-world investments and loans use compound interest.

**Real-life applications:**
- A basic savings bond paying simple interest versus a savings account paying compound interest
- Comparing loan costs when one lender charges simple interest and another charges compound interest
- Calculating the total amount owed on a student loan with compound interest
- Understanding why credit card debt grows so quickly due to daily compounding

**Key concepts:** `simple interest`, `compound interest`, `principal`, `interest calculation`, `debt growth`

---

## Q14. What is risk in the context of investing?

**Level:** Basic

Investment risk is the possibility that actual returns will differ from expected returns, including the chance of losing some or all of the original investment. Risk encompasses both the probability of loss and the uncertainty of outcomes. Different types of investments carry different levels and types of risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- A stock investor facing the risk of a 20 percent market decline
- A bondholder concerned about the issuer defaulting on interest payments
- An international investor exposed to currency exchange rate fluctuations
- A real estate investor worried about property values declining in a recession

**Key concepts:** `investment risk`, `uncertainty`, `expected return`, `loss probability`, `risk types`

---

## Q15. What is the difference between systematic and unsystematic risk?

**Level:** Basic

Systematic risk affects the entire market and cannot be eliminated through diversification. It includes risks from economic recessions, interest rate changes, and geopolitical events. Unsystematic risk is specific to a particular company or industry and can be reduced or eliminated by holding a diversified portfolio of investments.

**Real-life applications:**
- A recession causing all stock prices to decline regardless of company quality
- A pharmaceutical company's stock dropping after a drug fails clinical trials
- An investor holding 50 different stocks to eliminate company-specific risk
- Oil prices rising and hurting airline stocks while benefiting energy stocks

**Key concepts:** `systematic risk`, `unsystematic risk`, `market risk`, `diversifiable risk`, `non-diversifiable risk`

---

## Q16. What is standard deviation as a measure of risk?

**Level:** Basic

Standard deviation measures how much an investment's returns vary from its average return over a given period. A higher standard deviation means greater variability and therefore higher risk, while a lower standard deviation indicates more consistent returns. It is the most widely used single measure of total investment risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing the volatility of a tech stock fund versus a bond fund
- A financial planner using standard deviation to match investments to a client's risk tolerance
- Rating agencies classifying mutual funds by volatility categories
- An investor choosing a low-standard-deviation fund for short-term savings goals

**Key concepts:** `standard deviation`, `volatility`, `return variability`, `risk measurement`, `dispersion`

---

## Q17. What is beta?

**Level:** Basic

Beta is a measure of how sensitive a stock's returns are to movements in the overall market. A beta of one means the stock moves in line with the market, a beta greater than one means it is more volatile than the market, and a beta less than one means it is less volatile. Beta specifically measures systematic risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- A conservative investor choosing stocks with betas below one for lower volatility
- A hedge fund manager selecting high-beta stocks to amplify returns in a bull market
- A portfolio manager calculating the overall beta of a diversified portfolio
- A financial website listing beta values alongside other stock statistics

**Key concepts:** `beta`, `market sensitivity`, `systematic risk`, `volatility`, `market benchmark`

---

## Q18. What is the Sharpe ratio?

**Level:** Basic

The Sharpe ratio measures risk-adjusted return by dividing the difference between a portfolio's return and the risk-free rate by the portfolio's standard deviation. A higher Sharpe ratio indicates better return per unit of risk taken. It allows investors to compare investments with different risk levels on an equal basis.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing two mutual funds to determine which delivers better returns for the risk involved
- A pension fund evaluating the efficiency of its external money managers
- An investor deciding between a hedge fund and a stock index fund
- Rating agencies ranking funds by their risk-adjusted performance

**Key concepts:** `Sharpe ratio`, `risk-adjusted return`, `excess return`, `standard deviation`, `performance measurement`

---

## Q19. What is diversification?

**Level:** Basic

Diversification is the strategy of spreading investments across multiple assets, sectors, or geographies to reduce overall portfolio risk. When one investment performs poorly, others may perform well, smoothing out overall returns. Diversification reduces unsystematic risk but cannot eliminate systematic risk that affects the entire market.

**Real-life applications:**
- An investor holding stocks from technology, healthcare, energy, and consumer sectors
- A mutual fund owning hundreds of stocks across different industries
- Adding international stocks to a portfolio of domestic equities
- Mixing stocks, bonds, and real estate in a retirement account

**Key concepts:** `diversification`, `portfolio risk`, `unsystematic risk`, `asset mix`, `correlation`

---

## Q20. What is asset allocation?

**Level:** Basic

Asset allocation is the process of dividing an investment portfolio among different asset classes such as stocks, bonds, and cash. Research shows that asset allocation is the primary determinant of long-term portfolio performance, more important than individual security selection. The right allocation depends on the investor's goals, time horizon, and risk tolerance.

**Real-life applications:**
- A young investor allocating 80 percent to stocks and 20 percent to bonds
- A retiree shifting to 40 percent stocks and 60 percent bonds for income and stability
- A target-date retirement fund automatically adjusting allocation over time
- An endowment fund setting allocation targets reviewed annually by the board

**Key concepts:** `asset allocation`, `portfolio construction`, `risk tolerance`, `time horizon`, `investment policy`

---

## Q21. What is a portfolio?

**Level:** Basic

A portfolio is a collection of financial investments such as stocks, bonds, cash, mutual funds, and other assets held by an individual or institution. The purpose of building a portfolio rather than holding a single investment is to achieve diversification and manage risk. The composition of a portfolio should reflect the investor's specific objectives and constraints.

**Real-life applications:**
- A retirement account containing a mix of index funds and bond funds
- A university endowment portfolio spanning equities, fixed income, and real estate
- An individual investor's brokerage account with ten different stock positions
- A family office managing a portfolio across multiple asset classes and geographies

**Key concepts:** `portfolio`, `investment holdings`, `diversification`, `asset mix`, `portfolio management`

---

## Q22. What is a mutual fund?

**Level:** Basic

A mutual fund is a professionally managed investment vehicle that pools money from many investors to purchase a diversified portfolio of stocks, bonds, or other securities. Investors buy shares of the fund and benefit from professional management and instant diversification. Mutual funds charge fees, typically expressed as an expense ratio, to cover management and operating costs.

**Real-life applications:**
- Investing in a broad market index fund through a retirement account
- A first-time investor buying a balanced mutual fund for simplicity
- Comparing expense ratios across similar funds to minimize costs
- A financial advisor recommending sector-specific mutual funds for targeted exposure

**Key concepts:** `mutual fund`, `pooled investment`, `expense ratio`, `net asset value`, `professional management`

---

## Q23. What is an exchange-traded fund?

**Level:** Basic

An exchange-traded fund, or ETF, is a type of investment fund that trades on a stock exchange like a regular stock. ETFs typically track an index, sector, commodity, or other asset class and offer the diversification of a mutual fund with the trading flexibility of a stock. They generally have lower expense ratios than actively managed mutual funds.

**Real-life applications:**
- Buying an S&P 500 ETF to gain broad market exposure at low cost
- Trading a gold ETF instead of purchasing physical gold
- Using sector ETFs to tilt a portfolio toward technology or healthcare
- An advisor building a client portfolio entirely from low-cost ETFs

**Key concepts:** `ETF`, `index tracking`, `exchange-traded`, `low cost`, `intraday trading`

---

## Q24. What is a dividend?

**Level:** Basic

A dividend is a payment made by a company to its shareholders, usually from profits. Dividends are typically paid quarterly in cash but can also be issued as additional shares. Companies that consistently pay dividends are often mature, profitable businesses, and dividends provide a steady income stream for investors.

**Real-life applications:**
- A retiree relying on dividend income from blue-chip stocks to cover living expenses
- Reinvesting dividends through a dividend reinvestment plan to compound wealth
- Evaluating a stock's dividend yield to compare income-generating potential
- A company announcing a dividend increase as a signal of financial health

**Key concepts:** `dividend`, `dividend yield`, `payout ratio`, `income investing`, `dividend reinvestment`

---

## Q25. What is a market index?

**Level:** Basic

A market index is a statistical measure that tracks the performance of a group of securities representing a specific market or segment. Indexes like the S&P 500, Dow Jones Industrial Average, and NASDAQ Composite serve as benchmarks for measuring investment performance. They provide a snapshot of overall market health and direction.

**Real-life applications:**

- A news anchor reporting that the Dow Jones rose 200 points today

## Q26. What is market capitalization?

**Level:** Basic

Market capitalization is the total market value of a company's outstanding shares of stock, calculated by multiplying the current share price by the total number of shares. It is used to classify companies as large-cap, mid-cap, or small-cap. Market capitalization gives investors a quick sense of a company's size and is a key factor in index construction.

**Real-life applications:**
- Classifying a company worth 500 billion dollars as a large-cap stock
- An index fund weighting its holdings by market capitalization
- A small-cap fund screening for companies with market caps below 2 billion dollars
- Comparing the market cap of two companies in the same industry to gauge relative size

**Key concepts:** `market capitalization`, `large-cap`, `mid-cap`, `small-cap`, `company size`

---

## Q27. What is the price-to-earnings ratio?

**Level:** Basic

The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E ratio, is calculated by dividing a stock's current market price by its earnings per share. It indicates how much investors are willing to pay for each dollar of a company's earnings. A high P/E may suggest the stock is overvalued or that investors expect high future growth, while a low P/E may indicate undervaluation or low growth expectations.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing the P/E ratios of two retail companies to assess relative value
- A value investor screening for stocks with P/E ratios below the industry average
- A financial news report highlighting that the overall market P/E is above historical norms
- An analyst using forward P/E based on projected earnings to value a growth stock

**Key concepts:** `price-to-earnings ratio`, `valuation metric`, `earnings per share`, `overvalued`, `undervalued`

---

## Q28. What is earnings per share?

**Level:** Basic

Earnings per share, or EPS, is a company's net profit divided by the number of its outstanding shares. It represents the portion of a company's profit allocated to each share of stock and is one of the most widely watched financial metrics. Rising EPS generally signals improving profitability and is a primary driver of stock prices.

**Real-life applications:**
- Analysts forecasting next quarter's EPS and comparing it to consensus estimates
- A stock price jumping after a company reports EPS that beats expectations
- Calculating basic versus diluted EPS to account for stock options and convertible securities
- Tracking a company's EPS growth over five years to assess its profit trajectory

**Key concepts:** `earnings per share`, `net income`, `outstanding shares`, `profitability`, `diluted EPS`

---

## Q29. What is return on equity?

**Level:** Basic

Return on equity, or ROE, measures how effectively a company uses shareholder capital to generate profits. It is calculated by dividing net income by shareholders' equity. A higher ROE indicates that the company is more efficient at turning invested capital into earnings, making it an important metric for comparing companies within the same industry.

**Real-life applications:**
- An investor comparing the ROE of two banks to determine which is more efficient
- A company's management setting ROE targets to satisfy shareholder expectations
- A stock screener filtering for companies with ROE above 15 percent
- Analyzing whether a company's high ROE is driven by genuine profitability or excessive debt

**Key concepts:** `return on equity`, `shareholder equity`, `profitability`, `capital efficiency`, `financial ratio`

---

## Q30. What is free cash flow?

**Level:** Basic

Free cash flow is the cash a company generates from its operations after subtracting capital expenditures needed to maintain or expand its asset base. It represents the cash available to pay dividends, reduce debt, buy back shares, or invest in growth. Free cash flow is considered a more reliable measure of financial health than accounting earnings because it is harder to manipulate.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company using strong free cash flow to fund a share buyback program
- An analyst valuing a business based on projected free cash flows rather than reported earnings
- A bondholder checking whether the issuer generates enough free cash flow to service its debt
- An investor avoiding companies with growing earnings but negative free cash flow

**Key concepts:** `free cash flow`, `operating cash flow`, `capital expenditure`, `cash generation`, `financial health`

---

## Q31. What is intrinsic value?

**Level:** Basic

Intrinsic value is the calculated true worth of a security based on its underlying fundamentals, independent of its current market price. It is typically estimated by discounting the expected future cash flows of the asset back to the present. If the intrinsic value is higher than the market price, the security is considered undervalued.

**Real-life applications:**
- A value investor buying stocks trading below their estimated intrinsic value
- An analyst publishing a target price for a stock based on a discounted cash flow model
- Warren Buffett famously seeking companies whose market price is well below intrinsic value
- A merger and acquisition team estimating the intrinsic value of a takeover target

**Key concepts:** `intrinsic value`, `fair value`, `discounted cash flow`, `undervalued`, `fundamental analysis`

---

## Q32. What is the discounted cash flow method?

**Level:** Basic

The discounted cash flow method, or DCF, estimates the value of an investment by projecting its future cash flows and discounting them back to the present using an appropriate discount rate. The sum of all discounted future cash flows represents the investment's estimated value today. DCF is one of the most widely used and theoretically sound valuation approaches.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing a startup by projecting its expected cash flows over ten years and discounting them
- A real estate investor estimating the value of a rental property based on expected rental income
- An investment bank using DCF to advise on the fair price in a corporate acquisition
- A portfolio manager building a DCF model to determine whether a stock is worth buying

**Key concepts:** `discounted cash flow`, `future cash flows`, `discount rate`, `valuation`, `present value`

---

## Q33. What is the dividend discount model?

**Level:** Basic

The dividend discount model values a stock by calculating the present value of all its expected future dividend payments. The simplest version, the Gordon Growth Model, assumes dividends grow at a constant rate forever. This model works best for mature companies with stable and predictable dividend histories.

**Real-life applications:**
- Valuing a utility company stock that has paid steadily growing dividends for decades
- An analyst using the Gordon Growth Model to set a target price for a blue-chip stock
- Comparing the DDM valuation to the current market price to identify buying opportunities
- A finance student learning valuation principles through dividend discount exercises

**Key concepts:** `dividend discount model`, `Gordon Growth Model`, `dividend growth`, `stock valuation`, `present value`

---

## Q34. What is yield to maturity?

**Level:** Basic

Yield to maturity is the total annual return an investor will earn if a bond is held until it matures, accounting for all coupon payments and any difference between the purchase price and face value. It is the most comprehensive measure of a bond's expected return. YTM assumes that all coupon payments are reinvested at the same rate.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing two corporate bonds with different coupon rates by looking at their YTM
- A bond trader deciding whether to buy a bond trading at a discount based on its YTM
- A fixed-income fund reporting its portfolio's weighted average yield to maturity
- An investor using YTM to determine if a bond offers adequate compensation for its risk

**Key concepts:** `yield to maturity`, `bond return`, `coupon payments`, `face value`, `reinvestment assumption`

---

## Q35. What is a coupon rate?

**Level:** Basic

The coupon rate is the annual interest rate that a bond issuer pays to bondholders, expressed as a percentage of the bond's face value. A bond with a face value of 1,000 dollars and a 5 percent coupon rate pays 50 dollars in interest per year. The coupon rate is fixed at issuance and does not change over the life of the bond.

**Real-life applications:**
- An investor choosing between a 3 percent coupon government bond and a 5 percent coupon corporate bond
- Calculating annual interest income from a portfolio of bonds with different coupon rates
- Understanding why a bond trades at a premium when its coupon rate exceeds current market rates
- A company setting the coupon rate on a new bond issue based on prevailing interest rates

**Key concepts:** `coupon rate`, `interest payment`, `face value`, `bond income`, `fixed rate`

---

## Q36. What is a credit rating?

**Level:** Basic

A credit rating is an assessment by rating agencies such as Moody's, S&P, and Fitch of the likelihood that a bond issuer will meet its financial obligations. Ratings range from AAA for the highest quality to D for default. Higher-rated bonds are considered safer and offer lower yields, while lower-rated bonds must offer higher yields to compensate investors for greater default risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- A pension fund restricted to investing only in bonds rated investment grade or higher
- A credit rating downgrade causing a company's bond prices to drop sharply
- An investor comparing the yields of AAA-rated and BBB-rated corporate bonds
- A government issuing bonds at low yields because of its strong sovereign credit rating

**Key concepts:** `credit rating`, `rating agency`, `investment grade`, `default risk`, `credit quality`

---

## Q37. What is duration in bond analysis?

**Level:** Basic

Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in interest rates. It is expressed in years and represents the weighted average time until a bond's cash flows are received. A bond with higher duration will experience larger price changes when interest rates move, making duration a key tool for managing interest rate risk in fixed-income portfolios.

**Real-life applications:**
- A portfolio manager shortening duration to protect against anticipated interest rate increases
- Comparing the duration of a 2-year treasury note versus a 30-year treasury bond
- A liability-driven investor matching the duration of assets to the duration of future obligations
- A bond fund prospectus reporting the portfolio's average duration as a risk measure

**Key concepts:** `duration`, `interest rate sensitivity`, `price volatility`, `bond risk`, `weighted average maturity`

---

## Q38. What is Modern Portfolio Theory?

**Level:** Basic

Modern Portfolio Theory, developed by Harry Markowitz in the 1950s, is a framework for building portfolios that maximize expected return for a given level of risk. The theory shows that the risk of a portfolio depends not only on individual asset risks but also on how assets move relative to each other. By combining assets with low correlations, overall portfolio risk can be reduced.

**Real-life applications:**
- A robo-advisor using optimization algorithms to build efficient portfolios for clients
- A wealth manager explaining to a client why adding international stocks reduces overall risk
- An academic researcher testing whether real-world portfolios align with MPT predictions
- An investment committee reviewing whether its endowment portfolio sits on the efficient frontier

**Key concepts:** `Modern Portfolio Theory`, `Markowitz`, `efficient frontier`, `correlation`, `portfolio optimization`

---

## Q39. What is the efficient frontier?

**Level:** Basic

The efficient frontier is a curve on a risk-return graph that represents the set of portfolios offering the highest expected return for each level of risk. Portfolios on the efficient frontier are considered optimal because no other combination of assets can achieve a better return for the same risk. Portfolios below the frontier are suboptimal because they take on unnecessary risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- A financial planner plotting a client's current portfolio against the efficient frontier to show room for improvement
- An investment software tool computing the efficient frontier from a set of available asset classes
- A pension fund aiming to move its portfolio closer to the efficient frontier during an asset-liability review
- A finance textbook illustrating the trade-off between risk and return using the efficient frontier

**Key concepts:** `efficient frontier`, `optimal portfolio`, `risk-return tradeoff`, `portfolio optimization`, `Markowitz`

---

## Q40. What is correlation in portfolio management?

**Level:** Basic

Correlation is a statistical measure that describes how two investments move relative to each other, ranging from negative one to positive one. A correlation of positive one means two assets move perfectly together, negative one means they move in exact opposite directions, and zero means no relationship. Low or negative correlations between portfolio assets are desirable because they reduce overall risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- Combining stocks and bonds in a portfolio because they often have low correlation
- An analyst calculating the correlation matrix for a set of asset classes before optimizing a portfolio
- Adding gold to a stock portfolio because gold frequently moves inversely to equities during crises
- A risk manager monitoring changes in asset correlations during periods of market stress

**Key concepts:** `correlation`, `positive correlation`, `negative correlation`, `diversification benefit`, `correlation matrix`

---

## Q41. What is the Capital Asset Pricing Model?

**Level:** Basic

The Capital Asset Pricing Model, or CAPM, is a formula that describes the relationship between an investment's expected return and its systematic risk as measured by beta. It states that expected return equals the risk-free rate plus the product of beta and the market risk premium. CAPM is widely used to estimate the cost of equity and to evaluate whether a security offers adequate compensation for its risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- A corporate finance team calculating the cost of equity for a net present value analysis
- An investor using CAPM to determine whether a stock is fairly priced relative to its risk
- A textbook teaching the foundations of asset pricing using the CAPM formula
- A financial regulator using CAPM to set allowed returns for regulated utilities

**Key concepts:** `CAPM`, `expected return`, `beta`, `market risk premium`, `risk-free rate`

---

## Q42. What is alpha in investment performance?

**Level:** Basic

Alpha represents the excess return of an investment relative to what would be expected given its level of systematic risk, as predicted by a benchmark or model like CAPM. A positive alpha means the investment outperformed expectations, while a negative alpha means it underperformed. Alpha is often used to measure the skill of a portfolio manager.

**Real-life applications:**
- An institutional investor hiring managers who have consistently generated positive alpha
- A hedge fund marketing its track record of positive alpha to attract new investors
- A mutual fund comparison showing that most active managers fail to generate alpha after fees
- A performance report attributing alpha to the manager's stock-picking ability

**Key concepts:** `alpha`, `excess return`, `outperformance`, `manager skill`, `benchmark`

---

## Q43. What is the security market line?

**Level:** Basic

The security market line is a graphical representation of the CAPM that plots expected return on the vertical axis against beta on the horizontal axis. All fairly priced securities should fall on this line. Securities above the line are considered undervalued because they offer more return than expected for their risk, while those below the line are considered overvalued.

**Real-life applications:**
- An analyst plotting several stocks on the SML to identify which are under or overvalued
- A finance class exercise where students determine if a stock is fairly priced using the SML
- A portfolio manager comparing actual portfolio returns to the return predicted by the SML
- An investment committee reviewing whether their equity holdings plot above or below the SML

**Key concepts:** `security market line`, `CAPM`, `expected return`, `beta`, `fair pricing`

---

## Q44. What is rebalancing a portfolio?

**Level:** Basic

Rebalancing is the process of realigning the weightings of portfolio assets back to the original target allocation. Over time, market movements cause some assets to grow faster than others, shifting the portfolio away from its intended risk profile. Rebalancing involves selling some of the outperformers and buying more of the underperformers to restore the desired balance.

**Real-life applications:**
- A target-date fund automatically rebalancing quarterly to maintain its stock-bond split
- An investor selling some stocks after a bull market run to bring equities back to 60 percent of the portfolio
- A financial advisor scheduling annual rebalancing reviews for client accounts
- A robo-advisor triggering rebalancing whenever any asset class drifts more than 5 percent from target

**Key concepts:** `rebalancing`, `target allocation`, `portfolio drift`, `buy low sell high`, `risk management`

---

## Q45. What is a support level in technical analysis?

**Level:** Basic

A support level is a price point at which a stock tends to stop falling because buying interest is strong enough to prevent further declines. It acts as a floor under the price where buyers consistently step in. When a stock bounces off the same support level multiple times, it reinforces the significance of that price point.

**Real-life applications:**
- A trader placing a buy order near a stock's established support level
- A chart analyst drawing a horizontal line at a price where a stock has bounced three times
- Setting a stop-loss order just below a support level to limit potential losses
- Recognizing that a break below a major support level may signal further declines

**Key concepts:** `support level`, `price floor`, `buying pressure`, `technical analysis`, `chart pattern`

---

## Q46. What is a resistance level in technical analysis?

**Level:** Basic

A resistance level is a price point at which a stock tends to stop rising because selling pressure is strong enough to prevent further gains. It acts as a ceiling above the price where sellers consistently emerge. A breakout above a strong resistance level is often considered a bullish signal indicating the stock may continue rising.

**Real-life applications:**
- A trader selling shares as a stock approaches a known resistance level
- A chart analyst noting that a stock has failed to break above 100 dollars five times
- A momentum investor buying when a stock breaks through resistance on high volume
- Setting a profit target near a major resistance level

**Key concepts:** `resistance level`, `price ceiling`, `selling pressure`, `breakout`, `technical analysis`

---

## Q47. What is a moving average?

**Level:** Basic

A moving average is a technical indicator that smooths out price data by calculating the average price over a specific number of periods. The two most common types are the simple moving average and the exponential moving average. Moving averages help identify the direction of a trend and are used to generate buy and sell signals.

**Real-life applications:**
- A trader using the 200-day moving average to determine whether a stock is in a long-term uptrend
- A golden cross signal occurring when the 50-day moving average crosses above the 200-day moving average
- A financial charting platform displaying multiple moving averages overlaid on a stock price chart
- An algorithmic trading system using moving average crossovers to automate trade execution

**Key concepts:** `moving average`, `simple moving average`, `exponential moving average`, `trend direction`, `trading signal`

---

## Q48. What is the relative strength index?

**Level:** Basic

The relative strength index, or RSI, is a momentum oscillator that measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes on a scale from zero to one hundred. Readings above 70 are generally considered overbought, suggesting the price may be due for a pullback, while readings below 30 are considered oversold, suggesting a potential rebound. RSI helps traders assess whether a security is being pushed to an extreme.

**Real-life applications:**
- A trader waiting for RSI to drop below 30 before buying a fundamentally strong stock
- A swing trader selling when RSI exceeds 70 after a sharp price rally
- Combining RSI with other indicators to confirm trade signals and reduce false positives
- A financial charting tool displaying RSI in a panel below the main price chart

**Key concepts:** `relative strength index`, `momentum`, `overbought`, `oversold`, `oscillator`

---

## Q49. What is loss aversion?

**Level:** Basic

Loss aversion is the psychological tendency for people to feel the pain of financial losses more intensely than the pleasure of equivalent gains. Studies suggest that losses are felt roughly twice as strongly as gains of the same size. This bias can cause investors to hold losing positions too long, hoping to avoid realizing a loss, or to sell winners too quickly to lock in gains.

**Real-life applications:**
- An investor refusing to sell a losing stock because it feels too painful to accept the loss
- A trader holding a declining position hoping it will return to the purchase price
- A portfolio manager recognizing loss aversion in client behavior during market downturns
- Behavioral coaching helping investors stick to their plan despite the emotional weight of losses

**Key concepts:** `loss aversion`, `behavioral bias`, `prospect theory`, `disposition effect`, `emotional investing`

---

## Q50. What is herd behavior in investing?

**Level:** Basic

Herd behavior is the tendency of investors to follow the actions of a larger group rather than making independent decisions based on their own analysis. When many investors rush to buy or sell the same assets at the same time, it can create market bubbles or panics. Herd behavior is driven by the fear of missing out or the fear of being the only one left holding a declining asset.

**Real-life applications:**
- Investors piling into technology stocks during the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s
- Panic selling during a market crash as investors follow others rushing for the exits
- Cryptocurrency price surges driven by social media hype and crowd behavior
- A contrarian investor profiting by going against the herd when sentiment reaches an extreme

**Key concepts:** `herd behavior`, `crowd psychology`, `market bubble`, `panic selling`, `contrarian investing`

---

## Q51. What is overconfidence bias?

**Level:** Basic

Overconfidence bias is the tendency of investors to overestimate their knowledge, ability to predict market movements, and the accuracy of their information. Overconfident investors tend to trade more frequently, take on excessive risk, and underestimate the probability of negative outcomes. Research consistently shows that overconfident traders earn lower returns on average than those who trade less.

**Real-life applications:**
- A day trader believing they can consistently beat the market despite evidence to the contrary
- An investor concentrating their portfolio in a few stocks because they are certain about their picks
- A fund manager ignoring warning signs because past success has inflated their confidence
- Behavioral finance workshops teaching investors to recognize and counteract overconfidence

**Key concepts:** `overconfidence bias`, `excessive trading`, `risk underestimation`, `behavioral finance`, `self-attribution`

---

## Q52. What is anchoring bias?

**Level:** Basic

Anchoring is the cognitive bias of relying too heavily on a specific reference point when making financial decisions. For investors, common anchors include the original purchase price of a stock, a previous high price, or an analyst's price target. This bias prevents investors from objectively evaluating current conditions because they are fixated on irrelevant historical numbers.

**Real-life applications:**
- An investor refusing to sell a stock until it returns to the price they paid, regardless of changed fundamentals
- A buyer anchoring on a house's listing price rather than its actual market value
- An analyst anchoring on last year's earnings estimate when projecting next year's numbers
- A negotiator anchored to the first number mentioned in a salary or deal discussion

**Key concepts:** `anchoring bias`, `reference point`, `cognitive bias`, `purchase price`, `objective evaluation`

---

## Q53. What is confirmation bias?

**Level:** Basic

Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek out, interpret, and remember information that supports your existing beliefs while ignoring or dismissing contradictory evidence. In investing, this means that someone who is bullish on a stock will focus on positive news and downplay negative developments. This bias can lead to poorly informed investment decisions and unexpected losses.

**Real-life applications:**
- An investor reading only favorable analyst reports about a stock they already own
- A fund manager ignoring deteriorating financial metrics because they believe in the company's story
- Social media echo chambers reinforcing bullish or bearish views on particular investments
- A financial advisor presenting both sides of an argument to help clients overcome confirmation bias

**Key concepts:** `confirmation bias`, `selective attention`, `information filtering`, `investment decision`, `cognitive bias`

---

## Q54. What is the Treynor ratio?

**Level:** Basic

The Treynor ratio measures the return earned in excess of the risk-free rate per unit of systematic risk, as measured by beta. Unlike the Sharpe ratio, which uses total risk (standard deviation), the Treynor ratio focuses only on market-related risk. It is most useful for evaluating well-diversified portfolios where unsystematic risk has been largely eliminated.

**Real-life applications:**
- Comparing the performance of two diversified mutual funds using the Treynor ratio
- A pension fund evaluating its equity managers on a systematic risk-adjusted basis
- A finance exam question asking students to calculate and interpret the Treynor ratio
- An investment report listing both Sharpe and Treynor ratios for a comprehensive performance assessment

**Key concepts:** `Treynor ratio`, `systematic risk`, `beta`, `risk-adjusted return`, `performance measurement`

---

## Q55. What is Jensen's alpha?

**Level:** Basic

Jensen's alpha measures the excess return that a portfolio generates above the return predicted by CAPM for its level of systematic risk. A positive Jensen's alpha indicates that the manager outperformed the risk-adjusted benchmark, while a negative alpha indicates underperformance. It is one of the most commonly used measures of portfolio manager skill.

**Real-life applications:**
- An institutional investor screening for fund managers with consistently positive Jensen's alpha
- A performance report showing that a fund generated 1.5 percent alpha over three years
- An academic study analyzing whether active managers generate statistically significant alpha
- A client presentation demonstrating the value added by a portfolio management team

**Key concepts:** `Jensen's alpha`, `CAPM`, `risk-adjusted performance`, `manager skill`, `excess return`

---

## Q56. What is the information ratio?

**Level:** Basic

The information ratio measures the consistency of a portfolio's excess returns relative to a benchmark by dividing the average excess return by the tracking error, which is the standard deviation of that excess return. A higher information ratio indicates that the manager is generating outperformance more consistently. It is particularly useful for evaluating active managers who aim to beat a specific benchmark.

**Real-life applications:**
- An investor choosing between two active fund managers based on their information ratios
- A pension consultant ranking managers by their ability to consistently outperform the benchmark
- A fund fact sheet reporting the information ratio alongside other performance statistics
- An investment team targeting an information ratio of at least 0.5 as a performance goal

**Key concepts:** `information ratio`, `tracking error`, `excess return`, `consistency`, `active management`

---

## Q57. What is net present value?

**Level:** Basic

Net present value, or NPV, is the difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows for an investment or project. A positive NPV means the investment is expected to add value, while a negative NPV means it is expected to destroy value. NPV is considered the gold standard for capital budgeting decisions because it accounts for the time value of money.

**Real-life applications:**
- A company calculating the NPV of a proposed factory to decide whether to proceed
- A real estate developer comparing the NPV of two potential building projects
- An MBA student solving NPV problems in a corporate finance class
- A private equity firm evaluating acquisition targets based on projected NPV

**Key concepts:** `net present value`, `capital budgeting`, `cash inflows`, `cash outflows`, `time value of money`

---

## Q58. What is the internal rate of return?

**Level:** Basic

The internal rate of return, or IRR, is the discount rate that makes the net present value of all cash flows from an investment equal to zero. In other words, it is the annualized rate of return an investment is expected to generate. Projects with an IRR exceeding the required rate of return are generally considered acceptable investments.

**Real-life applications:**
- A venture capital fund reporting its IRR to limited partners as a measure of fund performance
- A company comparing the IRR of two projects to determine which one to pursue
- A real estate investor calculating the IRR on a rental property investment
- A financial model computing IRR to assess the attractiveness of a leveraged buyout

**Key concepts:** `internal rate of return`, `discount rate`, `NPV equals zero`, `investment return`, `capital budgeting`

---

## Q59. What is a bull market?

**Level:** Basic

A bull market is a period of generally rising prices in a financial market, typically defined as a sustained increase of 20 percent or more from recent lows. Bull markets are characterized by optimism, investor confidence, and expectations of continued strong performance. They can last for months or years and are driven by strong economic conditions, rising corporate earnings, or accommodative monetary policy.

**Real-life applications:**
- The U.S. stock market bull run from 2009 to 2020 that followed the financial crisis
- Investors increasing their equity allocations during a bull market to capture gains
- Financial commentators debating whether a current rally qualifies as a new bull market
- A portfolio manager taking profits on stocks that have risen significantly in a prolonged bull market

**Key concepts:** `bull market`, `rising prices`, `investor confidence`, `market rally`, `economic expansion`

---

## Q60. What is a bear market?

**Level:** Basic

A bear market is a period of declining prices in a financial market, typically defined as a sustained drop of 20 percent or more from recent highs. Bear markets are characterized by pessimism, fear, and expectations of further losses. They are often associated with economic recessions and can last from several months to several years.

**Real-life applications:**
- The 2008 financial crisis causing a severe bear market in global equities
- Defensive investors moving to cash or bonds during a bear market to preserve capital
- Dollar-cost averaging through a bear market to buy stocks at progressively lower prices
- A hedge fund profiting from short positions during a market decline

**Key concepts:** `bear market`, `declining prices`, `market downturn`, `recession`, `defensive strategy`

## Q61. What is dollar-cost averaging?

**Level:** Basic

Dollar-cost averaging is an investment strategy where an investor puts a fixed amount of money into an investment at regular intervals, regardless of the asset's price. When prices are low, the fixed amount buys more shares, and when prices are high, it buys fewer shares. Over time, this approach lowers the average cost per share and removes the need to time the market.

**Real-life applications:**
- Contributing a fixed amount to a 401(k) plan with every paycheck
- Setting up automatic monthly purchases of an index fund in a brokerage account
- A new investor spreading a lump sum across 12 monthly investments to reduce timing risk
- A financial advisor recommending dollar-cost averaging for clients anxious about market volatility

**Key concepts:** `dollar-cost averaging`, `systematic investing`, `average cost`, `market timing`, `regular contributions`

---

## Q62. What is a stop-loss order?

**Level:** Basic

A stop-loss order is an instruction to automatically sell a security when its price falls to a specified level. It is designed to limit an investor's loss on a position by triggering a sale before the price drops further. Stop-loss orders are a basic risk management tool used by both short-term traders and long-term investors.

**Real-life applications:**
- A trader setting a stop-loss 10 percent below the purchase price to cap potential losses
- An investor placing a trailing stop that adjusts upward as the stock price rises
- A portfolio manager using stop-loss orders across all positions as part of a disciplined risk management strategy
- A stop-loss order triggering during a flash crash, selling shares at a temporarily depressed price

**Key concepts:** `stop-loss order`, `risk management`, `downside protection`, `sell trigger`, `trailing stop`

## Q63. What is the difference between active and passive investing?

**Level:** Basic

Active investing involves a manager or investor selecting individual securities and timing trades with the goal of outperforming a benchmark index. Passive investing involves buying and holding a diversified portfolio that tracks a market index, accepting market returns at low cost. Research shows that most active managers fail to consistently beat their benchmarks after fees, which has driven massive growth in passive investing.

**Real-life applications:**
- A mutual fund manager picking stocks to try to beat the S&P 500
- An investor buying a low-cost S&P 500 index fund and holding it for decades
- A pension fund debating whether to allocate more capital to passive strategies to reduce fees
- The growth of ETFs making passive investing accessible to individual investors

**Key concepts:** `active investing`, `passive investing`, `index fund`, `benchmark`, `expense ratio`

---

## Q64. What is the efficient market hypothesis?

**Level:** Basic

The efficient market hypothesis states that asset prices fully reflect all available information, making it impossible to consistently achieve returns above the market average through stock picking or market timing. It comes in three forms: weak, semi-strong, and strong, depending on what information is considered reflected in prices. This hypothesis underpins the argument for passive investing.

**Real-life applications:**
- The growth of index funds built on the belief that markets are efficient
- Active managers arguing that markets are not perfectly efficient and opportunities exist
- Academic research testing whether publicly available information predicts future stock returns
- A debate between proponents of efficient markets and behavioral finance advocates

**Key concepts:** `efficient market hypothesis`, `market efficiency`, `information`, `stock picking`, `passive investing`

---

## Q65. What is inflation risk?

**Level:** Basic

Inflation risk is the danger that the purchasing power of investment returns will be eroded by rising prices over time. Even if an investment generates a positive nominal return, the real return may be negative if inflation is higher. Fixed-income investments are particularly vulnerable to inflation risk because their payments are fixed in nominal terms.

**Real-life applications:**
- A retiree finding that fixed pension payments buy less each year as prices rise
- An investor choosing Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities to hedge against inflation
- Comparing nominal versus real returns when evaluating long-term investment performance
- Central banks raising interest rates to combat inflation, which in turn affects bond and stock prices

**Key concepts:** `inflation risk`, `purchasing power`, `real return`, `nominal return`, `TIPS`

---

## Q66. What is interest rate risk?

**Level:** Basic

Interest rate risk is the risk that changes in interest rates will negatively affect the value of an investment, particularly bonds. When interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall because newly issued bonds offer higher yields. The longer a bond's maturity or duration, the more sensitive it is to interest rate changes.

**Real-life applications:**
- A bond portfolio losing value when the central bank unexpectedly raises interest rates
- A portfolio manager reducing duration to protect against expected rate hikes
- Homebuyers locking in mortgage rates to protect against rising interest rates
- Banks managing the mismatch between the interest rates on their assets and liabilities

**Key concepts:** `interest rate risk`, `bond prices`, `duration`, `rate changes`, `fixed income`

---

## Q67. What is credit risk?

**Level:** Basic

Credit risk is the risk that a bond issuer or borrower will fail to make required payments, including interest and principal. Higher credit risk means a greater chance of default, and investors demand higher yields to compensate. Credit ratings from agencies like Moody's and S&P help investors assess the credit risk of different issuers.

**Real-life applications:**
- An investor receiving a higher yield on a junk bond to compensate for elevated default risk
- A corporate bond being downgraded from investment grade to speculative grade
- A bank assessing the credit risk of loan applicants before approving mortgages
- The 2008 financial crisis triggered partly by underestimated credit risk in mortgage-backed securities

**Key concepts:** `credit risk`, `default`, `credit rating`, `yield spread`, `creditworthiness`

---

## Q68. What is the price-to-book ratio?

**Level:** Basic

The price-to-book ratio compares a stock's market price to its book value per share, which is the net asset value of the company divided by the number of outstanding shares. A ratio below one may suggest the stock is undervalued relative to its assets, while a high ratio may indicate that investors expect strong future growth. Value investors often screen for stocks with low price-to-book ratios.

**Real-life applications:**
- A value investor screening for bank stocks trading below book value during a market downturn
- Comparing the price-to-book ratios of companies in the same industry to identify relative value
- An analyst noting that technology companies often have high price-to-book ratios because their value lies in intangible assets
- Using price-to-book alongside other metrics to build a comprehensive valuation picture

**Key concepts:** `price-to-book ratio`, `book value`, `net assets`, `valuation metric`, `value investing`

---

## Q69. What is a benchmark in portfolio management?

**Level:** Basic

A benchmark is a standard against which the performance of a portfolio or investment fund is measured. Common benchmarks include market indexes like the S&P 500 for U.S. large-cap stocks or the Bloomberg Aggregate Bond Index for fixed income. Choosing the right benchmark is critical because comparing a portfolio to an inappropriate benchmark can give a misleading picture of performance.

**Real-life applications:**
- A large-cap equity fund comparing its annual return to the S&P 500 index
- A global bond fund using the Bloomberg Global Aggregate as its benchmark
- An investor questioning a fund manager who consistently trails the benchmark after fees
- A pension fund's investment policy statement specifying the benchmark for each asset class

**Key concepts:** `benchmark`, `performance measurement`, `index`, `relative return`, `investment policy`

---

## Q70. What is the difference between nominal and real returns?

**Level:** Basic

Nominal return is the raw percentage gain or loss on an investment without adjusting for inflation. Real return adjusts the nominal return for the effects of inflation, showing the actual increase in purchasing power. The real return is always lower than the nominal return when inflation is positive, and it provides a more accurate picture of investment performance.

**Real-life applications:**
- An investment earning 7 percent nominal return with 3 percent inflation delivers approximately 4 percent real return
- Retirement planners using real returns to estimate future purchasing power of savings
- Comparing returns across countries with different inflation rates using real returns
- Historical stock market data often presented in both nominal and real terms for accuracy

**Key concepts:** `nominal return`, `real return`, `inflation adjustment`, `purchasing power`, `investment performance`

**Components:** Cash → T-Bills → G-Sec → IG → HY → LC Equity → SC → EM → PE/VC.
**Worked example (Consulting):** Advising SaaS CFO on idle $50M — cap at IG corporate, 6-9 mo duration matched.
**Trigger:** Any allocation or treasury mandate.

**Purpose:** Show MPT's optimal risk-return tradeoff and tangency.

     |                   __--
     |                _-- * Tangency (max Sharpe)
     |    CAL      _--
     |   / ___---
     | /__--
     |Rf
     +--------------------------------> σ

### 3.4 Valuation Method Selector
**Purpose:** Pick the right model in 10 seconds.
```
Is firm dividend-paying & stable? -- Yes --> DDM (Gordon / H-model)
              | No
              v
Positive, forecastable FCF? -- Yes --> DCF
              | No
              v
Peer comps available & liquid? -- Yes --> Multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales)
              | No
              v
Asset-heavy / distress? --------------> NAV / Sum-of-parts
```
**Components:** Stability, FCF quality, comps, asset intensity.
**Worked example (IT):** Valuing pre-IPO SaaS → no dividends, lumpy FCF → EV/Sales on ARR with Rule of 40 adjustment.
**Trigger:** "What's this company worth?" in any interview.

## 4. Formulas

1. **TVM:** `PV = FV / (1+r)^n`; `FV = PV(1+r)^n`.
   Threshold: use r = WACC for firms, opportunity cost for individuals.
   **Example (IT):** $100K RSU vesting in 4Y at r=8% → PV = 100,000/1.08^4 ≈ **$73,503**.

2. **Sharpe & Beta:** `Sharpe = (Rp − Rf)/σp`; `β = Cov(Ri,Rm)/Var(Rm)`.
   Threshold: Sharpe >1 good, >2 very good; β>1 aggressive.
   **Example (Product):** Portfolio Rp=14%, Rf=6%, σ=12% → Sharpe = 8/12 = **0.67** (average).

3. **CAPM:** `E(R) = Rf + β(Rm − Rf)`.
   Threshold: use β from 2-5Y regression; ERP 5-6% for US, 7-8% India.
   **Example (Consulting):** β=1.3, Rf=7%, ERP=7% → E(R) = 7 + 1.3·7 = **16.1%** cost of equity.

4. **Gordon Growth (DDM):** `P = D1/(r−g)`.
   Threshold: g < r strictly; g ≤ long-run GDP growth (~5-6%).
   **Example (AI):** Dividend-paying IT major D1=₹30, r=11%, g=6% → P = 30/0.05 = **₹600**.

5. **Duration Price Change:** `ΔP/P ≈ −Modified Duration · Δy`.
   Threshold: convexity correction needed beyond ±100 bps moves.
   **Example (Consulting):** 5Y duration bond, rates rise 50 bps → price falls ≈ **2.5%**.

6. **DCF Intrinsic Value:** `IV = Σ FCFt/(1+WACC)^t + TV/(1+WACC)^n`, `TV = FCFn+1/(WACC−g)`.
   Threshold: explicit horizon 5-10Y; TV often 60-80% of value — stress-test g.
   **Example (SaaS):** FCF $10M growing 15% 5Y, WACC 10%, g=3% → TV = FCF6/(0.10-0.03); full IV ≈ **$245M** (model-dependent).

## 5. Do vs Don't

| Do | Don't |
|----|-------|
| Quote Sharpe with σ context | Compare Sharpe across asset classes with different distributions |
| Use CAPM β from 2-5Y weekly regression | Use β without checking R² significance |
| Apply margin of safety 20-30% on DCF IV | Trade DCF point estimate as truth |
| Match bond duration to liability horizon | Chase YTM without duration awareness |
| Rebalance using ±5% bands or quarterly cadence | Rebalance on P&L FOMO |
| Separate systematic (β) from unsystematic (σ-β) risk | Confuse total risk (σ) with market risk (β) |
| Anchor valuation to 2+ methods (DCF + Multiples) | Rely on single-method valuation in interview |
| Call out behavioral biases in investment memo | Treat markets as perfectly rational always |

## 9. Quick-Recall Card
- Value = PV of cashflows; always apply margin of safety.
- CAPM prices only systematic risk (β), not total risk (σ).
- Diversification works when ρ < 1; beyond ~20 stocks, gains flatten.
- Sharpe compares total-risk-adjusted; Treynor compares β-adjusted; IR tracks active skill.
- Duration tells you rate sensitivity; match to liability horizon.
- As a PM/Consultant/AI Lead, the one question to answer with this framework is: **Is the risk-adjusted return on this capital deployment (project, portfolio, or treasury float) superior to the CAPM-implied hurdle, after a 25% margin of safety?**
