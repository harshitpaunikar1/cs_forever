# 27. Consumer Protection

## Overview

Consumer law protects buyers from defective goods, poor services, and unfair business practices.

---

## Why It Matters

It builds trust and forces businesses to maintain quality and honest dealing.


## Key Principles

- Consumer rights (safety, information, choice, redressal)
- Complaint and dispute redressal forums
- Liability for defects/deficiency
- Refund/replacement/compensation remedies


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Deficiency** | Fault in service |
| **Defect** | Problem in product |
| **Redressal** | Remedy given by authority |


## Use Case

Customer files complaint for defective appliance.


## Scenario

> A phone delivered is damaged; seller refuses replacement. Consumer forum orders refund.


## Examples

- Airline cancels flight and fails refund as per rules → complaint.
- Defective helmet causes injury → compensation claim.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Redressal Priority Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide how fast and how strongly to respond to a customer issue.
[verified from model knowledge, not source] This is a practical triage overlay for complaints about defects, deficiencies, and unfair practices.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                         Harm severity
                      Low                         High
Fixability
Easy                  Refund or quick replacement   Fast remedy plus apology and monitoring
Hard                  Manual case handling           Legal, compliance, and leadership escalation
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Harm severity means whether the customer lost money, time, trust, safety, or access.
Component 2: Fixability means whether the issue can be solved by support, billing, or operations without a wider intervention.
Component 3: Evidence quality means screenshots, logs, invoices, and complaint history that show what happened.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A SaaS customer says a reporting module missed three days of data and caused a missed board update. If the missing data can be restored quickly, the team should treat it as easy-fix/high-harm and issue a fast remedy. If the issue is tied to a recurring integration failure, it moves into hard-fix/high-harm and needs escalation.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when support, product, and legal disagree about whether to refund, replace, or investigate.

## 4. Formulas

[verified from model knowledge, not source] Formula: `Complaint Rate per 1,000 Users = (Total complaints / Active users) x 1,000`
Variables:
Total complaints = all logged grievances in the period
Active users = customers or users who were actually served in the period
Why this formula exists: It answers whether consumer issues are isolated or systemic.
How to interpret the output:
Below 1 -> low issue density -> monitor
1 to 5 -> warning zone -> investigate patterns
Above 5 -> high issue density -> escalate and fix root cause
Worked example with numbers: [verified from model knowledge, not source] If a subscription product has 12 complaints and 2,000 active users, the complaint rate is 6 per 1,000 users. That lands above the warning zone and should trigger a root-cause review.

## 5. Do vs Don't

[verified from model knowledge, not source] These controls translate consumer rights into business actions.

| Don't | Do |
|---------|-------|
| Dismiss every complaint as an isolated annoyance | Track complaint patterns by product, team, and issue type |
| Promise a fix without checking feasibility | Verify refund, replacement, and repair options first |
| Hide terms in a pricing page or contract footnote | Disclose costs, limits, and exclusions clearly |
| Treat service outages as only a support problem | Escalate repeated failures to product and operations |
| Wait until a forum notice arrives | Resolve early and document the remedy trail |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

[verified from model knowledge, not source] The scenarios below turn the source rights and remedies into measurable operating rules.

Scenario 1: SaaS outage and billing refund
Situation: A B2B SaaS platform loses access for 90 minutes during a customer launch. The customer files a complaint for service deficiency and asks for a refund.
Applicable framework/metric: Complaint Rate per 1,000 Users.
Analysis: If 18 customers complain out of 3,600 active users, the rate is 5 per 1,000. That is in the warning zone and suggests the incident is not just a one-off.
Decision rule: If the rate is above 5, escalate. If it is between 1 and 5, investigate. If it is below 1, monitor.
Action: Issue a service credit, publish the incident review, and log the root cause.

Scenario 2: AI feature gives misleading outputs
Situation: A consulting team uses an AI research tool that returns incorrect market numbers in a client deck. The client complains that the service output was deficient and asks for compensation.
Applicable framework/metric: Harm severity and fixability.
Analysis: If the issue affected one slide, the harm is low and the fix is easy. If it changed a strategic recommendation used in a steering committee, the harm is high and the fix is hard.
Decision rule: If harm is high and fixability is hard, escalate to legal and leadership. If harm is low and fixability is easy, replace the deck and apologize.
Action: Reissue the deck, add a QA step, and review the AI validation process.

Scenario 3: Hardware defect in a field deployment
Situation: A product team ships a device with a battery defect that causes repeated shutdowns. The customer has 40 units live and two have already failed.
Applicable framework/metric: Defect rate.
Analysis: If 2 of 40 units fail, the observed defect rate is 5 percent.
Decision rule: If defect rate is above 3 percent, trigger a replacement plan. If it is between 1 and 3 percent, monitor with containment. If it is below 1 percent, keep watch and log complaints.
Action: Replace the failed units, isolate the lot, and prepare a customer notice.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

[verified from model knowledge, not source] This playbook makes the source's consumer rights usable inside a business.

1. Build a complaint intake form that captures product, service, date, harm, and evidence.
2. Separate defect, deficiency, and unfair practice cases at triage.
3. Define refund, replacement, and compensation rules by scenario.
4. Set service-level targets for first response and final redressal.
5. Escalate safety or repeat-failure cases to product, legal, and leadership.
6. Review complaint trends monthly to find systemic issues.
7. Record every remedy so the team can defend the decision later.
