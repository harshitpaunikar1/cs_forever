# 36. Patents, Copyrights and Designs

## Overview

These laws protect inventions (patents), creative works (copyright), and product appearance (designs).

---

## Why It Matters

IP protection stops copying and helps businesses earn through exclusivity and licensing.


## Key Principles

- Patents need novelty and registration
- Copyright protects original work (often automatically)
- Designs protect visual look/shape
- Enforcement through notices and legal action


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Patent** | Right over invention |
| **Copyright** | Right over original expression |
| **Design** | Protection for product appearance |
| **Licensing** | Permission to use IP for payment |


## Use Case

Tech company patents a new process and licenses it.


## Scenario

> A competitor copies a product design. Original company sues for design infringement.


## Examples

- Patent for a new medical device component.
- Copyright in software code and marketing content.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Patents, copyright, and designs are the three basic IP shields in the source: inventions, creative works, and product appearance. The business decision is whether to protect what you built, license it, or defend it when someone copies it.
For IT, AI, product, and consulting leaders, the point is to turn knowledge assets into defensible revenue instead of leaving them exposed to imitation.

## 2. Jargon & Terminology

| Term | Full Form | Plain-English Meaning | Why It Exists | How It's Measured | Where You'll Hear It |
|------|-----------|----------------------|---------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Patent | - | Right over an invention | To protect technical novelty | Filing status, grant status | R&D, product strategy |
| Copyright | - | Right over original expression | To protect creative output | Ownership, registration where applicable | Software, content, media |
| Design | - | Protection for product appearance | To protect look and shape | Design filing, protected features | Industrial design, UX, packaging |

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### IP Protection Stack
**Purpose:** Decide what kind of IP protection fits the asset.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
      Most technical / functional
                 ^
                 |
              Patent
                 |
      Original code / content
                 |
             Copyright
                 |
      Look / shape / visual form
                 |
               Design
                 v
      Most visual / aesthetic
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Functionality, which points toward patent protection.
Component 2: Expression, which points toward copyright.
Component 3: Appearance, which points toward design protection.
Component 4: Monetization path, which tells whether to license or enforce.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company builds a new workflow engine, writes the code for it, and creates a distinct UI shell. The algorithmic method may be patentable if it is truly novel [verified from model knowledge, not source], the code is copyrightable as original expression, and the UI skin or device shell may be design-protectable. A consulting team would recommend filing and marking each asset separately instead of treating all IP as one bucket. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when a team asks "how do we protect this?" and the answer depends on whether the value is technical, textual, or visual.

### Protect vs License Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether to keep exclusivity or monetize the IP through permission grants.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    High direct control
                           ^
                           |
          Protect and use  |   Protect and license
          internally only   |   to selected partners
---------------------------+------------------------------> Low direct control
          Low monetization  |   Broad licensing / aggressive monetization
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Direct control over use, which matters when the IP is strategic.
Component 2: Revenue potential, which matters when licensing can scale faster than in-house use.
Component 3: Competitive risk, which rises if the IP is over-shared.
Component 4: Enforcement cost, which influences whether to sue or license.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm has a proprietary risk-assessment template and an AI prompt library. If the assets are core to client differentiation, it should keep them internal. If they are easy to package and non-core, it can license them to channel partners under a tight agreement. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when legal says "we can license it," but strategy needs to know whether that weakens the moat.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: Licensing revenue = royalty rate x revenue base
Variables:
royalty rate = fee charged for IP use
revenue base = sales or usage volume the royalty applies to
Why this formula exists: It tells you how much cash the IP can generate through permission-based monetization.
How to interpret the output:
Value < expected enforcement cost -> licensing may be unattractive
Value close to enforcement cost -> negotiate terms carefully
Value > enforcement cost -> licensing becomes attractive
Worked example with numbers: If a software patent is licensed at 4% on a 50,00,000 revenue base, revenue is 2,00,000. That can be better than a dispute if the asset is non-core. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: Infringement exposure score = unauthorized uses / total monitored uses
Variables:
unauthorized uses = observed misuse instances
total monitored uses = all relevant uses being tracked
Why this formula exists: It shows how much misuse is slipping through the control system.
How to interpret the output:
Value = 0 -> no known misuse -> continue monitoring
Value 0-0.05 -> low leakage -> send a notice and tighten monitoring
Value > 0.05 -> serious leakage -> escalate enforcement
Worked example with numbers: If 2 of 30 monitored product samples show copied design elements, the score is 0.067. That is enough to justify counsel review and a notice. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat invention, code, and product look as the same thing | Match the IP type to the asset being protected |
| Wait until copying is obvious before filing or documenting | Capture novelty, authorship, and design evidence early |
| Assume copyright protects an idea by itself | Protect the original expression, not just the concept |
| License IP without checking scope, territory, and term | Put clear boundaries in the license agreement |
| Ignore copying because the copied item seems small | Track infringement patterns and enforce consistently |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI model workflow protection
Situation: An AI startup builds a novel data-cleaning pipeline that improves model quality. A competitor copies the workflow after seeing the product demo.
Applicable framework/metric: IP Protection Stack; novelty review.
Analysis: If the core value is technical and truly new, patent protection may matter. If the value is in the code and documentation, copyright helps. If the product shell is copied too, design protection may also be relevant. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If the asset is functional and novel, assess patentability; if it is code or content, document copyright; if appearance is copied, review design rights.
Action: Run an IP inventory and route each asset to legal, product, or engineering.

Scenario 2: Product company licensing packaging design
Situation: A consumer software product has a distinctive interface and brand skin that another channel partner wants to use in a white-label deployment. The product team is deciding whether to license the look-and-feel or keep it exclusive.
Applicable framework/metric: Protect vs License Matrix; licensing revenue.
Analysis: If the design is part of the core brand moat, over-licensing can damage differentiation. If the partner is strategic and the design is not the main moat, a narrow license can create revenue. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If the asset is strategic, protect; if it is monetizable and non-core, license; if both are true, carve out limited rights.
Action: Draft a narrow, territory-limited license with renewal and audit clauses.

Scenario 3: Consulting content infringement
Situation: A consulting team finds that a competitor copied its client workshop deck and framework language into a public-facing sales pitch.
Applicable framework/metric: Infringement exposure score.
Analysis: If repeated unauthorized uses are showing up across sales materials, the score rises and a notice is justified. If the copying is a one-off and low impact, a notice may still be enough to stop it without litigation. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If exposure is near zero, monitor; if small but real, send a notice; if repeated, escalate legally.
Action: Preserve evidence, notify counsel, and request takedown or settlement.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Inventory every invention, code base, design, and content asset that has business value.
2. Separate what is patentable, copyrightable, or design-protectable.
3. Document authorship, dates, and prior art before the asset is public.
4. Decide which assets stay internal and which should be licensed.
5. Write license terms for scope, territory, term, payment, and enforcement rights.
6. Create a monitoring process for copying, misuse, and competitor similarity.
7. Escalate notices quickly when infringement appears.
