# 25. Agency: Rights and Duties

## Overview

Agents have duties like honesty and care. Principals have duties like paying commission and supporting the agent.

---

## Why It Matters

Clear duties reduce fraud, mis-selling, and disputes over commission.


## Key Principles

- Agent must follow instructions
- Duty of loyalty and confidentiality
- Principal must pay remuneration
- Agent must keep proper accounts


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Fiduciary duty** | Duty of loyalty and trust |
| **Commission** | Agent’s earning per deal |
| **Accounting** | Record of transactions |


## Use Case

Commission-based distribution agreement.


## Scenario

> An agent hides extra profit from principal; principal can sue for breach of duty.


## Examples

- Agent must not represent competitor without permission.
- Principal must reimburse agent’s valid expenses.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
This topic explains what an agent owes the principal and what the principal owes the agent.  
It matters when an IT, AI, product, or consulting team uses resellers, channel partners, freelance sales reps, or implementation agents.  
The core decision is how to structure authority, commission, confidentiality, and recordkeeping so the relationship scales without disputes.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Principal-Agent Duty Map
**Purpose:** Turn the source’s short list of rights and duties into an operating checklist for an agency relationship.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                PRINCIPAL
      pays commission / supports / reimburses
                          ^
                          |
Agent follows instructions |  Agent keeps accounts
and acts loyally           |  and protects confidentiality
                          |
                          v
                 PERFORMANCE + TRUST
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Agent duties - follow instructions, loyalty, confidentiality, proper accounts.
Component 2: Principal duties - pay remuneration, support the agent, reimburse valid expenses.
Component 3: Trust controls - records, approvals, conflict checks, and payment clarity.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm appoints a regional AI reseller to sell a workflow automation product. The reseller may quote only approved pricing, cannot share customer data with a competitor, must file a weekly deal log, and gets commission only on closed-won deals that survive the refund window.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When a channel partner, contractor, or broker asks, “Who owns what, who pays for what, and what can I do on your behalf?”

### Commission-Control Matrix
**Purpose:** Separate legitimate incentive design from avoidable disputes.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    CONTROL LEVEL
                 Low ---------------- High
PAYMENT CLARITY  --------------------------------
Low              | disputed payouts | fraud risk  |
High             | simple payouts    | scalable    |
                 --------------------------------
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Payment clarity - whether the formula, timing, and triggers are written down.
Component 2: Control level - whether approvals, reconciliation, and evidence exist.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team uses freelance enterprise sellers for a new AI feature launch. If commission is defined only as “good faith success fee,” the team gets disputes; if it is tied to signed order forms and logged approvals, payout and audit are both manageable.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When compensation design is becoming subjective or the agent is pushing for exceptions.

## 4. Formulas
Formula: `Net commission payout = agreed commission - disputed reversals - unreimbursed offsets` [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Variables:
`agreed commission` = the amount promised in the contract  
`disputed reversals` = clawbacks or cancelled-deal deductions  
`unreimbursed offsets` = approved business costs not yet settled

Why this formula exists: It answers the finance question, “What should the agent actually receive after adjustments?”

How to interpret the output:
Value = agreed commission → clean payout path → pay on schedule
Value below agreed commission but above zero → partial reversal → reconcile and explain
Value near zero or negative → serious dispute or excessive offsets → pause payout and review

Worked example with numbers: If agreed commission is $8,000, reversals are $1,200, and unreimbursed offsets are $300, net payout is $6,500. A consulting ops team should pay that amount only after confirming the offsets are contractually valid.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Let the agent improvise pricing or terms in the field. | Write down the exact authority the agent has before they contact customers. |
| Ignore side deals or hidden margin capture. | Require the agent to disclose all compensation and benefits tied to the relationship. |
| Pay commission without a clear trigger. | Tie commission to a documented event such as signed order, delivery milestone, or invoice collection. |
| Treat expenses as informal favours. | Pre-approve reimbursable expenses and require receipts. |
| Assume trust removes the need for records. | Keep proper accounts so finance and legal can reconcile the relationship. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI reseller commission dispute
Situation: An AI startup uses a regional reseller to sell a workflow automation add-on to enterprise IT buyers. The reseller says a verbal commitment entitles them to commission on a deal that later got repriced by the principal.
Applicable framework/metric: Commission-Control Matrix.
Analysis: Payment is unclear and control is weak, so dispute risk is high. If the contract does not define the commission trigger, the startup has no clean basis to reject the claim.
Decision rule: If the deal trigger is documented, pay the agreed commission. If the trigger is ambiguous, settle only after written clarification. If no evidence exists, treat it as a disputed claim.
Action: Freeze future verbal exceptions, add a signed payout schedule, and require order-form linkage for all channel payments.

Scenario 2: Consulting field expense abuse
Situation: A consulting associate acting as the principal’s agent books client dinners, travel, and local logistics while closing a transformation project. Some receipts are missing, and the expense report includes items unrelated to client work.
Applicable framework/metric: Proper-accounts control.
Analysis: Missing documentation breaks the accounting duty and makes reimbursement unverifiable. The issue is not just cost, but whether the agent can prove the expense was valid.
Decision rule: If receipts and business purpose are documented, reimburse. If documentation is partial, hold and request support. If the expense is unrelated, deny it.
Action: Reimburse only approved items, publish an expense policy, and audit every high-value claim.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Draft an agency charter that names the agent, the scope of authority, and the forbidden actions.
2. Define the commission trigger, payment date, and reversal rules in one place.
3. Set a reimbursement policy with pre-approval thresholds and receipt requirements.
4. Require weekly transaction accounting so the principal can reconcile deals, payments, and expenses.
5. Add a conflict-of-interest disclosure step before any partner or broker starts selling.
6. Create a confidentiality clause covering customer data, pricing, roadmap, and implementation details.
7. Review disputes monthly and update the contract language when ambiguity repeats.
