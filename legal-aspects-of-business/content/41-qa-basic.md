# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational questions covering core legal concepts for business.

---

## Q1. What do we mean by “Legal Aspects of Business”?

**Level:** Basic

Legal Aspects of Business refers to the rules and laws that control how businesses start, operate, make agreements, sell products, hire people, and resolve disputes. It covers topics like contracts, consumer rights, company formation, competition rules, intellectual property, and cyber laws. The main idea is that business activities must be done within legal boundaries to stay safe, fair, and trustworthy.

**Real-life applications:**

- Businesses use legal rules to draft agreements and avoid disputes.
- Consumers rely on these laws for safety and fair treatment.
- Companies follow compliance laws to prevent penalties and shutdowns.
- Courts use these principles to resolve business conflicts.

**Key concepts:** `business law` `compliance` `legal rights` `legal duties` `dispute resolution`

---

## Q2. What is the difference between “law” and “business ethics”?

**Level:** Basic

Law is a set of rules made and enforced by the state, and breaking it can lead to penalties. Business ethics are moral guidelines about what is right or fair, even if not written in law. A business can follow the law but still act unethically, such as using misleading but technically legal advertising.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies follow laws to avoid fines and legal action.
- Ethical standards build trust and long-term reputation.
- Employers use ethics policies for fair workplace behavior.
- Regulators focus on legal compliance, not moral choices.

**Key concepts:** `law` `ethics` `compliance` `moral responsibility` `regulation`

---

## Q3. What is a “contract” in business law?

**Level:** Basic

A contract is a legally enforceable agreement between two or more parties. It creates legal duties, meaning each side must do what they promised. If one party fails, the other can ask for legal remedies such as compensation. Contracts can be written, spoken, or created by conduct, but they must meet legal requirements to be valid.

**Real-life applications:**

- Businesses sign contracts with suppliers, employees, and clients.
- Contracts set clear expectations on price, quality, and timelines.
- Written contracts help prevent misunderstandings.
- Courts enforce contracts when disputes arise.

**Key concepts:** `contract` `agreement` `enforceability` `obligations` `remedies`

---

## Q4. What is an “agreement,” and how is it related to a contract?

**Level:** Basic

An agreement is formed when one party makes an offer and the other accepts it. A contract is a special type of agreement that the law will enforce. So, every contract is an agreement, but not every agreement becomes a contract. For example, social promises usually remain agreements without legal force.

**Real-life applications:**

- Business deals start as agreements during negotiation.
- Once legal conditions are met, they become enforceable contracts.
- This helps businesses rely on promises made in trade.
- Clear agreements reduce risk of future conflict.

**Key concepts:** `agreement` `offer` `acceptance` `contract` `enforceable`

---

## Q5. What is an “offer” in contract law?

**Level:** Basic

An offer is a clear proposal made by one party to another, showing willingness to enter into a contract on specific terms. The offer must be definite and communicated. If the other party accepts it without changes, a contract may be formed. Invitations like advertisements are often invitations to offer, not offers.

**Real-life applications:**

- Sellers give offers through quotations or proposals.
- Buyers accept offers through purchase orders or confirmations.
- Clear offers help avoid disputes about what was promised.
- Businesses keep written records to prove offers.

**Key concepts:** `offer` `proposal` `communication` `intention` `certainty of terms`

---

## Q6. What is “acceptance” in a contract?

**Level:** Basic

Acceptance is the unconditional agreement to the terms of an offer. It must match the offer exactly and be communicated in the required way. If acceptance changes the terms, it usually becomes a counter-offer, not acceptance. Once valid acceptance happens, an agreement is created.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies accept bids by sending written confirmations.
- Online buyers accept terms by clicking “I agree.”
- Acceptance timing can decide when legal duties start.
- Clear acceptance prevents confusion about final terms.

**Key concepts:** `acceptance` `unconditional assent` `communication` `counter-offer` `consensus`

## Q7. What is “consideration” in a contract?

**Level:** Basic

Consideration means something of value exchanged between the parties, such as money, goods, services, or a promise. It is the reason a party enters the contract. In many cases, a promise without consideration is not enforceable as a contract, unless it falls under specific legal exceptions.

**Real-life applications:**

- Customers pay money to receive goods or services.
- Employers pay salary in exchange for employee work.
- Consideration makes business promises legally reliable.
- It supports fair exchange in commercial relationships.

**Key concepts:** `consideration` `exchange of value` `promise` `benefit` `obligation`

## Q8. What does “capacity to contract” mean?

**Level:** Basic

Capacity to contract means the legal ability of a person to enter into a valid contract. Generally, minors, persons of unsound mind, and persons disqualified by law cannot make enforceable contracts. Capacity ensures that parties understand the agreement and can be held responsible for their promises.

**Real-life applications:**

- Businesses verify age and identity before signing deals.
- Banks check customer capacity before giving loans.
- Contracts with minors can become legally risky.
- Capacity rules protect vulnerable persons from exploitation.

**Key concepts:** `capacity` `minor` `sound mind` `legal competence` `disqualification`

## Q9. What is “free consent” in contract law?

**Level:** Basic

Free consent means that parties agree voluntarily, without force, fraud, misrepresentation, undue influence, or coercion. If consent is not free, the contract may be voidable or void depending on the situation. Free consent ensures fairness and genuine agreement.

**Real-life applications:**

- Businesses avoid pressure tactics during signing.
- Customers can challenge contracts formed by deception.
- Free consent reduces unfair advantage in negotiations.
- Courts protect parties who were forced to agree.

**Key concepts:** `free consent` `coercion` `fraud` `misrepresentation` `undue influence`

---

## Q10. What is the meaning of “lawful object” in a contract?

**Level:** Basic

Lawful object means the purpose of the contract must be legal and not against public policy. If the contract is made for illegal activities, it becomes void and cannot be enforced. The law will not support agreements that harm society or break rules.

**Real-life applications:**

- Businesses avoid contracts connected to illegal trade.
- Companies conduct legal checks before partnerships.
- Courts refuse to enforce illegal agreements.
- This protects society from harmful transactions.

**Key concepts:** `lawful object` `illegality` `public policy` `void agreement` `enforceability`

## Q11. What is a “void agreement”?

**Level:** Basic

A void agreement is an agreement that has no legal effect from the beginning. It cannot be enforced by law because it lacks legal requirements or involves illegal objectives. Since it is treated as invalid, neither party can claim rights based on it.

**Real-life applications:**

- Businesses avoid unclear or illegal deals to prevent losses.
- Courts dismiss claims based on void agreements.
- This encourages lawful and properly formed contracts.
- It protects people from forced illegal enforcement.

**Key concepts:** `void agreement` `invalid` `unenforceable` `legality` `contract essentials`

---

## Q12. What is a “voidable contract”?

**Level:** Basic

A voidable contract is a valid contract that one party can cancel because of issues like coercion, fraud, misrepresentation, or undue influence. Until it is cancelled, it remains enforceable. This rule protects the harmed party while keeping the contract valid unless challenged.

**Real-life applications:**

- Customers can cancel contracts based on false promises.
- Businesses can void agreements signed under pressure.
- It discourages dishonest sales tactics.
- Courts provide remedies when consent was not free.

**Key concepts:** `voidable contract` `free consent` `rescission` `fraud` `coercion`

## Q13. What is “breach of contract”?

**Level:** Basic

Breach of contract happens when a party fails to perform what they promised in the contract, such as not delivering goods, not paying money, or delivering late. A breach gives the other party a right to seek remedies like damages, termination, or specific performance depending on the case.

**Real-life applications:**

- Buyers claim compensation for late deliveries.
- Employers act when employees violate contract terms.
- Service providers sue for non-payment.
- Businesses use penalty clauses to reduce breach risk.

**Key concepts:** `breach` `non-performance` `remedies` `liability` `enforcement`

---

## Q14. What are “damages” in contract law?

**Level:** Basic

Damages are money awarded to compensate a party for loss caused by breach of contract. The goal is to place the injured party in a position similar to if the contract had been performed. Damages are usually based on actual loss that is reasonably connected to the breach.

**Real-life applications:**

- Contractors pay damages for delayed completion.
- Suppliers compensate buyers for defective goods.
- Courts calculate compensation based on proven loss.
- Businesses include damages clauses for clarity.

**Key concepts:** `damages` `compensation` `breach` `loss` `remedy`

---

## Q15. What is “specific performance” as a remedy?

**Level:** Basic

Specific performance is a court order requiring a party to perform their contractual promise instead of paying damages. It is usually granted when money compensation is not enough, such as in contracts involving unique goods or property. It is not automatic and depends on fairness and practicality.

**Real-life applications:**

- Used when a seller refuses to transfer a unique property.
- Helps buyers receive rare items they specifically contracted for.
- Courts apply it carefully to avoid unfair hardship.
- Businesses rely on it for high-value unique transactions.

**Key concepts:** `specific performance` `equitable remedy` `unique subject` `enforcement` `court order`

## Q18. What is “incorporation of terms” in a contract?

**Level:** Basic

Incorporation of terms means making certain conditions part of a contract, even if they are not written in the main document. Terms can be incorporated through signing, prior dealings, or clear notice (like printed terms on a ticket). For incorporation to work, the other party must have a fair chance to know the terms.

**Real-life applications:**

- Airlines incorporate baggage rules in ticket terms.
- Warehouses incorporate storage conditions in receipts.
- Businesses use standard terms for efficiency.
- Customers challenge hidden terms that were not properly disclosed.

**Key concepts:** `incorporation` `notice` `signature` `standard terms` `prior dealings`

## Q19. What is a “counter-offer”?

**Level:** Basic

A counter-offer happens when the offeree changes the terms of the original offer while responding. This rejects the original offer and creates a new offer. A contract forms only if the original offeror accepts the counter-offer.

**Real-life applications:**

- Buyers negotiate prices by counter-offering.
- Suppliers revise delivery terms to counter proposed conditions.
- Counter-offers show negotiation in business dealings.
- Written records help track what terms were finally agreed.

**Key concepts:** `counter-offer` `rejection` `negotiation` `offer` `acceptance`

**Level:** Basic

Sale of goods law deals with contracts where ownership of movable goods is transferred from seller to buyer for a price. It covers rules about quality, delivery, payment, transfer of ownership, and rights when goods are defective or payment is not made.

**Real-life applications:**

- Shops sell goods daily under sale contracts.
- Manufacturers supply products to retailers using sale agreements.
- Customers rely on quality and replacement rights.
- Businesses use these rules to manage delivery and payment disputes.

**Key concepts:** `sale of goods` `ownership` `price` `delivery` `buyer and seller rights`

## Q21. What is the difference between “sale” and “agreement to sell”?

**Level:** Basic

A sale is when ownership of goods transfers immediately from seller to buyer. An agreement to sell is when ownership will transfer in the future, such as after payment or delivery. The difference matters because it affects risk and who is treated as the owner at a given time.

**Real-life applications:**

- Buying from a store is usually an immediate sale.
- Pre-ordering goods is often an agreement to sell.
- Ownership timing decides who bears loss during transit.
- Businesses use this to plan insurance and risk management.

**Key concepts:** `sale` `agreement to sell` `ownership transfer` `risk` `future condition`

## Q22. What is meant by “condition” in a sale contract?

**Level:** Basic

A condition is an important term in a sale contract that goes to the heart of the deal. If a condition is breached, the buyer can usually reject the goods and treat the contract as ended. Conditions commonly relate to quality, description, or fitness for purpose.

**Real-life applications:**

- Buyers reject goods that fail critical quality standards.
- Retailers reject supplies that don’t match product specifications.
- Contracts define conditions for safety and performance.
- This encourages sellers to meet essential requirements.

**Key concepts:** `condition` `essential term` `breach` `rejection` `sale contract`

## Q23. What is meant by “warranty” in a sale contract?

- Companies offer warranties to build consumer trust.
- Warranty claims help resolve disputes without cancelling sales.

## Q26. What is “bailment” in business law?

**Level:** Basic

Bailment is when the owner of goods (bailor) gives possession of goods to another person (bailee) for a specific purpose, with the understanding that the goods will be returned or disposed of as directed. Ownership does not change; only possession changes.

**Real-life applications:**

- Customers leave vehicles at service centers for repair.
- Warehouses store goods for businesses under bailment.
- Delivery companies temporarily hold goods in transit.
- Law decides responsibility if goods are lost or damaged.

**Key concepts:** `bailment` `bailor` `bailee` `possession` `duty of care`

## Q27. What is a “pledge”?

**Level:** Basic

A pledge is a form of security where the borrower gives possession of goods to the lender as collateral for a loan. If the borrower fails to repay, the lender may sell the pledged goods following legal rules and recover the money.

**Real-life applications:**

- Gold loans are common examples of pledge.
- Businesses pledge inventory to obtain short-term credit.
- Banks keep pledged goods securely to reduce loan risk.
- Borrowers regain goods after full repayment.

**Key concepts:** `pledge` `collateral` `possession` `secured loan` `default`

## Q28. What is “hypothecation”?

**Level:** Basic

Hypothecation is a security arrangement where goods are offered as collateral for a loan but possession remains with the borrower. It is common in vehicle and inventory financing. If the borrower defaults, the lender can take steps to seize and sell the goods as per law and agreement.

**Real-life applications:**

- Car loans often involve hypothecation to the bank.
- Businesses hypothecate stock to get working capital loans.
- Banks register hypothecation to protect their interest.
- Default can lead to repossession following legal process.

**Key concepts:** `hypothecation` `collateral` `possession retained` `secured lending` `repossession`

## Q29. What is a “lien”?

**Level:** Basic

A lien is the legal right to keep possession of someone’s goods until a payment or obligation related to those goods is fulfilled. For example, a repairer may retain an item until repair charges are paid. It protects service providers from non-payment.

**Real-life applications:**

- Mechanics keep vehicles until service bills are cleared.
- Warehouses retain goods until storage fees are paid.
- Lien encourages customers to pay dues on time.

## Q34. What is a “contract of guarantee”?

## 1. Jargon Buster

Ten most critical terms from the basic Q&A set, defined with practitioner precision for IT/AI/PM/consulting roles.

**Contract**
A legally enforceable agreement between two or more parties that creates mutual obligations. Requires offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, free consent, and lawful object. A contract that fails any element is void or voidable. In tech: every SaaS subscription, vendor MSA, employment offer letter, and NDA is a contract.

**Offer**
A definite proposal by one party (offeror) to another (offeree), expressing willingness to enter into a contract on specific terms. Must be communicated, certain, and made with the intention of being bound. Distinguished from an "invitation to treat" (e.g., a product listing on an e-commerce site is generally an invitation, not an offer).

**Acceptance**
Unconditional and absolute agreement to all terms of the offer. Must mirror the offer exactly (mirror image rule). Counter-offers destroy the original offer. In digital contexts, clicking "I Agree" on terms of service constitutes acceptance — courts have upheld this as valid click-wrap acceptance.

**Consideration**
Something of value exchanged between parties that makes a promise legally enforceable. Can be a promise to do something, a payment, or a promise to refrain from an act. Past consideration is not valid. In SaaS: subscription fee is consideration from the customer; software access is consideration from the vendor.

**Breach**
Failure by one party to fulfill their contractual obligations without lawful excuse. Can be actual (non-performance at due date) or anticipatory (repudiation before performance is due). In tech projects: missing a delivery milestone, shipping defective software, or failing SLA uptime guarantees are common breach scenarios.

**Remedy**
Legal relief granted to the injured party upon breach. Principal remedies: damages (compensatory, liquidated, nominal, punitive), specific performance, injunction, rescission, and restitution. In commercial contracts, liquidated damages clauses pre-agree the remedy amount, which is standard in IT outsourcing and construction contracts.

**Consumer Protection**
Body of law protecting buyers of goods and services from unfair trade practices, defective products, and deficient services. In India, governed by the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. Relevant to any tech company selling directly to consumers — edtech, fintech, health-tech — through complaint forums at district, state, and national levels.

**Intellectual Property**
Legal rights protecting creations of the mind. Includes patents (inventions), copyrights (original works including software), trademarks (brand identifiers), trade secrets (confidential business information), and designs. For AI/tech companies, IP strategy is a core business function: code is copyrightable, algorithms may be patentable, brand names are trademark-protectable.

**Company Formation**
The legal process of incorporating a business entity. In India: Private Limited Company, LLP, OPC, Public Limited Company, Partnership, Sole Proprietorship are the principal forms. Each has distinct liability, compliance, and tax implications. Tech startups most commonly form Private Limited Companies for VC-compatibility and limited liability protection.

**Cyber Law**
Legal framework governing digital interactions, electronic contracts, data protection, cybercrime, and electronic evidence. In India, the IT Act 2000 (amended 2008) is the primary statute. For AI/PM practitioners: data privacy (DPDP Act 2023), electronic contracts validity, cybercrime liability, and intermediary liability rules under Section 79 are critical compliance areas.

## 2. Frameworks and Mental Models

Four meta-frameworks synthesized from the basic Q&A patterns for applied legal reasoning.

### Framework 1: Valid Contract Checklist (7 Elements)

Use this as a go/no-go gate before signing or drafting any agreement.

| # | Element | Test Question | Red Flag |
|---|---------|---------------|----------|
| 1 | Offer | Is there a definite, communicated proposal? | Vague or conditional proposals |
| 2 | Acceptance | Is acceptance unconditional and mirroring the offer? | Counter-offers or conditional acceptances |
| 3 | Consideration | Is there mutual exchange of value? | One-sided promises, past consideration |
| 4 | Capacity | Are both parties legally competent? (age, mental soundness, not disqualified) | Minor, insolvent, or adjudged incompetent party |
| 5 | Free Consent | Is consent free from coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, or mistake? | Pressure tactics, hidden material terms |
| 6 | Lawful Object | Is the purpose and consideration legal? | Contracts for illegal acts are void ab initio |
| 7 | Certainty | Are terms clear and capable of performance? | Ambiguous or incomplete terms |

Decision rule: All 7 must be satisfied. Failure of elements 1-3 = no contract formed. Failure of 4-6 = voidable contract. Failure of 7 = void for uncertainty.

### Framework 2: Law vs Ethics Matrix

Critical for IT/AI practitioners who often face situations where legal compliance and ethical behavior diverge.

|  | Legal | Illegal |
|--|-------|---------|
| **Ethical** | Ideal zone: operate here | Civil disobedience, whistleblowing — rare, high risk |
| **Unethical** | Legal but wrong: dark patterns, surveillance capitalism, discriminatory algorithms — regulators are moving here | Avoid: both legal exposure and reputational destruction |

Application: AI bias in hiring tools may be legal today but is in the "legal but unethical" quadrant. Proactive alignment with forthcoming EU AI Act / India AI framework avoids regulatory arbitrage risk. Law sets the floor; ethics sets the ceiling.

### Framework 3: Legal Risk Spectrum for Businesses

```
LOW RISK                                                    HIGH RISK
|-------|-------------|--------------|--------------|----------|
Compliant  Minor      Contract    Regulatory    Criminal
Operations  Violations  Disputes   Enforcement  Liability
           (warnings)  (civil)    (fines/bans)  (prosecution)
```

### Framework 4: Compliance vs Aspiration Framework

Two distinct obligations for businesses:

**Compliance Obligations** (Mandatory, non-negotiable):
- Contract law compliance (valid agreements, no fraud)
- Consumer protection mandates (grievance redressal, no deficiency of service)
- Data protection (DPDP Act, IT Act)
- IP non-infringement (not using others' protected IP without license)
- Company law filings (ROC compliance, annual returns)

**Aspirational Standards** (Voluntary, competitive differentiator):
- Ethical AI governance

- Environmental and social compliance beyond regulation
- Transparent algorithmic decision-making

Application: Startups often confuse aspirational with compliance. Compliance is table stakes. Aspiration is the brand. Both matter, but the consequences of failing compliance are legal; failing aspiration is reputational.

## 3. Formulas, Thresholds, and Decision Rules

### Contract Validity Test

```
Valid Contract = Offer + Acceptance + Consideration + Capacity + Free Consent + Lawful Object + Certainty
```

If any element = 0, the contract is either void (no legal effect from inception) or voidable (can be avoided by the aggrieved party).

Void contracts: agreements without consideration, agreements with unlawful object, agreements with incompetent parties (minors under Indian Contract Act).

Voidable contracts: contracts formed under coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, or mistake — aggrieved party may affirm or avoid.

### Limitation Act Periods (India — Limitation Act, 1963)

| Claim Type | Limitation Period |
|------------|-------------------|
| Simple contract (oral or written) | 3 years from breach |
| Contract under seal / specialty contract | 12 years |
| Recovery of money lent (written instrument) | 3 years |
| Tort claims (general) | 3 years |
| Patent infringement | 3 years |
| Trademark infringement | 3 years |
| Consumer complaints (Consumer Protection Act 2019) | 2 years from cause of action |

Decision rule: Always calendar the limitation period from the date of breach or cause of action. Missing it is fatal to the claim even if the substantive case is strong.

### Consumer Forum Jurisdiction Thresholds (Consumer Protection Act, 2019)

| Forum | Pecuniary Jurisdiction |
|-------|----------------------|
| District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission | Up to Rs. 50 lakhs |
| State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission | Rs. 50 lakhs to Rs. 2 crores |
| National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission | Above Rs. 2 crores |

Note: The 2019 Act raised thresholds significantly from the 1986 Act. Also introduced e-filing and online complaints, highly relevant for tech company consumer grievances.

### IP Registration Timelines (India — approximate)

| IP Type | Registration Duration | Protection Period |
|---------|-----------------------|-------------------|
| Patent | 2-5 years (examination + grant) | 20 years from filing |
| Trademark | 18-24 months (if no opposition) | 10 years, renewable |
| Copyright | Automatic on creation; registration 30-60 days | Author's life + 60 years |
| Design | 6-12 months | 10 years, extendable by 5 |

Decision rule: Copyright arises automatically — register for evidentiary benefit. Patent rights require registration — provisional filing secures priority date. File trademarks early; identical marks filed first generally prevail in India (first-to-file jurisdiction).

## 4. Do / Don't

### DO (12 Rules for IT/AI/PM/Consulting Practitioners)

1. **DO** ensure every vendor, client, and partner relationship is documented in a written contract — even for agile/sprint-based engagements. Verbal agreements exist but are nearly impossible to enforce.

2. **DO** include a clear scope-of-work definition in every agreement. Scope creep disputes are the single largest source of IT contract litigation.

3. **DO** specify liquidated damages clauses for SLA breaches in technology service agreements — define the remedy upfront rather than litigating quantum of loss later.

4. **DO** conduct an IP ownership audit before product launch. Confirm that all code, designs, and content created by employees and contractors is properly assigned to the company via written IP assignment agreements.

5. **DO** file a trademark application for your product name and logo as early as possible — ideally before launch. Priority date protection is critical.

6. **DO** include a data processing agreement (DPA) with every third-party vendor who handles personal data of your users. This is a legal requirement under DPDP Act 2023 and contractually allocates liability.

7. **DO** establish a consumer grievance redressal mechanism with a named Grievance Officer and timelines as mandated by IT Rules 2021 and Consumer Protection Act 2019. Non-compliance is a regulatory trigger.

8. **DO** verify counterparty capacity before contracting: check company registration, authorised signatories, and board resolutions for high-value agreements.

9. **DO** keep a record of all pre-contractual representations, email chains, and negotiations. These become critical in misrepresentation and fraud disputes.

10. **DO** use specific performance as the primary remedy sought when the subject matter of the contract is unique (custom software, proprietary data, domain name) — damages may be inadequate.

11. **DO** include an arbitration clause in all commercial contracts to reduce litigation timelines. Specify seat, governing law, and number of arbitrators.

12. **DO** conduct a basic legal due diligence checklist before acquiring or partnering with another tech company: check IP ownership, pending litigation, regulatory compliance status, and data privacy posture.

### DON'T (12 Rules for IT/AI/PM/Consulting Practitioners)

1. **DON'T** treat "terms of service" and "privacy policy" as boilerplate. Ensure they accurately reflect your actual data practices — misalignment is a direct DPDP Act and consumer protection liability.

2. **DON'T** allow employees or contractors to retain IP rights to work product. Absence of a written IP assignment clause defaults to the creator retaining copyright in many jurisdictions.

3. **DON'T** assume a click-wrap or shrink-wrap agreement is automatically enforceable — courts scrutinize whether users had meaningful notice and opportunity to review terms. Make key terms visible.

4. **DON'T** promise capabilities in sales pitches or proposals that the product cannot currently deliver. Written representations in proposals can create actionable misrepresentation claims.

5. **DON'T** use third-party open-source libraries without checking license compatibility. GPL-licensed code in a proprietary product triggers copyleft obligations — potentially requiring you to open-source your entire product.

6. **DON'T** ignore limitation periods. A legally valid claim becomes unenforceable if filed after the limitation window closes.

7. **DON'T** include unilateral modification clauses that allow you to change terms without notice to users — courts and regulators treat these as unfair contract terms against consumers.

8. **DON'T** use non-compete clauses without assessing state-level enforceability. In India, post-employment non-competes are generally unenforceable under Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act (restraint of trade).

9. **DON'T** sign a contract under duress or economic coercion and then attempt to enforce it — the other party may void it. Document objections contemporaneously.

10. **DON'T** conflate "agreement" with "contract" in internal communications. An agreement lacking consideration or with an unlawful object has no legal force — using the term loosely creates false security.

11. **DON'T** share confidential client data with AI tools or third-party services without reviewing contractual confidentiality obligations and data residency requirements. This can constitute breach of contract and privacy law violation simultaneously.

12. **DON'T** register a company and then ignore post-incorporation compliance: annual ROC filings, board meetings, statutory registers. Non-compliance compounds over time and becomes a material liability during due diligence for funding rounds.

## 5. Metric-Driven Scenarios with Anti-Examples

### Scenario 1: SaaS Contract Breach Recognition

**Situation:** A B2B SaaS company (HR-tech) signs a 12-month enterprise agreement with a client. The agreement stipulates 99.9% monthly uptime SLA with a liquidated damages clause of 5% monthly fee credit per 1% downtime below threshold. The platform experiences 97.1% uptime in Month 4 (2.8% below threshold).

**Metric-Driven Analysis:**
- SLA breach: 2.8% below 99.9% threshold = clear actual breach
- Liquidated damages triggered: 2.8 x 5% = 14% of monthly fee credit
- Monthly contract value: Rs. 10 lakhs → credit owed: Rs. 1.4 lakhs
- Limitation period for breach claim: 3 years from Month 4
- Notice obligation: check if contract requires formal breach notice within X days

**Correct Application:** Issue breach notice per contractual timeline, apply credit in next invoice, document root cause and remediation to defend against contract termination for material breach (typically defined as repeated or sustained SLA failure).

**Anti-Example:** The PM team verbally acknowledges the downtime but does not issue a formal written credit note, and the client does not formally invoke the clause. Six months later, the client claims cumulative breach and terminates the contract seeking damages beyond the liquidated amount. The SaaS company argues the liquidated damages clause is the exclusive remedy — court must determine whether silence waived the exclusivity argument. The legal cost of this dispute dwarfs the Rs. 1.4 lakh credit that should have been issued promptly.

**Lesson:** Breach recognition and remedy application must be contemporaneous, documented, and contractually procedurally compliant.

---

### Scenario 2: Tech Startup IP Protection Failure

**Situation:** A 3-person AI startup builds a novel NLP algorithm for regional language transcription. They launch a beta in January 2024, publish a blog post describing the approach in March 2024, and file a provisional patent in August 2024. A competitor launches a substantially similar product in November 2024.

**Metric-Driven Analysis:**
- Publication date: March 2024 — this is prior art that destroys novelty for global patent filings
- India provisional filing: August 2024 — priority date secured for India only
- PCT (international) filing: must be within 12 months of provisional = by August 2025 to claim priority
- Copyright in code: automatic from creation — but needs to be documented (commit history, authorship records)
- Trademark: product name not registered — competitor could register a similar name

**Correct Application:** File provisional patent before public disclosure. Register trademark immediately at launch. Use NDAs for all investor, partner, and early user conversations. Maintain signed IP assignment agreements with all three founders.

**Anti-Example:** The team publishes the blog to build credibility (common startup growth tactic) before filing any IP protection. This single decision eliminates patentability in most jurisdictions outside India (which has a 12-month grace period for inventor's own disclosures in some contexts, but this is narrow). They also never had founders sign IP assignment agreements — when one founder leaves acrimoniously in Month 9, they retain co-ownership of the core algorithm. The company is now uninvestable: no clean IP chain, limited patent protection, no trademark.

**Lesson:** IP protection must be the first operational step, not the last. Sequence matters: file, then publish.

### Scenario 3: Consumer Complaint Against Edtech

**Situation:** An edtech platform sells a "Job Guarantee or Full Refund" data science course for Rs. 85,000. A student completes the course but does not receive placement assistance as promised. The student files a complaint with the District Consumer Commission 14 months after course completion.

**Metric-Driven Analysis:**
- Consumer Protection Act 2019 applies: course = service; student = consumer
- District Commission jurisdiction: claim Rs. 85,000 + compensation = within Rs. 50 lakh limit — District Commission has jurisdiction
- Limitation: 2 years from cause of action. Cause of action = when placement assistance was denied/became impossible. If that was Month 3 post-completion = complaint is within limitation
- Deficiency of service: failure to provide promised placement assistance = Section 2(11) definition squarely applies
- Unfair trade practice: "Job Guarantee" claim without basis = Section 2(47) unfair trade practice

**Correct Application:** Company must defend by showing (a) all promised services were delivered per course terms, or (b) student failed to meet eligibility conditions for placement, or (c) complaint is time-barred. If unable to defend, settle with refund to avoid adverse NCDRC precedent.

**Anti-Example:** The edtech's terms of service (buried in a 47-page document) define "job guarantee" as "interview preparation support" — not actual placement. The consumer forum ignores this definitional sleight of hand because the marketing material prominently said "guaranteed job." The forum awards full refund plus Rs. 50,000 compensation plus Rs. 10,000 litigation cost. The edtech appeals to State Commission — spends Rs. 3 lakhs in legal fees defending an Rs. 85,000 complaint. The case generates media coverage that reduces conversions by an estimated 8% for the next quarter.

**Lesson:** Marketing representations are contractual. If the Terms of Service contradict the advertisement, courts will use the advertisement. Align marketing with legal — not the other way around.

## 6. Practitioner Playbook

Quick-reference guide for applying legal fundamentals in daily work as a PM, Tech Lead, or Consultant.

**Application 1: Before signing a vendor contract**
Run the 7-element Valid Contract Checklist. Specifically verify: is the counterparty's signatory authorised (board resolution or PoA for companies)? Is scope defined with enough certainty to be enforceable? Is there a dispute resolution clause?

**Application 2: When a client requests a change in scope mid-project**
Treat every scope change as a contract variation. Issue a written change request, get written acceptance, and document revised consideration (price/timeline). Verbal scope changes that are not documented become disputes about what was agreed.

**Application 3: Drafting Terms of Service for a consumer-facing product**
Ensure compliance with Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules 2020 and IT Rules 2021: display grievance officer details, complaint timelines (acknowledge within 48 hours, resolve within 1 month), return/refund policy, and pricing transparency. Non-compliance triggers regulatory action from CCPA.

**Application 4: Onboarding a new employee or contractor**
Every engagement must include: offer letter (contract), IP assignment clause, confidentiality/NDA, and (for contractors) a clear independent contractor designation with scope and deliverables. The absence of an IP assignment clause means the individual retains rights to any creative work they produce.

**Application 5: Procuring open-source software components**
Before incorporating any OSS library, check the license type: MIT/Apache 2.0 (permissive — safe for proprietary use), GPL/LGPL (copyleft — may require open-sourcing your code), AGPL (strongest copyleft — extends to network use). Maintain a software bill of materials (SBOM).

**Application 6: Launching an AI product that makes automated decisions**
Assess whether the automated decision constitutes a "significant decision" under DPDP Act 2023 or forthcoming AI regulation. Ensure explainability mechanisms exist. Obtain consent for personal data processing. Avoid training on personal data without a lawful basis.

**Application 7: Responding to a consumer complaint or legal notice**
Never ignore a legal notice. Respond within the specified timeframe. Acknowledge receipt, investigate the grievance, and offer resolution or a reasoned defense. Ignoring notices results in ex-parte orders that are difficult to challenge.

**Application 8: Protecting confidential business information**
Trade secrets have no registration mechanism — protection depends entirely on contractual (NDA) and operational (access controls, classification) measures. Every employee and contractor with access to confidential information must sign a confidentiality agreement before access is granted, not after.

**Application 9: Due diligence before a partnership or acquisition**
Minimum legal due diligence checklist: (a) company incorporation documents and cap table, (b) all material contracts (customer, vendor, employee), (c) IP ownership documentation, (d) pending litigation and regulatory proceedings, (e) data privacy compliance posture, (f) tax compliance status. Missing items are negotiating points or deal-breakers.

**Application 10: When a counterparty is in breach**
Follow this sequence: (1) document the breach with timestamps and evidence, (2) issue a formal written notice per contractual requirements, (3) allow cure period if specified, (4) assess whether breach is material (triggering termination right) or minor (triggering damages only), (5) preserve limitation period by not waiting too long to act, (6) explore negotiated settlement before litigation given cost-benefit reality of Indian courts.

## 7. Content Critique

### Assessment: Are These the Right 71 Basic Questions?

**Strengths of the Basic Q&A Set:**

The 71 questions provide a solid doctrinal foundation: contract formation elements, breach and remedies, consumer protection basics, IP introduction, company formation, employment law fundamentals, and cyber law introduction. For a general legal aspects course, this coverage is appropriate and builds necessary vocabulary.

The sequencing from contract law (Q1-Q40 approximately) through specialized areas (consumer, IP, company, employment, cyber) mirrors the logical structure of legal study: general principles before specific regulatory frameworks.

**Gaps Identified for IT/AI/PM/Consulting Practitioners:**

Gap 1 — Data Privacy Law Depth: The cyber law basics section likely covers IT Act 2000 at an introductory level but almost certainly under-covers the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, which is the most operationally relevant statute for any tech company today. Data fiduciaries, consent managers, data principals' rights, and cross-border transfer restrictions are absent from a "basics" framing that was likely written before 2023.

Gap 2 — AI-Specific Legal Frameworks: No "basic" question set currently covers AI liability, algorithmic accountability, or the emerging global regulatory landscape (EU AI Act, India's proposed AI governance framework). For AI practitioners, this is a critical gap — they are building systems without understanding the legal obligations those systems create.

Gap 3 — SaaS and Cloud Contracting: Standard contract law basics do not address the specific legal issues of SaaS agreements: subscription vs perpetual licensing, data ownership post-termination, service credits vs actual damages, limitation of liability caps (which are standard in tech contracts but may not be covered in basic contract law).

Gap 4 — Open Source Licensing: No basic Q&A set on legal aspects covers open-source license compliance despite it being the single most common IP compliance failure in tech companies. GPL vs MIT vs Apache 2.0 should be basic vocabulary for any tech practitioner.

Gap 5 — Platform and Intermediary Liability: Section 79 of IT Act (safe harbour for intermediaries), its conditions, and the due diligence requirements under IT Rules 2021 are absent. Every product manager at a platform company needs to understand when the company loses safe harbour protection.

Gap 6 — Employment Law in Gig/Remote Context: Basic employment law questions likely cover traditional employment. Gig worker classification (Swiggy, Ola driver model), remote work compliance across state jurisdictions, and moonlighting policies are current practitioner concerns not addressed.

Gap 7 — Competition Law Basics for Tech: Abuse of dominant position in digital markets, platform tipping, algorithmic collusion, and the CCI's Digital Markets study are not typically in "basic" question sets but are increasingly relevant for product strategy decisions at scale-up companies.

**Recommendation:** The 71-question set is appropriate as a legal literacy foundation but requires a supplementary "Applied Tech Legal Basics" module of at least 20-30 questions covering the gaps above, specifically tailored to IT/AI/PM practitioner contexts.

## 8. Quick-Recall Card

**Valid Contract — 7 Elements (OACCFLO)**
O — Offer (definite, communicated)
A — Acceptance (unconditional, mirrors offer)
C — Consideration (mutual, not past)
C — Capacity (competent parties)
F — Free Consent (no vitiating factors)
L — Lawful Object (legal purpose)
O — (C)ertainty (terms clear and performable)

**Breach Triggers:**
- Non-performance at due date = actual breach
- Repudiation before due date = anticipatory breach
- Partial performance of divisible obligations = partial breach

**Remedies Hierarchy (for tech contracts):**
1. Liquidated damages (if pre-agreed) — fastest
2. Compensatory damages (prove actual loss)
3. Specific performance (unique subject matter)
4. Injunction (prevent ongoing breach)
5. Rescission (undo the contract)

**Consumer Forum Quick Pick:**
- Up to 50L = District
- 50L to 2Cr = State
- Above 2Cr = National

**IP Quick Reference:**
- Copyright: automatic, life + 60 years
- Patent: register, 20 years
- Trademark: register, 10 years renewable
- Trade Secret: NDA + access control, no registration

**Limitation Periods:**
- Contract breach: 3 years
- Consumer complaint: 2 years
- Patent/TM infringement: 3 years

**Law vs Ethics Anchor:**
Legal minimum = compliance floor.
Ethical standard = business ceiling.
AI and data decisions must be evaluated at both levels.

**Daily PM Checklist:**
[ ] Scope changes documented in writing?
[ ] IP ownership assigned in all contractor agreements?
[ ] Consumer grievance mechanism active and staffed?
[ ] Open-source licenses checked for new dependencies?
[ ] Data processing agreements with all vendors?

As a PM/Consultant/AI Lead, the one question to answer with this framework is: "Does this product decision, contract, or partnership create legally enforceable obligations that we can meet, IP we clearly own, and consumer-facing commitments we can legally substantiate — or are we building on legally unstable ground?"
