# 30. Introduction to Company Law

## Overview

Company law explains how companies are created and run, and what rules protect investors and the public.

---

## Why It Matters

Companies get benefits like separate identity and easier fundraising—but must follow governance rules.


## Key Principles

- Incorporation creates a legal entity
- Limited liability
- Disclosure and compliance filings
- Board management and shareholder control


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Company** | Registered business entity |
| **Shareholder** | Owner through shares |
| **Board** | Directors managing company |


## Use Case

Choosing between partnership and private limited company.


## Scenario

> A business incorporates to raise funds and limit owner liability.


## Examples

- Company files annual returns and maintains accounts.
- Investors buy shares and get ownership rights.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Incorporation Readiness Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether a business should incorporate now or stay informal for the moment.
[verified from model knowledge, not source] This matrix extends the source's "why it matters" into a practical setup decision.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                      Governance readiness
                   Low                          High
Growth need
Low                Stay simple                  Optional; incorporate later
High               Incorporate with support      Incorporate now and formalize controls
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Growth need means fundraising, hiring, contracting, or investor entry.
Component 2: Governance readiness means whether the team can handle boards, filings, and formal approvals.
Component 3: Risk exposure means whether founders need limited liability and separate legal identity.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A consulting startup wants to hire contractors, sign enterprise MSAs, and raise external funding. If it has high growth need and low governance readiness, the matrix says incorporate now but add a compliance owner. If growth need is low and the team is still testing the market, staying simple can be acceptable for a short period.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when founders are deciding whether to register a company before signing customers or taking investment.

## 4. Formulas

[verified from model knowledge, not source] Formula: `Incorporation Priority Score = growth need + liability risk + investor demand - governance readiness`
Variables:
growth need = 0 to 3 based on hiring, fundraising, and contracting pressure
liability risk = 0 to 3 based on exposure to claims, debt, or delivery failure
investor demand = 0 to 3 based on external funding pressure
governance readiness = 0 to 3 based on the team's ability to run boards and filings
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the business should form a company now or later.
How to interpret the output:
0 to 2 -> wait and keep the structure simple
3 to 5 -> prepare incorporation and compliance support
6 or above -> incorporate now and formalize governance
Worked example with numbers: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A product company scores growth need 3, liability risk 2, investor demand 3, and governance readiness 1. The score is 7, so incorporation should happen now with formal controls.

## 5. Do vs Don't

[verified from model knowledge, not source] These controls translate company-law basics into operating choices.

| Don't | Do |
|---------|-------|
| Sign major customer contracts in a founder's personal name | Sign through the company once incorporation is complete |
| Treat "separate identity" as only a paper exercise | Keep assets, contracts, and liabilities in the entity |
| Ignore filing deadlines because the team is busy | Calendar and own every disclosure and compliance filing |
| Let ownership and management blur together | Use a board and approval process for key decisions |
| Assume limited liability covers every risk automatically | Check guarantees, fraud, and personal commitments separately |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

[verified from model knowledge, not source] The scenarios below turn company law into operational decisions.

Scenario 1: AI startup preparing for enterprise sales
Situation: An AI startup has 9 employees, is negotiating 3 enterprise contracts, and expects investor diligence within 60 days. The founders are deciding whether to incorporate before signing.
Applicable framework/metric: Incorporation Priority Score.
Analysis: growth need = 3, liability risk = 2, investor demand = 3, governance readiness = 1, so the score is 7.
Decision rule: If the score is 6 or above, incorporate now. If it is 3 to 5, prepare incorporation. If it is 0 to 2, stay simple for now.
Action: Form the company, set up a board cadence, and move all new contracts into the entity name.

Scenario 2: Consulting firm deciding on shareholder control
Situation: A consulting business has two founders, one silent investor, and a planned hiring round. The team needs a rule for who can approve new debt and client discounts.
Applicable framework/metric: Governance readiness.
Analysis: If approval rights are unclear and there are no board minutes, governance readiness is low.
Decision rule: If governance readiness is low, set control rules before growth. If medium, document the approvals. If high, formalize the board process and keep it current.
Action: Write a reserved-matters list and assign voting thresholds.

Scenario 3: Product company weighing limited liability
Situation: A hardware startup is about to ship a device with warranty exposure. The founders want to know whether personal exposure is too high to keep operating informally.
Applicable framework/metric: Liability risk.
Analysis: If warranty claims could exceed founder cash, liability risk is high.
Decision rule: If liability risk is high and growth need is also high, incorporate immediately. If liability risk is low, the team can plan a slower transition. If liability risk is moderate, get legal and finance input first.
Action: Move contracts, insurance, and purchasing into the company structure.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

[verified from model knowledge, not source] This playbook turns the source's high-level concepts into a startup operating model.

1. Choose the legal entity that matches the business stage and risk profile.
2. Complete incorporation and store the registration documents in one place.
3. Open contracts, bank accounts, and vendor relationships in the company name.
4. Define shareholder rights, board roles, and approval thresholds early.
5. Set a recurring compliance calendar for filings and disclosures.
6. Review liability exposure before signing large deals or borrowing money.
7. Revisit the structure when the business adds funding, geography, or regulated products.
