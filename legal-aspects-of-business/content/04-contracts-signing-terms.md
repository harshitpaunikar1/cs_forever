# 4. Contracts by Signing and Incorporation of Terms

## Overview

When you sign a contract, you are usually bound by its terms. Sometimes terms are included by reference, like “terms on website” or “printed on ticket.”

---

## Why It Matters

Businesses often use standard forms. Understanding incorporated terms avoids hidden surprises.


## Key Principles

- Signature generally binds you
- Terms must be reasonably brought to notice
- Unusual terms need clearer notice
- Terms must be available before agreement


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Incorporation** | Making terms part of contract |
| **Standard form** | Pre-written contract template |
| **Notice** | Informing the other party about terms |


## Use Case

Using standard customer terms printed on invoices or order forms.


## Scenario

> A courier company tries to rely on a hidden “no liability” clause not shown at booking. Customer challenges it.


## Examples

- Ticket terms on a parking slip shown at entry.
- Contract says: “Terms as per Annexure A attached.”

## 1. Topic Snapshot
This topic explains when a signed contract binds you to its terms, including terms included by reference from a website, ticket, invoice, order form, or annexure.  
It matters because procurement, SaaS, AI pilots, and consulting SOWs often hide liability and warranty language inside standard forms.  
The decision is whether the terms were clearly incorporated before agreement.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Incorporation Check
**Purpose:** Decide whether standard terms are likely enforceable.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
        Clear notice
             ^
             |
 Terms attached / linked -->  Incorporated
             |
             v
        Hidden / buried
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: signature, which shows assent.
Component 2: notice, which shows the terms were brought to attention.
Component 3: availability before agreement, which shows the terms existed before the deal closed.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm sends a signed SOW that says “terms as per Annexure A attached”; the annexure is present in the deal room and the liability cap is visible, so the clause is more likely incorporated.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when legal asks, “Were these terms actually part of the deal?”

### Hidden-Term Risk Matrix
**Purpose:** Judge the risk that a clause will be challenged as a surprise term.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
High risk: hidden clause + unusual term
Low risk: visible clause + ordinary term
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: visibility, meaning how easy the term was to spot.
Component 2: unusualness, meaning whether the clause is a surprise in context.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team accepts a SaaS order form with a buried “no liability” clause in tiny print; because the term is unusual and not pointed out, the risk of challenge is high.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when a vendor pushes a one-sided limitation buried in standard terms.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Assume a signature automatically saves a hidden clause | Make sure the clause was reasonably brought to notice before signing |
| Buried a “no liability” term in fine print | Put unusual terms in a visible place and highlight them |
| Reference “website terms” without linking or showing them | Attach the terms or make them available before agreement |
| Rely on a ticket, invoice, or order form after the fact | Show the customer the terms at the point of purchase or booking |
| Treat an annexure as optional baggage | Include the annexure and label it clearly in the signed set |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: SaaS checkout terms
Situation: A product manager wants the customer to accept a subscription agreement that links to website terms. The legal concern is whether a limitation of liability clause will hold up if the customer complains later.
Applicable framework/metric: Incorporation checklist score.
Analysis: Signature = 1, notice = 1 if the link is visible before purchase, availability before agreement = 1 if the terms are accessible before checkout. Score 3/3 means the clause is much safer to rely on.
Decision rule: If score = 3/3, proceed; if 2/3, fix notice; if 0-1/3, re-paper the deal.
Action: Put the terms on the checkout page, require an explicit acceptance step, and keep a versioned copy in the contract record.

Scenario 2: AI pilot with annexure-based limits
Situation: An AI consulting team signs a pilot SOW where Annexure A contains an indemnity carve-out. The business wants to know if the annexure can be enforced after delivery issues appear.
Applicable framework/metric: Hidden-term risk matrix.
Analysis: Visibility is high only if Annexure A is attached and named in the signature pack. Unusualness is high because indemnity carve-outs matter commercially. A high/high combination creates low challenge risk.
Decision rule: If visibility and attachment are both clear, accept; if either is weak, renegotiate before signature.
Action: Put Annexure A in the same signing packet and call it out in the main body.

Scenario 3: Consulting order form with website reference
Situation: A consulting vendor sends an order form that says “terms on website,” but the customer never saw the page before signing. Later, the vendor tries to enforce service exclusions.
Applicable framework/metric: Notice test.
Analysis: Notice is weak because the terms were not reasonably brought to the customer's attention at the right time. The safer conclusion is that incorporation is contestable.
Decision rule: If notice is explicit and pre-signature, rely on it; if not, do not assume the clause sticks.
Action: Send the website terms with the order form and store evidence of pre-signature access.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build a contract intake checklist that records signature, notice, and referenced documents for every deal.
2. Attach all website terms, annexures, and standard forms to the signing packet before approval.
3. Highlight any unusual clause, especially liability caps and no liability language, in the cover note.
4. Store a versioned PDF or link archive so the business can prove what was shown before agreement.
5. Require legal review for any term incorporated by reference rather than written in the main body.
6. Train sales and procurement teams to escalate hidden clauses before signature.
