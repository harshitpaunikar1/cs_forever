# 32. Corporate Personality

## Overview

A company is treated as a separate “person” in law—different from its owners.

---

## Why It Matters

It protects owners from personal liability and allows the company to own property and sue/be sued.


## Key Principles

- Separate entity
- Limited liability
- Continuity (exists even if owners change)
- “Lifting the corporate veil” in fraud cases


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Separate legal entity** | Company is independent |
| **Corporate veil** | Separation between company and owners |
| **Lifting the veil** | Court ignores separation in wrongdoing |


## Use Case

Company signs a lease and owns assets.


## Scenario

> Company defaults on loan; generally, shareholders aren’t personally liable beyond shares (unless fraud).


## Examples

- Company owns trademark in its own name.
- Court lifts veil when company used to cheat creditors.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Veil Risk Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether the company is being run as a real separate entity or whether the separation is at risk.
[verified from model knowledge, not source] This matrix is a practical control for avoiding veil-piercing risk.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                      Fraud / misuse signals
                   Low                          High
Formality
High               Clean separation              Strong company form; low veil risk
Low                Sloppy but repairable          High risk; fix immediately
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Formality means separate bank accounts, records, contracts, and approvals.
Component 2: Fraud or misuse signals mean sham dealings, concealment, or using the company to cheat.
Component 3: Owner overlap means whether personal and company assets are mixed in practice.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A software founder pays vendor invoices from a personal card, signs customer contracts personally, and uses company IP in a side project. That lands in low formality and higher misuse risk, so the team should clean up entity boundaries immediately.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when the company's bank account, contracts, or IP ownership look mixed with the founders' personal affairs.

## 4. Formulas

[verified from model knowledge, not source] Formula: `Veil Risk Score = commingling + fraud signals + formality gaps + undercapitalization`
Variables:
commingling = 0 to 3 based on mixing personal and company money/assets
fraud signals = 0 to 3 based on dishonesty or concealment
formality gaps = 0 to 3 based on missing records, approvals, or contracts
undercapitalization = 0 to 3 based on whether the company is too thinly funded for its commitments
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the company form is being respected or is likely to be challenged.
How to interpret the output:
0 to 3 -> low risk -> keep monitoring
4 to 7 -> medium risk -> fix entity separation now
8 or above -> high risk -> urgent legal review
Worked example with numbers: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A consulting founder has commingling 2, fraud signals 0, formality gaps 2, and undercapitalization 2. The score is 6, so the entity separation needs correction now.

## 5. Do vs Don't

[verified from model knowledge, not source] These controls make the corporate veil real in day-to-day operations.

| Don't | Do |
|---------|-------|
| Pay company bills from personal accounts | Use a dedicated company bank account |
| Sign leases or customer contracts in your own name | Sign in the company name and capacity |
| Treat company cash like founder cash | Keep assets and liabilities separate |
| Ignore board minutes and approvals | Maintain records for major decisions |
| Use the company to hide wrongdoing | Fix behavior immediately and seek legal review |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

[verified from model knowledge, not source] The scenarios below convert veil language into operating decisions.

Scenario 1: AI startup with mixed personal and company spending
Situation: A founder charges cloud bills, office rent, and personal travel to one card, then reimburses irregularly. The company is also signing enterprise contracts.
Applicable framework/metric: Veil Risk Score.
Analysis: commingling = 3, fraud signals = 0, formality gaps = 2, undercapitalization = 1, so the score is 6.
Decision rule: If the score is 8 or above, escalate urgently. If it is 4 to 7, fix entity separation now. If it is 0 to 3, keep monitoring.
Action: Open a company account, clean up reimbursements, and move all contracts into the entity name.

Scenario 2: Consulting firm using the company as a side wallet
Situation: A consulting founder pays personal taxes from company funds and books a lease in the company name but uses one office room for private work.
Applicable framework/metric: Formality and owner overlap.
Analysis: If formal separation is weak and owner overlap is high, the veil-risk matrix lands in the repairable/high-risk band.
Decision rule: If formality is high, risk is low. If formality is low and misuse signals are present, fix immediately. If fraud is detected, escalate legally.
Action: Separate personal and company expenses, update records, and review the lease and payroll setup.

Scenario 3: Product company facing creditor pressure
Situation: A hardware product company defaults on supplier invoices and the owners have been using company assets informally. The business is trying to keep trading without proper records.
Applicable framework/metric: Undercapitalization and formality gaps.
Analysis: If the company cannot meet commitments and records are weak, the score increases sharply.
Decision rule: If the score is 0 to 3, monitor. If 4 to 7, fix separation and funding. If 8 or above, stop and get counsel involved.
Action: Review solvency, document assets, and avoid any further informal transfers.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

[verified from model knowledge, not source] This playbook turns the source's theory into a control checklist.

1. Open a dedicated company bank account and stop mixing personal and company money.
2. Sign every contract in the company name with the right officer capacity.
3. Keep a clean asset register for IP, equipment, and cash.
4. Record board and shareholder approvals for major decisions.
5. Review funding levels and liabilities before taking on new obligations.
6. Check for fraud, concealment, or sham transactions whenever records look odd.
7. Run a quarterly entity-separation audit across finance, legal, and operations.
