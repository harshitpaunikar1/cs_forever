# 28. Advertising and Unfair Trade Practices

## Overview

This covers rules against misleading ads, fake discounts, and dishonest sales tricks.

---

## Why It Matters

Misleading marketing damages consumers and can lead to penalties and reputation loss.


## Key Principles

- Ads must not mislead
- Claims should be supported by proof
- Hidden charges should be disclosed
- No bait-and-switch tactics


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Misleading advertisement** | False or deceptive claim |
| **Unfair trade practice** | Dishonest sales/marketing method |
| **Bait offer** | Attractive offer not actually available |


## Use Case

E-commerce “sale” campaigns and influencer promotions.


## Scenario

> A brand claims “doctor approved” without evidence. Complaint leads to removal and penalty.


## Examples

- “Up to 90% off” but most products only 10% off.
- “Free trial” but auto-charges without clear notice.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
This topic covers misleading ads, fake discounts, hidden charges, and dishonest sales tactics.  
It matters to IT, AI, product, and consulting leaders because bad claims can trigger refunds, regulator attention, channel mistrust, and brand damage.  
The decision is whether a campaign is provable, transparent, and actually deliverable before it goes live.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Claim Substantiation Ladder
**Purpose:** Check whether a marketing claim has enough evidence behind it before launch.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
claim idea -> internal proof -> third-party proof -> legal review -> publish
      low trust ----------------------------------------------> high trust
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Claim strength - how bold or specific the statement is.
Component 2: Evidence strength - whether the claim is backed by real proof.
Component 3: Review gate - whether legal/compliance has signed off.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team wants to say its AI support bot “cuts handling time by 40%.” The team should first check logs, run a controlled pilot, and confirm the sample size before publishing the claim in a launch page or sales deck.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When marketing wants to publish a strong performance promise without a data pack.

### Offer Honesty Matrix
**Purpose:** Separate legitimate promotions from bait offers and bait-and-switch risk.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                 DELIVERY
              promised  not promised
CLAIMED  ------------------------------
truthful | safe promo | bait risk
misleading| risky      | unfair trade
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Claim truthfulness - whether the ad is accurate.
Component 2: Delivery reality - whether the promoted offer is actually available as stated.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm advertises a “free strategy assessment” but plans to force every lead into a paid workshop. That is bait-and-switch territory if the free assessment is not genuinely available.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When a promo sounds great but the delivery team says, “We cannot actually honour that at scale.”

## 4. Formulas
Formula: `Claim support ratio = supported claims / total public claims` [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Variables:
`supported claims` = claims with evidence, logs, or substantiation
`total public claims` = all externally published statements in the campaign

Why this formula exists: It answers the practical question, “How much of our marketing is actually backed by proof?”

How to interpret the output:
Value below 0.7 → too many unsupported statements → pause launch and fix proof
Value 0.7–0.9 → usable but risky → tighten review and disclosures
Value above 0.9 → strong substantiation discipline → approve with monitoring

Worked example with numbers: If a product launch page has 12 claims and 10 are backed by evidence, the ratio is 0.83. A product marketing team should still review the two weak claims before launch.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Say “doctor approved” or “industry leading” without evidence. | Keep proof for every public claim before launch. |
| Hide fees until the last step. | Disclose hidden charges upfront and early. |
| Advertise inventory or slots you do not actually have. | Tie the offer to real capacity or stock. |
| Use a free trial that auto-charges without clear notice. | Make renewal and billing terms obvious before signup. |
| Push a different offer after the buyer clicks a specific ad. | Sell the same offer you advertised, or clearly separate the new offer. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI benchmark claim
Situation: An AI SaaS company wants to say its model is “30% more accurate than competitors” in a launch deck used by sales and investors. The benchmark comes from an internal test, but the sample is tiny and the comparison set is not documented.
Applicable framework/metric: Claim Substantiation Ladder.
Analysis: The claim strength is high but the evidence strength is low. Without a documented methodology, the statement is vulnerable to challenge.
Decision rule: If the benchmark is independently reproducible, publish. If proof is partial, soften the wording. If proof is missing, remove the claim.
Action: Build a substantiation file, rewrite the claim, and add a legal sign-off step before the next campaign.

Scenario 2: Consulting lead-gen bait offer
Situation: A consulting firm advertises a “free digital transformation roadmap” for enterprise clients, but the sales team plans to replace it with a paid discovery workshop after the first call.
Applicable framework/metric: Offer Honesty Matrix.
Analysis: The offer is truthful only if the free roadmap is genuinely delivered. If the real intent is to switch the buyer into a paid offer, the campaign is deceptive.
Decision rule: If the free offer is real, launch. If it is capacity-constrained, disclose that. If it is only a hook, stop it.
Action: Align marketing, sales, and delivery on one promise and one fulfillment path.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Create a claim register for every campaign, landing page, ad, and sales deck.
2. Attach proof to every measurable statement before publication.
3. Require explicit disclosure of fees, exclusions, and billing terms.
4. Review promotional inventory, availability, and delivery capacity before launch.
5. Train sales and marketing teams on bait-and-switch red flags.
6. Put a legal/compliance checkpoint in the launch workflow for high-risk claims.
7. Monitor complaints, refund requests, and ad takedowns after launch.
