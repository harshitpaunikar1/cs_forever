# 8. Consideration

## Overview

Consideration is “something in return” for a promise—like money for goods, or service for payment.

---

## Why It Matters

Without consideration, many agreements are not enforceable as contracts (with some exceptions).


## Key Principles

- Must have value (money, service, promise)
- Can be past/present/future (as allowed by law)
- Must be lawful
- Not necessarily adequate, but must be real


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Consideration** | Exchange of value |
| **Gratuitous promise** | Promise without exchange (often not enforceable) |


## Use Case

Discount deal: buyer pays advance; seller promises delivery.


## Scenario

> A friend promises to gift a laptop but changes mind. Without consideration, it’s hard to enforce as a contract.


## Examples

- Paying ₹10,000 as advance for furniture.
- Consultant provides service; company pays fee.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Consideration is the “something in return” that makes a promise look like a real contract rather than a free favor.
For IT, AI, product, and consulting teams, the key decision is whether the exchange has real value and is lawful enough to support enforcement.
The practical result is knowing when a deal is contract-grade and when it is only a gratuitous promise.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Exchange Value Test
**Purpose:** Check whether a promise is backed by a real exchange.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Promise -> Value returned -> Lawful? -> Real exchange -> Enforceable deal
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1, Promise: what one side says it will do.
Component 2, Value returned: the money, service, or promise going back the other way.
Component 3, Lawful?: the exchange must be allowed by law.
Component 4, Real exchange: the consideration must be genuine, not a sham.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm agrees to design a cloud migration roadmap in exchange for a fixed fee. The fee is the consideration for the service, and the service is the consideration for the fee. That makes the deal contract-grade, not just a polite commitment.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when someone asks whether a “handshake deal” or informal promise is actually enforceable.

### Consideration Strength Matrix
**Purpose:** Separate strong exchanges from weak or gift-like promises.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    Realness of exchange
                  Low                 High
Lawful  +----------------+----------------+
No      | Risky / invalid | Not usable    |
Yes     | Gratuitous      | Strong deal    |
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Lawful: the exchange must be allowed.
Realness of exchange: the value must exist in practice.
Gratuitous promise: lawful maybe, but no exchange, so usually not enforceable.
Strong deal: lawful and real, which is the target state.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI vendor promises a free feature upgrade “because we like the client” with no fee, service, or other exchange. That looks like a gratuitous promise. If the vendor wants enforceability, it needs a real exchange or a different contract structure.
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when legal or finance wants to know whether the deal is a gift, a discount, or an enforceable bargain.

## 4. Formulas

No explicit formula appears in the source. One practical operating metric [verified from model knowledge, not source] is:

Formula: Real consideration coverage = contracts with real exchange / agreements reviewed

Variables:
Contracts with real exchange = agreements where both sides give something of value.
Agreements reviewed = all deals the team checks for enforceability.

Why this formula exists: It answers how many deals are structured as true bargains rather than casual promises.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 70% → too many informal commitments → tighten contract intake.
Value 70%–95% → normal operating range → keep reviewing edge cases.
Value > 95% → strong discipline → verify you are not over-lawyering simple changes.
Worked example with numbers: 48 of 60 reviewed agreements had a clear exchange of value. Real consideration coverage = 48 / 60 = 80%. The team should keep the review gate but standardize how exchanges are documented.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat a free promise like a binding bargain. | Check that there is something in return. |
| Ignore whether the consideration is lawful. | Verify the exchange is allowed by policy and law. |
| Assume the price must be perfectly equal. | Require the exchange to be real, not necessarily equal. |
| Rely on past goodwill as if it were fresh consideration. | Confirm the timing and source of the exchange. |
| Call a gift an enforceable contract without documentation. | Label gratuitous promises correctly. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: SaaS renewal discount
Situation: A SaaS company offers a renewal discount if the customer commits to a longer term and earlier payment. The commercial team wants to know if the new promise is backed by enough value to be contract-grade.
Applicable framework/metric: Exchange Value Test; real consideration coverage.
Analysis: The customer gives earlier payment and commitment length; the vendor gives a discount and continued service. That is a real exchange, not a gratuitous promise.
Decision rule: If both sides give value, treat it as enforceable consideration. If one side gives nothing back, treat it as a gift-like promise. If the exchange is unclear, re-paper it.
Action: Put the discount and commitment term in the order form and have legal confirm the wording.

Scenario 2: AI pilot extension
Situation: An enterprise AI vendor offers an extra month of pilot support at no charge because the client is “important.” The product leader wants to know whether that promise is binding.
Applicable framework/metric: Consideration Strength Matrix.
Analysis: If the support extension has no return value, it is closer to a gratuitous promise than a bargain. The team should not assume enforceability unless the extension is tied to a new exchange.
Decision rule: If there is no value in return, treat it as non-binding. If there is value in return, document it. If the deal is unclear, consult legal before promising delivery.
Action: Decide whether the extension is a courtesy or a priced commercial amendment.

Scenario 3: Consulting milestone payment
Situation: A consulting engagement pays ₹10,000 in advance for a clearly defined workshop deliverable. The consulting manager needs to decide whether the agreement has consideration.
Applicable framework/metric: Exchange Value Test; lawful and real exchange.
Analysis: The client pays money, the consultant provides the workshop, and the exchange is lawful. That is real consideration.
Decision rule: If the payment and service are both real and lawful, proceed. If one side is missing, fix the contract. If it is a sham exchange, do not rely on it.
Action: Tie the milestone payment to the workshop scope and preserve the acceptance record.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Identify what each side gives and receives before you call the deal a contract.
2. Flag any promise with no return value as potentially gratuitous.
3. Confirm the exchange is lawful before routing for approval.
4. Document whether the exchange is present, past, or future where relevant.
5. Avoid describing a gift, courtesy, or goodwill gesture as enforceable consideration.
6. Put the exchange terms in the commercial document, not only in email.
7. Review edge cases with legal when the value is indirect or non-cash.
