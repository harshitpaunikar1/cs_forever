# 26. Partnership

## Overview

Partnership is when two or more people run a business together and share profits (and usually risks).

---

## Why It Matters

Many small businesses operate as partnerships. Partners can be personally liable for business debts.


## Key Principles

- Agreement between partners
- Profit-sharing
- Mutual agency (partner can bind firm)
- Rights/duties depend on partnership deed
- Dissolution rules apply when ending


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Partnership deed** | Written rules of partnership |
| **Mutual agency** | Each partner acts for firm |
| **Dissolution** | Ending partnership |


## Use Case

Two founders run a trading firm and share profits 60:40.


## Scenario

> One partner takes a loan for the firm’s business. The firm and partners may be liable.


## Examples

- Law firm partnership among advocates.
- Family-run shop with partners sharing profits.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Partnership is a shared-business structure where two or more people run a business together and split profits, usually with shared risk.  
It matters for IT, AI, product, and consulting founders because one partner’s act can bind the firm and expose everyone to liability.  
The key decision is whether the deed, authority, and exit rules are tight enough to prevent a small disagreement from becoming a personal loss.

```text
   \      |       /

```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Agreement between partners - establishes the partnership in the first place.
Component 2: Partnership deed - defines rights, duties, profit-sharing, authority, and dissolution.
Component 3: Mutual agency - means one partner’s act can create obligations for the firm.
Component 4: Dissolution rules - how the partnership ends and liabilities are settled.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: Two product founders launch an AI workflow startup. The deed says one partner can sign customer contracts up to a fixed value, profits are split 60:40, and dissolution triggers if either partner leaves or breaches the non-compete clause. That structure avoids a surprise binding contract or an unmanaged exit.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When co-founders say, “We trust each other, so we do not need a detailed agreement.”

### Mutual Agency Risk Map
**Purpose:** Show where one partner can create firm-wide risk through a single act.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
LOW RISK  -------------------------------  HIGH RISK
Minor admin tasks                         Loan, contract, hiring
Routine approvals                         Customer commitment
Internal discussion                       External binding action
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Low-risk actions - internal coordination and routine admin.
Component 2: High-risk actions - borrowing, signing, and binding commitments.
Component 3: Control point - whether the deed authorizes the partner to act.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting partner promises a client a fixed delivery timeline without checking the other partners. Under mutual agency, the firm may still be bound, so the deed must define which promises require prior approval.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When one partner wants “full freedom” to sign or borrow on behalf of the firm.

## 4. Formulas
Formula: `Partner profit share = total distributable profit × agreed ratio`

Variables:
`total distributable profit` = profits available after business costs  
`agreed ratio` = the split set in the partnership deed, such as 60:40

Why this formula exists: It answers the basic accounting question, “How do we divide the money?”

How to interpret the output:
Value follows the deed → distribution is straightforward → pay according to agreement
Value is disputed because the ratio is unclear → pause distribution → amend the deed
Value is negative or zero → no distributable profit → do not force a payout

Worked example with numbers: If a consulting partnership has Rs 10,00,000 distributable profit and the deed sets a 60:40 split, Partner A gets Rs 6,00,000 and Partner B gets Rs 4,00,000.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Assume a handshake is enough for a multi-year partnership. | Put the agreement and deed in writing before work starts. |
| Let any partner borrow money without limits. | Define who can bind the firm and under what approval rules. |
| Ignore profit-sharing details until year end. | Fix the split ratio and distribution timing in the deed. |
| Treat exits as “we will sort it out later.” | Write dissolution rules and buyout logic up front. |
| Forget that a partner’s act can bind everyone. | Train partners on mutual agency and external commitment authority. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI startup loan exposure
Situation: Two founders run an AI testing startup. One partner takes a bank loan for “business expansion” without telling the other, then the business misses revenue targets.
Applicable framework/metric: Mutual Agency Risk Map.
Analysis: Because a partner can bind the firm, the debt may attach to the partnership and create personal exposure. The issue is not just the loan size; it is whether the deed authorized borrowing.
Decision rule: If borrowing authority is written and approved, accept the obligation. If authority is partial, review the deal immediately. If the partner acted outside authority, pursue internal remedy and tighten controls.
Action: Add borrowing limits, dual-signature rules, and a debt-approval clause to the deed.

Scenario 2: Consulting profit split dispute
Situation: A consulting partnership wins a six-month transformation project. One partner handled sales and another handled delivery, but the deed never defined the split.
Applicable framework/metric: Partner profit share formula.
Analysis: With no agreed ratio, profit distribution is negotiable rather than automatic. That creates avoidable conflict after the project closes.
Decision rule: If the deed fixes the ratio, distribute accordingly. If the ratio is absent, pause payout and renegotiate. If the business is not yet profitable, do not create a fake distribution.
Action: Document a profit split, reserve policy, and milestone-based settlement process.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Draft a partnership deed before the business starts collecting revenue.
2. Define each partner’s authority to sign, borrow, hire, and contract.
3. Write the profit-sharing ratio and distribution timing in the deed.
4. Add a dissolution clause covering exit, winding up, and debt settlement.
5. Set dual-control rules for high-risk commitments such as loans and major contracts.
6. Review the deed after any major business change, such as new funding or a partner exit.
