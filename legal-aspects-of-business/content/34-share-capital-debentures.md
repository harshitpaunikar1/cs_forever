# 34. Share Capital, Debentures, Prospective Shares

## Overview

Share capital is money raised by issuing shares. Debentures are borrowed money instruments. “Prospective shares” refers to shares offered to investors (like public issue concepts).

---

## Why It Matters

Funding decisions affect control, risk, and legal compliance.


## Key Principles

- Shares = ownership
- Debentures = debt (repayment obligation)
- Rules for issuing and allotment
- Investor protection and disclosures


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Equity share** | Ownership share |
| **Preference share** | Priority dividend/repayment |
| **Debenture** | Debt instrument |
| **Allotment** | Issuing shares to applicants |


## Use Case

Company raises money through equity funding vs debt.


## Scenario

> Company issues debentures to fund expansion; later repays with interest.


## Examples

- Startup issues shares to angel investors.
- Company issues debentures for plant expansion.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Share capital is how a company raises money by issuing shares, while debentures are borrowed money instruments. This topic matters when a founder, CFO, or consultant has to choose between giving up ownership and taking on repayment obligations.
The decision affects control, risk, investor protection, and legal compliance around issuing and allotment.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Equity vs Debenture Funding Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether the company should raise money by selling ownership or by borrowing.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                 Higher control retained
                        ^
                        |
      Debentures        |         Equity shares
 (fixed repayment,      |      (ownership dilution,
  no ownership loss)    |       no repayment promise)
                        |
------------------------+------------------------> Lower control retained
        Lower cash strain               Higher investor rights
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Control retained, which matters for founders and boards.
Component 2: Repayment burden, which matters for cash flow and default risk.
Component 3: Investor rights, which matter when choosing between debt and equity.
Component 4: Compliance load, which matters for disclosures and allotment.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI SaaS company needs capital for GPU spend. If it raises equity, it dilutes founders but avoids fixed monthly repayments. If it issues debentures, it keeps ownership tighter but must service interest from operating cash. A consulting partner would often recommend equity early if cash flow is volatile, and debentures later when recurring revenue is stable. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when a financing choice is being framed as "cheaper capital" but the real question is control plus repayment risk.

### Share Issue Readiness Matrix
**Purpose:** Check whether a share issue is ready to launch and allot legally.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                High readiness
                     ^
                     |
  Clean disclosures   |   Clean disclosures + allotment process
  but weak process    |   = go forward
                     |
---------------------+----------------------------> Low readiness
   Weak disclosures    |   Weak disclosures + messy allotment
                        |   = pause and fix
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Disclosure quality, which protects investors.
Component 2: Allotment process clarity, which prevents disputes.
Component 3: Subscription visibility, which shows whether the offer is supported.
Component 4: Compliance evidence, which helps defend the issue later.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product company planning a new feature-platform launch wants to issue equity to strategic angels. Before launch, the CFO checks whether the use of proceeds, valuation logic, and allotment steps are documented. If those are weak, the deal is delayed until the legal pack is clean. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it right before signing off a fundraising or allotment process.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: Ownership percentage = shares held / total shares issued
Variables:
shares held = number of shares owned by the investor
total shares issued = total outstanding shares after the issue
Why this formula exists: It answers how much control and economic exposure each investor actually has.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 10% -> minority position -> usually limited control
Value 10-25% -> meaningful stake -> negotiate information rights
Value > 25% -> strong stake -> likely material influence
Worked example with numbers: If a founder holds 300,000 shares out of 1,000,000 total issued shares, ownership is 30%. That is enough to matter in governance discussions, even after bringing in new capital. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: Debt service burden = interest payment + principal repayment due
Variables:
interest payment = periodic coupon or loan interest
principal repayment due = scheduled return of borrowed amount
Why this formula exists: It shows the minimum cash the company must generate to stay current on debentures.
How to interpret the output:
Value < available operating cash -> manageable -> debt may fit
Value close to operating cash -> tight -> negotiate maturity or lower coupon
Value > operating cash -> risky -> avoid or restructure
Worked example with numbers: If a debenture requires 80,000 in annual interest and 500,000 principal due at maturity, the company must plan for both the yearly servicing and the balloon repayment. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Use shares and debentures as if they are interchangeable | Separate ownership capital from borrowed capital before deciding |
| Treat issuing shares as a pure cash decision | Check dilution, control, and investor rights first |
| Launch a share issue without clean disclosures | Prepare offer materials and legal review before allotment |
| Assume debentures are "free money" | Model repayment obligations and cash-flow stress first |
| Fund long-term expansion with a structure the business cannot service | Match the instrument to the time profile of the project |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI startup choosing seed equity
Situation: An AI tooling startup needs capital for model training, but revenue is still experimental. The founders can either take a small equity round or issue debt-like instruments.
Applicable framework/metric: Equity vs Debenture Funding Matrix; ownership percentage.
Analysis: If founders currently hold 100% and sell 20% equity, they retain 80% but give up control room. If they instead borrow, ownership stays intact but cash must cover repayments. A consulting recommendation would usually favor equity when revenue volatility is high. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If operating cash is not predictable, do equity; if cash coverage is stable, consider debt; if neither is true, delay the raise.
Action: Build a financing memo showing dilution, runway, and repayment stress under both choices.

Scenario 2: Product company financing cloud infrastructure
Situation: A product team wants to scale a B2B platform and needs money for cloud compute and security compliance. The board is considering debentures instead of a new equity round.
Applicable framework/metric: Debt service burden.
Analysis: If annual interest and principal due exceed the company’s conservative free cash flow, debt will create pressure even if it protects ownership. The right lens is whether expansion cash generation can cover the fixed obligation. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If debt service is below conservative free cash flow, debt is viable; if it is near break-even, renegotiate; if it exceeds free cash flow, reject or reduce the raise.
Action: Create a 12-month cash bridge and test the effect of slower revenue growth.

Scenario 3: Consulting firm funding branch expansion
Situation: A consulting firm wants to open a new delivery center. Leadership needs to decide whether to issue shares to a strategic partner or borrow through debentures.
Applicable framework/metric: Share Issue Readiness Matrix.
Analysis: If disclosures, allotment steps, and use-of-funds logic are all documented, a share issue is execution-ready. If the documentation is weak, the firm should not rush the issue just to hit a launch date. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If readiness is high, launch; if mixed, fix process; if low, postpone.
Action: Assign legal, finance, and operations owners to produce a launch checklist.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Map the funding need to the business use case: working capital, product build, AI compute, or plant expansion.
2. Quantify the capital gap and split it into one-time and recurring cash requirements.
3. Compare equity and debenture options on dilution, repayment, and compliance burden.
4. Draft the disclosure pack and issue/allotment checklist before any investor outreach.
5. Stress-test repayment under a downside case if debentures are being considered.
6. Review ownership concentration after the issue and document control implications.
7. Get legal sign-off on investor protection language and records of allotment.
