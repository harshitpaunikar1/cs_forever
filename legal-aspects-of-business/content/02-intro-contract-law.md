# 2. Introduction to Contract Law

## Overview

Contract law is about agreements that the law will enforce. If people promise something with legal rules followed, it becomes a contract.

---

## Why It Matters

Almost every business activity—sales, rent, hiring, supply—depends on contracts.


## Key Principles

- Agreement + legal enforceability = contract
- Free consent is required
- Lawful object and consideration are required
- Breach leads to remedies


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Contract** | Enforceable agreement |
| **Agreement** | Offer + acceptance |
| **Enforceable** | Can be upheld by law |


## Use Case

Drafting a service agreement with timelines and payment terms.


## Scenario

> A designer and company agree on deliverables in writing, so payment disputes are reduced.


## Examples

- Employment contract between company and employee.
- Supply contract with delivery schedule.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Contract law turns business promises into enforceable obligations.
For an IT, AI, Product, or Consulting leader, it answers a simple question: do we have a deal that can survive delivery delays, payment disputes, or scope changes?
The source reduces contract law to four checkpoints: agreement, consent, lawful purpose, and remedies for breach.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Contract Lifecycle Stack
**Purpose:** Decide whether a deal is ready to sign, needs edits, or should be walked away from.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
1. Offer
   -> Is the proposal specific?
2. Acceptance
   -> Did both sides agree to the same terms?
3. Consent
   -> Was the agreement free and voluntary?
4. Legality
   -> Is the object lawful?
5. Performance
   -> Are deliverables, payment, and timelines clear?
6. Remedy
   -> What happens if someone breaches?
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Offer and acceptance create the deal.
Free consent and lawful object determine whether the deal is valid.
Performance terms and remedies determine whether the deal is operationally usable.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team signs a SaaS services agreement with a consulting vendor. The stack forces the team to check scope, acceptance criteria, payment terms, and breach remedies before signature.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a deal is moving fast and the team is assuming "we can fix it later."

## 4. Formulas

Formula: Contract completeness score = present critical clauses / required critical clauses [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
present critical clauses = key terms already written into the draft
required critical clauses = the set needed for the deal to work
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the draft is safe enough to sign or still too vague.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.70 -> weak draft -> do not sign
Value 0.70-0.90 -> workable draft -> close the gaps
Value > 0.90 -> strong draft -> sign and monitor
Worked example with numbers: A consulting MSA has 9 of 10 critical clauses in place. The score is 0.90. That is nearly complete, but the missing clause should be fixed before signature.

Formula: Breach risk score = dependency on the counterparty x ambiguity x consequence [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Variables:
dependency on the counterparty = how much your business relies on the deal
ambiguity = how unclear the obligations are
consequence = how painful a failure would be
Why this formula exists: It helps prioritize contract negotiations and post-signature monitoring.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.25 -> low risk -> standard monitoring
Value 0.25-0.50 -> medium risk -> tighten the contract
Value > 0.50 -> high risk -> escalate and renegotiate
Worked example with numbers: A product company depends heavily on one supplier, the scope is vague, and a failure would halt launch. The score comes out to 0.56, so the deal needs stronger clauses and a fallback supplier.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Assume a verbal promise is enough for a high-value deal. | Put key terms in writing and confirm acceptance. |
| Sign before checking consent, legality, and consideration. | Verify the deal is voluntary, lawful, and supported by value exchange. |
| Leave timelines and payment terms vague. | Make milestones, invoices, and due dates explicit. |
| Treat breach as a surprise. | Write remedies, escalation steps, and exit rights into the contract. |
| Use one generic template for every deal. | Tailor the contract to the service, supply, or employment relationship. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

Scenario 1: AI services deal with a consulting vendor
Situation: An AI team hires a consulting firm to build evaluation dashboards and wants a fast signature. The vendor's draft is short on timelines and breach remedies.
Applicable framework/metric: Contract completeness score.
Analysis: The draft has 8 of 10 critical clauses, so the score is 0.80. That is workable but not strong enough for a launch-critical engagement.
Decision rule: If the score is below 0.70, do not sign. If it is 0.70-0.90, fix the missing clauses. If above 0.90, sign and track performance.
Action: Add milestone dates, acceptance criteria, and a termination clause before work starts.

Scenario 2: Product team managing a supply contract
Situation: A hardware product manager is negotiating a supply contract for a key component. One supplier wants to keep delivery dates flexible, but the launch date is fixed.
Applicable framework/metric: Breach risk score.
Analysis: Dependency is 0.8, ambiguity is 0.6, and consequence is 0.9 because the launch depends on the part. The score is 0.432.
Decision rule: If the score is below 0.25, standard contract terms are fine. If 0.25-0.50, tighten the wording. If above 0.50, renegotiate aggressively.
Action: Add a delivery schedule, a penalty for late shipment, and a backup supplier clause.

Scenario 3: Consulting leader reviewing an employment contract
Situation: A consulting firm is hiring a senior analyst to support an enterprise client rollout. The draft employment contract is missing confidentiality and timeline language.
Applicable framework/metric: Contract completeness score.
Analysis: The document has 9 of 10 required clauses, so the score is 0.90. That is strong, but the missing confidentiality term could become a dispute point later.
Decision rule: If completeness is above 0.90, sign only after closing the final gap. If below 0.90, keep editing.
Action: Add confidentiality, deliverable ownership, and notice-period language before onboarding.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Standardize an offer-and-acceptance checklist for every deal.
2. Require free-consent and legality checks before signature.
3. Turn scope, payment terms, and timelines into a redlined contract template.
4. Add breach remedies, termination rights, and escalation steps to every commercial agreement.
5. Store signed contracts in one repository with renewal reminders.
6. Review supply and service contracts before any launch or renewal.
7. Escalate high-dependency deals to legal and finance together.
