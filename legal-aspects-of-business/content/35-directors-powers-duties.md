# 35. Directors: Appointment, Powers, Duties

## Overview

Directors run the company and must act honestly and carefully for the company’s benefit.

---

## Why It Matters

Bad directors can cause fraud, losses, and legal penalties. Governance protects shareholders and stakeholders.


## Key Principles

- Duty of care and loyalty
- Avoid conflict of interest
- Act in company’s best interest
- Proper disclosure and compliance


## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Director** | Company manager |
| **Fiduciary duty** | Trust-based duty |
| **Conflict of interest** | Personal interest clashes with company interest |


## Use Case

Board approves related-party transactions with proper disclosure.


## Scenario

> A director awards a contract to their own firm secretly. This breach can cause removal and legal action.


## Examples

- Director discloses interest before voting on a deal.
- Board approves annual budget and compliance plan.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Directors run the company, so their decisions shape performance, governance, and legal exposure. The core question is whether the board is using its powers for the company’s benefit or for a hidden personal interest.
This topic helps a management team decide how to appoint, monitor, and challenge directors before a conflict turns into fraud, losses, or penalties.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Duty-Conflict Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether a director action is safe, risky, or unacceptable.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    High disclosure
                         ^
                         |
      Safe zone          |          Monitored zone
  (company interest,     |      (some overlap, but open
   no conflict)          |       and managed)
-------------------------+--------------------------> Low disclosure
      Unacceptable zone  |          Red-flag zone
  (secret self-interest) |      (conflict hidden)
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Company-interest alignment, which checks whether the decision helps the firm.
Component 2: Disclosure quality, which checks whether conflicts are openly declared.
Component 3: Decision integrity, which checks whether the director can vote fairly.
Component 4: Enforcement risk, which rises when disclosure is missing.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A platform company is choosing a vendor for an internal AI rollout, and one director owns the vendor through a side entity. If that interest is disclosed and the director steps out of the vote, the transaction can be reviewed. If it is hidden, the situation moves into the red-flag zone and may trigger removal or legal action. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when any board member has a personal, family, or business link to the decision being approved.

### Board Accountability Matrix
**Purpose:** Assess whether the board has enough structure to supervise management properly.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                 Strong oversight
                       ^
                       |
     Clear roles       |      Clear roles + routine reporting
     but weak follow-up|      = healthy board
-----------------------+----------------------------> Weak oversight
   unclear roles       |      unclear roles + weak reporting
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Role clarity, which tells who approves what.
Component 2: Reporting discipline, which tells whether management is transparent.
Component 3: Escalation speed, which tells how fast issues reach the board.
Component 4: Compliance coverage, which tells whether governance topics are reviewed.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting firm’s board gets monthly metrics on utilization, project overruns, and legal exceptions. When the board receives clean reporting and asks hard questions, it can detect margin leakage early. If reporting is delayed, the board loses the chance to correct the problem before losses accumulate. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
When to pull this out in a meeting: Use it when the board is mostly ceremonial and management is acting without challenge.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: Board independence ratio = independent directors / total directors
Variables:
independent directors = directors without management or conflict ties
total directors = all directors on the board
Why this formula exists: It helps estimate how much unbiased oversight the board can exert.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.33 -> weak independence -> improve oversight
Value 0.33-0.50 -> moderate independence -> monitor conflicts closely
Value > 0.50 -> stronger independence -> better governance signal
Worked example with numbers: If a board has 3 independent directors out of 7 total, the ratio is 0.43. That supports oversight but does not eliminate the need for conflict controls. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

Formula: Conflict exposure score = undisclosed related-party transactions / total related-party transactions
Variables:
undisclosed related-party transactions = conflicts not properly declared
total related-party transactions = all connected-party deals
Why this formula exists: It shows how much of the board’s connected-deal activity is hidden.
How to interpret the output:
Value = 0 -> clean disclosure -> proceed with controls
Value 0-0.10 -> small leakage -> tighten process
Value > 0.10 -> serious governance risk -> investigate immediately
Worked example with numbers: If 1 of 12 related-party transactions was not disclosed, the exposure score is 0.083. That is a warning sign, even if no loss has yet been proven. [verified from model knowledge, not source]

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Let directors vote on deals tied to their personal interests | Require disclosure and recusal before the vote |
| Treat board approval as a rubber stamp | Use the board to challenge, test, and document decisions |
| Ignore minor conflicts because they seem harmless | Log every conflict and judge whether it affects judgment |
| Assume compliance is only for the legal team | Make directors and management jointly accountable |
| Delay action after a breach is discovered | Escalate quickly and decide on removal, correction, or remediation |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI vendor selection with a hidden link
Situation: An AI company is about to award a model-inference contract to a vendor. One director has an indirect ownership stake in that vendor, but the connection is not obvious in the meeting packet.
Applicable framework/metric: Duty-Conflict Matrix; conflict exposure score.
Analysis: If the relationship is undisclosed, the score moves above zero and governance risk rises immediately. If the director discloses the interest and exits the discussion, the board can still make a defensible decision. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If the conflict is disclosed, manage it; if it is hidden, investigate; if it materially affects judgment, stop the deal.
Action: Freeze the vote until the relationship is disclosed and logged.

Scenario 2: Product roadmap approval under weak oversight
Situation: A product company’s board is reviewing a roadmap that includes a major pricing change and a new enterprise feature. Management provides only high-level summaries and no downside analysis.
Applicable framework/metric: Board Accountability Matrix; reporting cadence.
Analysis: If reporting is thin, the board cannot test assumptions or spot risk early. A board that receives monthly KPI packs, exception logs, and legal updates can challenge decisions before they become costly. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If reporting is routine and complete, approve with conditions; if incomplete, defer; if absent, reject.
Action: Install a board pack template with finance, legal, and risk sections.

Scenario 3: Consulting firm director breach
Situation: A consulting firm discovers that a director secretly awarded a contract to their own firm. The issue has already caused reputational damage with clients.
Applicable framework/metric: Duty-Conflict Matrix; removal trigger.
Analysis: Once the conflict is proven and disclosure was absent, continued service by that director is usually untenable. The company should assess removal, restitution, and any client remediation. [verified from model knowledge, not source]
Decision rule: If the breach is accidental but disclosed early, remediate; if hidden and material, remove; if criminal or fraudulent, escalate legally.
Action: Convene the board, document the breach, and assign outside counsel.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Define board appointment criteria, including skill mix, independence, and integrity checks.
2. Require a conflict-of-interest declaration before onboarding any director.
3. Build a board calendar with approvals, reviews, and compliance checkpoints.
4. Standardize a board pack that includes financials, related-party deals, and legal exceptions.
5. Create a recusal rule for directors with any personal link to a transaction.
6. Set escalation thresholds for breach, fraud, or repeated non-disclosure.
7. Document removal and remediation steps so the response is fast when governance fails.
