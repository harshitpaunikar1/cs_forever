# Hostile Bids and Cross-Border M&A

## Overview

A hostile bid is when a company tries to buy another against the wishes of its board. Cross-border M&A is when the buyer and the target are in different countries, which adds legal, currency, and cultural complexity.

---

## Why It Matters

Hostile deals can move markets, trigger defensive moves, and draw regulators. Cross-border deals open big opportunities but can stumble on approvals, tax rules, and country risks, so both must be planned with extra care.

## Key Principles

- Treat hostile bids as a public campaign, not a private negotiation
- Expect takeover defenses: poison pills, white knights, share buybacks
- For cross-border deals, study local approvals and foreign investment limits
- Hedge currency risk between signing and closing
- Build a local partner or advisor team early

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Hostile Bid** | Offer made directly to shareholders without board support |
| **Poison Pill** | Defense that makes a hostile takeover very expensive |
| **White Knight** | Friendly third-party buyer who rescues the target |
| **CFIUS / FDI Review** | Government review of foreign investments for security |
| **Tender Offer** | Public offer to buy shares at a set price |

## Use Case

An overseas chemicals firm wants to enter India. It identifies a listed Indian target, checks FDI caps and SEBI rules, and makes a public tender offer when the board refuses a friendly deal.

## Scenario

> A European food group launched a hostile bid for a UK rival. The UK board found a friendly Japanese buyer as a white knight, who offered a higher price. The Japanese firm won the deal after currency hedging and six months of antitrust review.

## Examples

- A US tech firm's hostile bid fails after the target adopts a poison pill and finds a white knight.
- An Indian pharma buys a German speciality drug maker after clearing EU merger control.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
A **hostile bid** is a public acquisition attempt made directly to target shareholders after the target's board refuses; a **cross-border deal** adds a second legal regime, currency, and culture to every decision. For IT/AI/Product/Consulting leaders, these deals dominate when a tech target sits in a regulated sector (CFIUS-sensitive AI, defence cloud, health data), when a services firm rolls up overseas boutiques, or when your own cap table attracts an unsolicited bidder. The decision this framework informs: **should we pursue (or defend) this deal publicly, which jurisdictions gate the outcome, and how do we price FX, antitrust, and political risk into our walk-away number?**

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### 3.1 Hostile Deal Playbook
**Purpose:** Sequence the moves from private approach to closed deal or failure.

```
 [1] Stake-Build (<5%)  -->  [2] Bear-Hug Letter (private to board)
         |                          |
         v                          v
 [3] Public Tender Offer  -->  [4] Proxy Contest (replace directors)
         |                          |
         v                          v
 [5] Target Defences: Pill / White Knight / Crown Jewel / Staggered Board
         |
         v
 [6] Negotiation / Sweetened Bid  -->  [7] Clearance & Close  OR  [Withdraw]
```

**Components:** stake-building within SEBI/HSR/13D limits; bear-hug price anchor; proxy timeline vs. staggered-board calendar; defence countermoves; antitrust gating.
**IT/AI/Consulting example:** A PE-backed AI analytics firm sends a bear-hug to a listed Indian SaaS target at 35% premium; on refusal, launches SEBI open offer at formula price + triggers independent directors' committee.
**Trigger:** Board rejects friendly approach OR activist stake >5% requests sale.

### 3.2 Defence Toolkit Matrix

| Defence | Precondition | Cost/Effort | Legality | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Poison Pill | Charter allows; board can adopt quickly (US) | Low cost; dilutive if triggered | Legal in US (Delaware); restricted in India | Days |
| White Knight | Network of friendly bidders; data-room ready | Auction costs; fees 1–2% | Legal everywhere | Weeks |
| Staggered Board | Pre-existing charter amendment | Must be set up in advance | Legal in US; frowned on by ISS | Years to set up |
| Crown Jewel | Divestible prized asset | Loss of strategic asset | Risks breach of fiduciary duty | Weeks |
| Pac-Man | Balance-sheet capacity > bidder | Very high debt | Legal but rare | Weeks |
| Golden Parachute | Board approval on comp plan | Exec payout cost | Legal; Say-on-Pay risk | Pre-deal |
| Greenmail | Cash reserves | Large premium paid | Legal but reputational | Days |

**IT/AI example:** SaaS target with dual-class founder voting already has built-in defence; AI hardware firm may rely on CFIUS + poison pill.
**Trigger:** Any unsolicited approach or >5% accumulation.

### 3.3 Cross-Border Risk Map (Scoring 1-5)

| Dimension | Low (1) | High (5) | IT/AI Example Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regulatory Approvals | Single jurisdiction, no sensitive sector | Multi-jurisdiction + CFIUS/FDI | India buys US AI firm = 5 |
| FX | Same-currency or pegged | Emerging-market crosses | INR↔USD 8-month close = 4 |
| Culture | Same language, similar norms | Hierarchical vs. flat | India IT buys Japan SI = 5 |
| Tax | Tax treaty exists | Treaty shopping risk / GAAR | India→Mauritius→US = 4 |
| Political | Stable democracies | Sanctions / war risk | Europe buys Russia = 5 |
| Country Risk | AAA sovereign | Sub-investment grade | Buyer in Turkey = 5 |

Score 1-5 per axis; deals scoring >18/30 need a dedicated cross-border steerco.
**Trigger:** Any cross-border LOI.

### 3.4 Merger-Control Timeline (Optional)

```
 CCI (India)     : 30 WD Phase I  | +210 days outer  | (Green-channel: instant)
 HSR (US)        : 30-day wait   | Second Request = +months
 EUMR (Brussels) : 25 WD Phase I  | Phase II = +90 WD
 CFIUS (US)      : 30 WD review   | 45 WD investigation | +15 WD extension
```

**IT/AI example:** A India-listed SaaS buying an EU+US target lives on the slowest clock (often EUMR Phase II or CFIUS investigation).

## 4. Formulas

### 4.1 Deal-Contingent FX Hedge Cost
```
 Hedge Cost ≈ (Forward Premium × Notional × Duration) + Deal-Contingent Premium
 where Deal-Contingent Premium ≈ 15%-40% of vanilla forward cost
```
**Thresholds:** If hedge cost >2% of deal value, revisit deal structure or local-currency financing.
**Example:** Indian AI-services firm bids GBP 200 m for UK SaaS target, 8-month close. Vanilla 8-m INR/GBP forward premium ≈ 2.8% annualised → on GBP 200 m ≈ ₹47 cr. Deal-contingent premium ≈ 30% × ₹47 cr = ₹14 cr extra. **Total hedge cost ≈ ₹61 cr.**

### 4.2 Country Risk Premium Adjustment to WACC
```
 CRP = λ × Sovereign Spread
 Adjusted Cost of Equity = Rf + β × ERP + CRP
```
Where λ (lambda) is the target's exposure to local economy (0-1.5), and Sovereign Spread = local USD bond yield − US Treasury yield of same tenor.
**Thresholds:** CRP >4% → price in a management-layer retention plan; >7% → require political-risk insurance.
**Example:** Indian buyer acquires Brazilian fintech. Brazil 10-yr USD spread ≈ 3%, λ = 1.2 → CRP = 3.6%. If vanilla Ke = 10%, adjusted Ke = 13.6%; valuation drops ~20% on DCF.

### 4.3 Break Fee / Reverse Break Fee
```
 Break Fee (target pays) ≈ 1%-3% of deal value
 Reverse Break Fee (buyer pays) ≈ 3%-6% of deal value in cross-border hostile
```
**Thresholds:** Reverse break fee >5% is a market signal of regulatory risk (CFIUS, CCI).
**Example:** US AI firm bids USD 1 bn for Indian pharma-tech target. Reverse break fee of 4% = **USD 40 m** if CFIUS or CCI kills the deal. If deal-contingent FX hedge breaks alongside, embed a 'cost-sharing' clause to recover up to 50%.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Map all approvals (CFIUS, FDI, EUMR, CCI) before LOI | Assume antitrust is a formality because "we're small" |
| Use deal-contingent FX hedges for long close windows | Use vanilla forwards that cost 100% if deal dies |
| Build a local advisor team (counsel + banker + PR) pre-announcement | Parachute a HQ team into a foreign target on day 1 |
| Model country-risk premium inside WACC, not as a haircut | Apply a flat 20% "EM discount" to IRR |
| Pre-stage poison pill and white-knight lists on your own board | Wait for a hostile letter to start defence planning |
| Size reverse break fee to regulatory risk, not to "market average" | Accept generic 2% fee when CFIUS risk is real |
| Run cultural-diligence workshops with target mid-management | Treat "culture" as a soft-skill afterthought |
| Disclose stake-builds exactly at SEBI/13D thresholds | Creep past thresholds by 0.1% and risk disgorgement |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios

### Scenario 1 — AI-SaaS Acquirer Targets US Firm (CFIUS + HSR)
An Indian-listed AI analytics firm bids USD 800 m for a US cybersecurity-SaaS vendor with federal customers. The deal triggers mandatory CFIUS filing (TID-US business) + HSR. Team files CFIUS declaration (30-day review) and HSR (30-day wait). Uses a **deal-contingent FX hedge** (INR/USD, 10-m tenor, premium ≈ 0.8% of notional = USD 6.4 m). Embeds a **reverse break fee of 5%** (USD 40 m) payable if CFIUS blocks. Tools: Mergermarket for deal precedents, Cravath for US counsel, DealRoom VDR, JPM hedging desk.

### Scenario 2 — Indian IT-Services Firm Cross-Border EU Acquisition (EUMR Phase I)
A Bengaluru IT-services firm acquires a German data-engineering boutique for EUR 300 m. Combined EU turnover breaches EUMR thresholds → Phase I filing in Brussels. Culture diligence reveals strong Betriebsrat (works council) expecting consultation. Buyer engages **Cyril Amarchand (India) + Freshfields (EU)**, runs 4-week culture workshops, and commits to a 24-month no-layoff clause. Phase I clears in 22 working days. FX exposure (EUR/INR) hedged with 6-month deal-contingent forward. Closes on schedule.

### Scenario 3 — ANTI-EXAMPLE: Hostile Bid Without Poison-Pill Strategy
A Mumbai-based SaaS acquirer launches an unsolicited tender offer for a US fintech target at a 20% premium — **without mapping defences**. Target board adopts a 10% poison pill within 48 hours, retains Goldman Sachs, and within 6 weeks finds a PE-backed white knight bidding 35% above the hostile price. Indian acquirer withdraws. Quantified cost:
- Advisor & counsel fees (banker retainer + legal): **₹42 cr**
- Deal-contingent FX hedge unwind cost: **₹18 cr**
- Reputational damage (stock fell 7% on withdrawal, ~₹380 cr mcap erosion)
- **Total hard cost ≈ ₹60 cr + ~₹380 cr mcap hit.**

Lesson: always scenario-plan the defence playbook (pill likelihood, white-knight universe, golden parachute triggers) before bear-hug stage.

**Tools across scenarios:** Mergermarket, Bloomberg M&A terminal, DealRoom VDR, local counsel (Cyril Amarchand, Cravath, Freshfields, Nishith Desai), deal-contingent hedging desks (JPM / GS / Citi), RWI insurance brokers (Marsh, Aon), political-risk insurers (MIGA).

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. **Draft** a cross-border readiness memo (jurisdictions × approvals × timelines) before LOI.
2. **Engage** local counsel + banker + PR in each target jurisdiction within 7 days of board go-ahead.
3. **Model** WACC with country-risk premium and stress-test DCF at 3 FX scenarios (spot ±15%).
4. **Procure** a deal-contingent FX hedge quote from ≥2 banks; embed break-fee-sharing clause.
5. **Pre-stage** defences on your own cap table (poison pill template, white-squire list, parachute plan).
6. **File** merger-control notifications in parallel (CCI + HSR + EUMR + CFIUS) with consistent narrative.
7. **Run** cultural-diligence workshops with target's top-2 management layers before signing.
8. **Publish** a 100-day integration plan with FX-adjusted synergy targets and local-hiring commitments.

## 9. Quick-Recall Card
- Hostile = public bid, expect pill + white knight; cross-border = stacked regulators + FX + culture.
- Map approvals (CFIUS, FDI, EUMR, CCI) **before** LOI, not after.
- Use **deal-contingent** FX hedges; size **reverse break fee** to regulatory risk (3-6%).
- Adjust WACC with CRP = λ × sovereign spread; stress-test DCF at FX ±15%.
- Build local advisor + PR team in each jurisdiction; run culture workshops with mid-management.
- **As a PM/Consultant/AI Lead, the one question to answer with this framework is: Which jurisdiction, defence, or FX move will decide whether this deal closes on time and at the price we underwrote, and what is our walk-away number in each scenario?**
