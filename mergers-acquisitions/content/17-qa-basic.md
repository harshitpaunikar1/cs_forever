# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational questions covering core M&A concepts. Q1 – Q70

---

## Q1. What does M&A stand for?

**Level:** Basic

M&A stands for "mergers and acquisitions." It covers deals where two companies combine into one (merger) or where one company buys control of another (acquisition). The term is used for both friendly and unfriendly deals of all sizes.

**Real-life applications:**
- A newspaper reports a "merger" when two airlines combine fleets.
- A CFO tracks M&A budgets for possible acquisitions next year.
- Students learn M&A basics in finance and strategy classes.
- Lawyers specialise in M&A documentation and approvals.

**Key concepts:** `Merger`, `Acquisition`, `Combination`, `Corporate finance`, `Deal`

---

## Q2. What is the main goal of most M&A deals?

**Level:** Basic

The main goal is to create more value by combining two businesses than they would have created separately. This extra value can come from cost savings, higher revenue, new markets, or shared technology. If no extra value is expected, the deal usually does not make sense.

**Real-life applications:**
- A bank buys a rival to save on branches and back office.
- A pharma firm acquires a startup to gain new drug patents.
- A retailer merges with a delivery firm to cut logistics cost.
- A tech group buys a startup to reach younger customers.

**Key concepts:** `Value creation`, `Synergy`, `Growth`, `Cost saving`, `Strategy`

---

## Q3. What is a merger?

**Level:** Basic

A merger is when two companies agree to combine and form a single new company. The two original firms may both dissolve into a new entity, or one may absorb the other. Mergers are usually friendly and agreed by both boards.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two regional banks merge into one larger national bank.
- Two pharma firms merge to combine research pipelines.
- A legal merger creates a new firm brand and leadership.
- Mergers often need shareholder and regulator approval.

**Key concepts:** `Merger`, `Combination`, `New entity`, `Board approval`, `Integration`

---

## Q4. What is an acquisition?

**Level:** Basic

An acquisition is when one company buys a majority stake in another and takes control. The target may continue to exist as a subsidiary or be absorbed into the buyer. The buyer pays in cash, shares, or a mix of both.

**Real-life applications:**
- A tech giant acquires a smaller AI startup for its team.
- A retailer acquires a supplier to secure supply.
- A PE firm acquires a family business from retiring owners.
- Shareholders of the target receive cash for their shares.

**Key concepts:** `Acquisition`, `Target`, `Control`, `Subsidiary`, `Consideration`

---

## Q5. Who is the acquirer in a deal?

**Level:** Basic

The acquirer is the company doing the buying. It is the party that pays the price and ends up owning the target. In a friendly merger, the larger or more influential firm is usually called the acquirer.

**Real-life applications:**
- A multinational is the acquirer when it buys a local distributor.
- The acquirer sets the integration plan after the deal.
- Boards of acquirers carry heavy diligence responsibility.
- Acquirers often need financing partners for large deals.

**Key concepts:** `Acquirer`, `Buyer`, `Control`, `Financing`, `Integration`

---

## Q6. Who is the target in a deal?

**Level:** Basic

The target is the company being bought. Its shareholders receive cash or shares in exchange for giving up ownership. After the deal closes, the target either becomes a subsidiary of the acquirer or is merged into it.

**Real-life applications:**
- A startup is the target when a tech giant acquires it.
- The target's board negotiates for the highest possible price.
- Employees of the target often worry about job changes.
- Target directors owe duties to their shareholders in the deal.

**Key concepts:** `Target`, `Seller`, `Shareholders`, `Consideration`, `Board duty`

---

## Q7. What is synergy in M&A?

**Level:** Basic

Synergy is the extra value that is expected when two companies combine. It includes cost savings (fewer offices, shared systems) and revenue gains (cross-selling, bigger customer base). Synergy is the reason why 1 + 1 can equal more than 2 in a good deal.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two banks merge to save on duplicated branches.
- A soap maker uses its big distribution to sell the target's shampoo.
- A tech firm adds an acquired product to its existing contracts.
- A buyer shuts an extra factory after merging.

**Key concepts:** `Synergy`, `Cost saving`, `Revenue growth`, `Combination`, `Value`

---

## Q8. What is a cost synergy?

**Level:** Basic

A cost synergy is a saving that comes from cutting duplicate costs after a merger. Examples include merging head offices, shared IT systems, or one legal team instead of two. Cost synergies are usually easier and faster to achieve than revenue synergies.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two merged companies close one of two head offices.
- IT teams merge and cancel duplicate software contracts.
- Combined procurement gets bigger supplier discounts.
- Shared finance teams replace two separate ones.

**Key concepts:** `Cost synergy`, `Overhead`, `Procurement`, `Consolidation`, `Savings`

---

## Q9. What is a revenue synergy?

**Level:** Basic

A revenue synergy is extra sales that come from combining two businesses. It can come from cross-selling products, using a larger customer base, or entering new markets. Revenue synergies are usually harder to prove and slower to deliver than cost synergies.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer sells the target's insurance to its banking clients.
- A retailer adds the target's products to its online shelf.
- A tech company offers target software to its global base.
- A pharma firm uses its sales reps to promote new drugs.

**Key concepts:** `Revenue synergy`, `Cross-selling`, `Market reach`, `Customer base`, `Growth`

---

## Q10. What is a hostile takeover?

**Level:** Basic

A hostile takeover is when a buyer tries to acquire a target without the agreement of the target's board. The buyer goes directly to shareholders with an offer, bypassing management. Hostile bids are rare but often public and dramatic.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer launches a tender offer after the target board refuses.
- A hostile bid can lead to a higher price from rival bidders.
- The target may search for a friendly rescuer ("white knight").
- Hostile deals often end up in court battles.

**Key concepts:** `Hostile takeover`, `Tender offer`, `Board opposition`, `Defense`, `Shareholders`

---

## Q11. What is a friendly takeover?

**Level:** Basic

A friendly takeover happens when the target's board agrees to be bought. The two sides negotiate price and terms, and the deal is recommended to shareholders. Most M&A deals in the world are friendly.

**Real-life applications:**
- A target CEO jointly announces the deal with the buyer.
- Two boards agree a merger ratio before going public.
- Friendly deals typically have less regulatory drama.
- Employees get clearer messages in a friendly transition.

**Key concepts:** `Friendly takeover`, `Negotiated deal`, `Board approval`, `Recommendation`, `Integration`

---

## Q12. What is a share purchase?

**Level:** Basic

A share purchase is when the buyer buys the shares of the target company. The target's business, assets, and liabilities all pass to the buyer as part of the share sale. It is the most common structure for acquiring private companies.

**Real-life applications:**
- A PE firm buys 100% of a company's shares from its founder.
- Share purchases transfer all contracts automatically.
- Share deals are often simpler but bring hidden liabilities.
- A share purchase agreement (SPA) governs the terms.

**Key concepts:** `Share purchase`, `Ownership`, `SPA`, `Liabilities`, `Structure`

---

## Q13. What is an asset purchase?

**Level:** Basic

An asset purchase is when the buyer buys specific assets and liabilities of the target instead of its shares. This lets the buyer pick what to take (like a factory) and leave what to avoid (like old lawsuits). Asset deals usually involve more paperwork.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer purchases only one factory and its workers.
- A bank buys a loan portfolio without buying the whole bank.
- A buyer avoids certain legal risks with an asset deal.
- Customer contracts may need fresh consents in an asset deal.

**Key concepts:** `Asset purchase`, `Business transfer`, `Liability carve-out`, `Consent`, `Structure`

---

## Q14. What is due diligence?

**Level:** Basic

Due diligence is the detailed check a buyer does on a target before closing a deal. It covers finance, tax, legal, operations, IT, HR, and more. The goal is to confirm what the seller says and find hidden risks.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer audits the target's last three years of accounts.
- Lawyers review all major contracts and licences.
- Tax specialists check for open tax demands.
- Technical teams check IT systems and cyber risk.

**Key concepts:** `Due diligence`, `Risk review`, `Data room`, `Specialists`, `Red flags`

---

## Q15. What is a data room?

**Level:** Basic

A data room is a secure online folder where the seller uploads documents for the buyer to review during diligence. It contains contracts, financials, HR files, and more. Access is tightly controlled and usually tracked.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seller uploads five years of accounts to a virtual data room.
- Buyers download HR policies from a data room.
- Access is usually logged for audit and confidentiality.
- Bankers host the data room during a sale process.

**Key concepts:** `Data room`, `Confidentiality`, `Diligence`, `Access control`, `Documents`

---

## Q16. What is a term sheet?

**Level:** Basic

A term sheet is a short document that lists the main commercial terms of a deal before lawyers draft the full agreement. It covers price, structure, exclusivity, and key conditions. Most of the term sheet is non-binding.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer signs a term sheet fixing price and exclusivity.
- Lawyers turn the term sheet into a long SPA later.
- The term sheet often includes a walk-away break fee.
- Venture capital deals also start with a term sheet.

**Key concepts:** `Term sheet`, `LOI`, `Non-binding`, `Main terms`, `Exclusivity`

---

## Q17. What is exclusivity in a deal?

**Level:** Basic

Exclusivity is a period during which the seller agrees not to talk to any other buyer. It protects the buyer while it spends money on diligence and drafting. Typical exclusivity lasts 30 to 90 days.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer pays for exclusivity with a better first offer.
- Sellers resist long exclusivity to keep competition.
- Exclusivity clauses often carry break fees.
- Ending exclusivity lets the seller restart talks with rivals.

**Key concepts:** `Exclusivity`, `Lockout`, `Protection`, `Break fee`, `Process`

---

## Q18. What is a break fee?

**Level:** Basic

A break fee is a payment one side makes to the other if the deal falls apart under certain conditions. It compensates the non-breaching party for wasted time and costs. Break fees are common in large public deals.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer pays $50M if it walks away without a valid reason.
- Sellers pay break fees if they accept a higher competing bid.
- Break fees cover diligence costs and management time.
- Regulators sometimes limit break fee size in listed deals.

**Key concepts:** `Break fee`, `Termination`, `Walk-away`, `Compensation`, `Protection`

---

## Q19. What is a conditions precedent (CP)?

**Level:** Basic

A condition precedent is something that must happen before a deal can close. Common CPs include regulatory approvals, shareholder consent, and no major bad event. If a CP is not met by a long-stop date, the deal can be cancelled.

**Real-life applications:**
- A deal needs CCI approval as a CP before closing.
- Lenders demand their own CPs before releasing funds.
- Buyers list retention of key employees as a CP.
- If a CP fails, parties walk away without damages.

**Key concepts:** `CP`, `Closing conditions`, `Approvals`, `Long-stop date`, `Termination`

---

## Q20. What is a MAC clause?

**Level:** Basic

A MAC (Material Adverse Change) clause lets the buyer back out if something very bad happens to the target between signing and closing. Examples include a huge loss, major lawsuit, or regulatory shock. MAC clauses are hard to trigger in practice.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer invokes MAC when a pandemic destroys demand.
- MAC clauses exclude normal industry-wide events.
- Courts set a high bar before allowing a MAC exit.
- MAC wording is heavily negotiated by both sides.

**Key concepts:** `MAC`, `Walk-away`, `Material change`, `Risk allocation`, `Clause`

---

## Q21. What is a share swap?

**Level:** Basic

A share swap is when the buyer pays for the target using its own shares instead of cash. The target's shareholders become new shareholders of the buyer. It keeps the buyer's cash free and aligns both sides going forward.

**Real-life applications:**
- A tech firm buys a startup using its listed shares.
- A merger of equals often uses a share swap.
- The swap ratio is based on each firm's valuation.
- Shareholders accept share consideration when they like the buyer's future.

**Key concepts:** `Share swap`, `Consideration`, `Valuation ratio`, `Dilution`, `Alignment`

---

## Q22. What is a leveraged buyout (LBO)?

**Level:** Basic

A leveraged buyout is the purchase of a company using a large amount of borrowed money. The debt is placed onto the target itself and repaid from the target's cash flows. LBOs are common in private equity deals.

**Real-life applications:**
- A PE fund buys a stable industrial firm with 60% debt.
- Debt is secured on the target's cash flows and assets.
- LBO targets usually have steady cash and low existing debt.
- High leverage magnifies returns (and risk).

**Key concepts:** `LBO`, `Leverage`, `Debt financing`, `Private equity`, `Cash flow`

---

## Q23. What is a management buyout (MBO)?

**Level:** Basic

A management buyout is when the current managers of a company buy it from the owners. They often team up with a PE firm to fund the deal. MBOs happen when founders retire or when a parent wants to sell a division.

**Real-life applications:**
- A regional branch managers buy out from a multinational parent.
- Founders sell to their management team on retirement.
- Banks lend to the management team against future cash flows.
- MBOs keep continuity because leaders stay.

**Key concepts:** `MBO`, `Management`, `Continuity`, `Succession`, `Financing`

---

## Q24. What is private equity (PE)?

**Level:** Basic

Private equity refers to investment firms that raise money from investors to buy, improve, and resell companies. They typically own each company for 3 to 7 years. PE firms focus on financial returns, not long-term operation.

**Real-life applications:**
- A PE firm raises a $1B fund and buys 8 companies.
- PE deals often use a mix of equity and debt.
- Investors expect returns of 2-3x their money over 5-7 years.
- PE firms sell businesses via IPO, strategic sale, or to another PE.

**Key concepts:** `Private equity`, `Financial buyer`, `Exit`, `Fund`, `Returns`

---

## Q25. What is the difference between a financial and a strategic buyer?

**Level:** Basic

A financial buyer (like a PE firm) buys for investment returns and plans to resell. A strategic buyer (another operating company) buys to gain business benefits like customers, products, or market share. Strategics often pay more because they see synergies.

**Real-life applications:**
- A PE firm (financial) competes with a rival company (strategic) for the same target.
- Strategic buyers pay a synergy premium.
- Financial buyers focus on cash flow and exit options.
- Sellers often prefer strategic buyers for speed and price.

**Key concepts:** `Strategic buyer`, `Financial buyer`, `Synergy`, `Returns`, `Exit`

---

## Q26. What is valuation?

**Level:** Basic

Valuation is the process of estimating how much a company is worth. It uses techniques like DCF, multiples, or asset value to arrive at a price range. A good valuation uses more than one method as a cross-check.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer values a target at Rs 500-600 crore before making an offer.
- Investment bankers prepare valuation reports for boards.
- Courts use valuation experts in shareholder disputes.
- Valuation supports fair pricing in related-party deals.

**Key concepts:** `Valuation`, `Pricing`, `DCF`, `Multiples`, `Range`

---

## Q27. What is DCF valuation?

**Level:** Basic

DCF (Discounted Cash Flow) is a valuation method that adds the future cash a business is expected to produce, discounted back to today's value. It reflects what the business can earn, not what the market is paying. DCF is sensitive to forecasts and the discount rate.

**Real-life applications:**
- A CFO builds a 5-year DCF for a potential acquisition.
- Bankers use DCF to set an offer range.
- DCF is common for growth and capital-heavy businesses.
- The result is shown alongside multiples to sanity-check.

**Key concepts:** `DCF`, `Cash flow`, `Discount rate`, `Terminal value`, `Forecast`

---

## Q28. What is multiples-based valuation?

**Level:** Basic

Multiples valuation values a company by applying a ratio (like EV/EBITDA) from similar listed or recently sold firms. It is fast and market-linked. Its accuracy depends on finding truly comparable companies.

**Real-life applications:**
- An analyst values a retail chain at 10x EBITDA based on peers.
- Deal bankers use recent transaction multiples for benchmarking.
- Pre-IPO firms are often priced using revenue multiples.
- A P/E multiple is used for listed stock comparisons.

**Key concepts:** `Multiples`, `EV/EBITDA`, `Comparable`, `Benchmark`, `Market pricing`

---

## Q29. What is EBITDA?

**Level:** Basic

EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortisation. It is a simple measure of operating cash-like profit, ignoring how a business is financed or its non-cash expenses. M&A deals use EBITDA as a core pricing metric.

**Real-life applications:**
- Bankers quote deals at "8x EBITDA."
- EBITDA is used to compare firms with different capital structures.
- Lenders rely on EBITDA to size debt.
- Adjusted EBITDA removes one-time items for a clearer picture.

**Key concepts:** `EBITDA`, `Operating profit`, `Multiple`, `Leverage`, `Comparability`

---

## Q30. What is enterprise value (EV)?

**Level:** Basic

Enterprise value is the total value of a business, including debt and minus cash. It is the price to "take over" the whole firm, not just its equity. Bankers use EV with EBITDA and sales to compute multiples.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer quotes an EV of Rs 1,000 crore for a deal.
- EV/EBITDA lets investors compare firms with different debt levels.
- EV = Equity value + Debt - Cash.
- EV is the base for most M&A pricing talk.

**Key concepts:** `Enterprise value`, `Equity value`, `Debt`, `Cash`, `Multiple`

---

## Q31. What is equity value?

**Level:** Basic

Equity value is the portion of enterprise value that belongs to shareholders after subtracting debt and adding back cash. It is what a buyer pays to the selling shareholders for the shares. Equity value per share is what retail investors usually see.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer pays Rs 700 crore of equity value to sellers.
- Equity value per share = equity value / number of shares.
- Share-purchase agreements focus on equity value.
- Market cap is the equity value of a listed firm.

**Key concepts:** `Equity value`, `Market cap`, `Share price`, `Shareholders`, `Consideration`

---

## Q32. What is a premium in M&A?

**Level:** Basic

A premium is the extra amount the buyer pays above the current market price of the target's shares. It reflects control, synergies, and the urgency of the deal. Typical premiums are 20% to 40% in listed-company deals.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer offers 30% premium to the last trading price.
- Premiums are higher in contested deals.
- Boards check if the premium is fair to shareholders.
- Analysts compare premiums across recent sector deals.

**Key concepts:** `Premium`, `Market price`, `Control`, `Synergy`, `Offer price`

---

## Q33. What is a scheme of arrangement?

**Level:** Basic

A scheme of arrangement is a court-approved plan to reorganise a company, often used for mergers, demergers, and amalgamations in India. It is approved by the NCLT and binds all shareholders and creditors. Schemes are the standard route for Indian listed-company mergers.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two Indian subsidiaries merge via an NCLT scheme.
- A listed company demerges its real estate arm via a scheme.
- Schemes need shareholder, creditor, NCLT, and SEBI nods.
- Schemes transfer all assets and contracts automatically.

**Key concepts:** `Scheme`, `NCLT`, `Amalgamation`, `Demerger`, `Approval`

---

## Q34. What is the NCLT?

**Level:** Basic

The NCLT is the National Company Law Tribunal, an Indian court that handles corporate matters like mergers, demergers, and insolvency. It approves schemes of arrangement under the Companies Act. Without NCLT approval, a scheme cannot take legal effect.

**Real-life applications:**
- NCLT hears objections from minority shareholders on a scheme.
- NCLT approves an amalgamation order.
- Companies file sworn statements and notices with NCLT.
- NCLT benches operate across major Indian cities.

**Key concepts:** `NCLT`, `Tribunal`, `Scheme approval`, `Companies Act`, `Insolvency`

---

## Q35. What is SEBI's role in M&A?

**Level:** Basic

SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) regulates M&A involving listed Indian companies. It enforces the Takeover Code, insider trading rules, disclosure rules, and listing obligations. Any listed-company deal must follow SEBI timelines and rules.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer makes a SEBI-mandated open offer when crossing 25%.
- SEBI approves schemes involving listed firms.
- SEBI punishes violations of insider-trading rules.
- SEBI checks disclosure of related-party deals.

**Key concepts:** `SEBI`, `Takeover code`, `Disclosure`, `Listed company`, `Regulation`

---

## Q36. What is an open offer?

**Level:** Basic

An open offer is a mandatory offer by a buyer to buy additional shares from public shareholders when it crosses certain thresholds in a listed Indian firm. It is triggered when a buyer gains 25% or more voting rights or control. The offer protects minority shareholders.

**Real-life applications:**
- A promoter crossing 25% triggers an open offer.
- The minimum open offer size is 26% of voting rights.
- Offer prices follow SEBI's formula for listed deals.
- Escrow accounts back open-offer commitments.

**Key concepts:** `Open offer`, `SAST`, `Minority protection`, `Thresholds`, `Escrow`

---

## Q37. What is the SEBI Takeover Code?

**Level:** Basic

The SEBI Takeover Code (SAST Regulations) sets out how acquirers can take over listed Indian companies. It specifies thresholds that trigger open offers, pricing rules, timelines, and disclosures. Every listed M&A follows the Takeover Code.

**Real-life applications:**
- Buyers follow SAST for listed-target deals in India.
- SAST mandates full public offers in certain cases.
- SAST sets rules on negotiated vs. market purchases.
- Merchant bankers handle open offers under SAST.

**Key concepts:** `SAST`, `Takeover code`, `Thresholds`, `Open offer`, `Listed deal`

---

## Q38. What is a representation in a deal?

**Level:** Basic

A representation is a statement of fact the seller makes about the business in the sale agreement. Examples include "our accounts are true" or "we have no major lawsuits." If a representation turns out to be false, the buyer can seek damages.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seller represents it owns all the IP in a software firm.
- Buyers make their own reps too, like ability to pay.
- Reps are supported by disclosure schedules.
- Breach of rep leads to a claim under warranties.

**Key concepts:** `Representation`, `Seller statement`, `Disclosure`, `Warranty`, `SPA`

---

## Q39. What is a warranty in a deal?

**Level:** Basic

A warranty is a promise in the sale agreement that a specific statement is true. If it turns out not to be true, the other side can claim compensation. Warranties are usually backed by caps, baskets, and survival periods.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sellers warrant that accounts follow accounting standards.
- Warranty claims give a route to recover losses.
- W&I insurance often covers unknown warranty breaches.
- Warranties survive for 12-24 months, tax often longer.

**Key concepts:** `Warranty`, `Promise`, `Claim`, `Cap`, `Survival`

---

## Q40. What is an indemnity?

**Level:** Basic

An indemnity is a specific promise by one side to pay the other for certain losses, often linked to known risks. Unlike warranties, indemnities do not need the buyer to prove a breach. Indemnities are common for tax, environment, and litigation risks.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seller indemnifies the buyer for a known tax dispute.
- Environmental cleanup costs are indemnified separately.
- Indemnities are uncapped or have higher caps than warranties.
- Indemnity claims are pound-for-pound recoveries.

**Key concepts:** `Indemnity`, `Specific risk`, `Protection`, `Claim`, `Tax`

---

## Q41. What is a disclosure schedule?

**Level:** Basic

A disclosure schedule is a list of exceptions to the seller's reps and warranties. It carves out known issues so the buyer cannot claim on them later. A buyer reviews the disclosure schedule carefully during diligence.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sellers disclose a pending customer lawsuit in the schedule.
- Disclosure schedules protect sellers from known-issue claims.
- Buyers negotiate which items are general vs. specific disclosure.
- Lawyers spend days perfecting the disclosure schedule.

**Key concepts:** `Disclosure`, `Exceptions`, `Protection`, `Reps`, `Carve-out`

---

## Q42. What is a cap on warranty claims?

**Level:** Basic

A cap is the maximum amount a seller must pay for warranty claims. It is usually set as a percentage of the purchase price (often 10% to 30%). The cap protects the seller from unlimited liability.

**Real-life applications:**
- A cap of 20% limits seller liability on warranty breaches.
- Tax indemnity is often uncapped up to 100% of price.
- Fundamental warranties often carry higher caps.
- Buyers and sellers negotiate caps hard.

**Key concepts:** `Cap`, `Liability limit`, `Warranty`, `Protection`, `Negotiation`

---

## Q43. What is a basket in M&A?

**Level:** Basic

A basket is a minimum threshold a buyer's total losses must cross before making warranty claims. It stops minor claims. Once the basket is crossed, claims can be made from the first rupee or only the excess.

**Real-life applications:**
- A basket of Rs 1 crore filters out small claims.
- "Tipping" baskets allow claims from the start after threshold.
- "Deductible" baskets only allow claims above the threshold.
- Baskets reduce post-closing friction.

**Key concepts:** `Basket`, `Threshold`, `Deductible`, `Tipping`, `Claims`

---

## Q44. What is W&I insurance?

**Level:** Basic

Warranty and Indemnity (W&I) insurance is a policy that covers breaches of warranties. The buyer or seller pays a premium and the insurer pays claims within the policy. W&I shifts risk from the seller to the insurance market.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer uses W&I to protect against unknown risks.
- PE sellers use W&I for clean exits.
- Policy cover is usually 10-30% of price for 7 years on tax.
- W&I adoption has grown rapidly in India.

**Key concepts:** `W&I insurance`, `Risk transfer`, `Premium`, `Claims`, `Protection`

---

## Q45. What is a locked-box mechanism?

**Level:** Basic

A locked-box is a pricing mechanism that fixes the price on a past balance sheet (like the last audited accounts). There is no post-closing adjustment. It is quick and certain but needs strict leakage protection.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seller prefers locked-box for speed to closing.
- Locked-box uses last-year-end audited figures.
- Interest is usually paid on price from box date to closing.
- Leakage clauses ban value extraction in the interim.

**Key concepts:** `Locked-box`, `Certainty`, `Leakage`, `Interest`, `Mechanism`

---

## Q46. What is a completion accounts mechanism?

**Level:** Basic

Completion accounts is a pricing mechanism where the final price is adjusted based on the target's numbers on the closing date. It reflects the "true" value at that moment. It takes longer to finalise but suits businesses with volatile working capital.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seasonal retailer uses completion accounts to fix closing cash.
- Price is adjusted for cash, debt, and working capital at close.
- Accountants prepare and dispute closing statements.
- Independent experts may resolve disputes.

**Key concepts:** `Completion accounts`, `Adjustment`, `Working capital`, `Close date`, `Expert`

---

## Q47. What is an earnout?

**Level:** Basic

An earnout is part of the purchase price that is paid later based on the target achieving certain performance targets. It bridges the gap when buyers and sellers disagree on future value. Earnouts are common in deals with high growth or forecast uncertainty.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seller gets an extra $20M if EBITDA grows 15% in 2 years.
- Earnouts align seller motivation post-closing.
- Revenue-based earnouts are easier to measure than EBITDA.
- Disputes on earnouts are common; drafting is critical.

**Key concepts:** `Earnout`, `Contingent`, `Performance`, `Targets`, `Alignment`

---

## Q48. What is net working capital in a deal?

**Level:** Basic

Net working capital (NWC) is current assets minus current liabilities of the target. Deals fix a "target NWC" at closing; any deviation adjusts the purchase price. This ensures the buyer gets a normal level of operating capital with the business.

**Real-life applications:**
- Closing NWC below target reduces the buyer's price.
- NWC target is typically a 12-month average.
- Bankers negotiate exclusions like cash and debt.
- Buyers monitor NWC carefully between sign and close.

**Key concepts:** `NWC`, `Working capital`, `Adjustment`, `Target`, `Closing`

---

## Q49. What is a share purchase agreement (SPA)?

**Level:** Basic

The SPA is the main contract in an M&A deal. It records the price, structure, representations, warranties, indemnities, and closing conditions. Lawyers spend weeks negotiating and drafting the SPA.

**Real-life applications:**
- A 200-page SPA governs a multi-million-dollar sale.
- Schedules to the SPA include disclosures and accounts.
- The SPA is signed first and closed later after CPs.
- Breach of SPA can trigger damages or walk-away rights.

**Key concepts:** `SPA`, `Contract`, `Warranties`, `Closing`, `Terms`

---

## Q50. What is a shareholders' agreement (SHA)?

**Level:** Basic

The SHA is an agreement among shareholders that governs how the company is run after a deal, especially in joint ventures or minority-stake deals. It covers board seats, veto rights, transfer rules, and exit plans. The SHA protects minority shareholders.

**Real-life applications:**
- A 40% investor gets board seats via the SHA.
- SHA includes drag-along and tag-along rights.
- Veto rights protect minorities on big decisions.
- SHA runs alongside the articles of association.

**Key concepts:** `SHA`, `Governance`, `Shareholder rights`, `Veto`, `Exit`

---

## Q51. What is a non-compete clause?

**Level:** Basic

A non-compete stops the seller from competing with the business they just sold. It typically applies for 2 to 3 years after closing in the same sector and territory. Buyers insist on non-competes to protect the value they paid for.

**Real-life applications:**
- A founder cannot start a rival firm for 3 years after sale.
- Senior staff sign non-competes tied to retention bonuses.
- Non-competes must be reasonable in scope to be enforceable.
- Violation can trigger damages or injunctions.

**Key concepts:** `Non-compete`, `Restriction`, `Protection`, `Scope`, `Enforcement`

---

## Q52. What is a no-solicitation clause?

**Level:** Basic

A no-solicitation clause stops the seller from poaching customers or employees of the sold business. It protects the buyer's workforce and revenue base after closing. It often lasts 1 to 3 years.

**Real-life applications:**
- A departing CEO cannot hire the company's engineers for 2 years.
- Non-solicits cover clients, suppliers, and staff.
- Breach triggers damages in the SPA.
- Non-solicits are usually easier to enforce than non-competes.

**Key concepts:** `No-solicitation`, `Employees`, `Clients`, `Protection`, `Restrictive covenant`

---

## Q53. What is a stand-still agreement?

**Level:** Basic

A stand-still stops a potential bidder from buying more shares or making a hostile bid for a set period. Targets use it during friendly talks to stop a buyer from going hostile if talks fail. It protects the target's options.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer signs a stand-still before accessing diligence.
- Stand-stills end when talks end or after a fixed period.
- Breach can lead to injunctions and lost trust.
- Stand-stills are common in contested situations.

**Key concepts:** `Stand-still`, `Bid restriction`, `Protection`, `Negotiation`, `Confidentiality`

---

## Q54. What is integration planning?

**Level:** Basic

Integration planning is the work to combine two companies after a deal closes. It covers people, processes, IT, systems, and customers. The plan is built before closing, so work starts on day one.

**Real-life applications:**
- An IMO prepares a 100-day plan before closing.
- Workstreams cover HR, IT, Finance, Sales, Operations.
- Day-one readiness checklists are prepared.
- Integration milestones are tracked by KPIs.

**Key concepts:** `Integration`, `Planning`, `IMO`, `Day one`, `Workstreams`

---

## Q55. What is an Integration Management Office (IMO)?

**Level:** Basic

An IMO is a dedicated team that runs integration work after a deal closes. It coordinates all workstreams, tracks progress, and escalates issues. A strong IMO is led by a senior leader with project and business experience.

**Real-life applications:**
- The IMO reviews all workstreams weekly for the first 100 days.
- IMO leads report to the CEO or CFO.
- IMO uses trackers, dashboards, and risk logs.
- IMO closes out once BAU teams can own the work.

**Key concepts:** `IMO`, `Coordination`, `Accountability`, `Tracking`, `Leadership`

---

## Q56. What is a 100-day plan?

**Level:** Basic

A 100-day plan is a prioritised list of actions to take in the first 100 days after closing. It focuses on quick wins, stability, and setting the direction for the full integration. It is published to employees and leaders early.

**Real-life applications:**
- The plan includes "merge payroll in 60 days."
- Quick wins build trust and visible momentum.
- Day 100 review shows progress and resets priorities.
- 100-day plans cover customer, employee, and system priorities.

**Key concepts:** `100-day plan`, `Priorities`, `Quick wins`, `Milestones`, `Communication`

---

## Q57. What is cultural integration?

**Level:** Basic

Cultural integration is the process of blending the values, habits, and ways of working of two companies. It covers how decisions are made, how people talk, and how performance is measured. Weak cultural fit is a top cause of deal failure.

**Real-life applications:**
- Surveys assess culture gaps before closing.
- Leaders role-model the new behaviours in town halls.
- Rituals and symbols are redesigned for the combined firm.
- Culture integration often takes 2-3 years.

**Key concepts:** `Culture`, `Values`, `Behaviour`, `Blend`, `Change management`

---

## Q58. What is a retention bonus?

**Level:** Basic

A retention bonus is extra pay offered to key employees for staying through the integration period. It is paid out over time, usually 12 to 24 months. Retention bonuses protect the buyer's investment in people.

**Real-life applications:**
- Key engineers get a 25% retention bonus paid after 18 months.
- CFOs of targets often receive retention packages.
- Bonuses are tied to non-compete and performance terms.
- Without retention, talent risk can destroy deal value.

**Key concepts:** `Retention`, `Bonus`, `Key people`, `Talent risk`, `Vesting`

---

## Q59. What is a merger of equals?

**Level:** Basic

A merger of equals is when two companies of similar size and strength combine with shared leadership and governance. Neither side is clearly the buyer. These deals are rare and often hard to execute because of power-sharing issues.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two banks of equal size merge with co-CEO for 2 years.
- Board seats are split evenly in a merger of equals.
- Branding and head office choices are heavily negotiated.
- Studies show MoEs often underdeliver vs. expectations.

**Key concepts:** `Merger of equals`, `Shared control`, `Governance`, `Branding`, `Integration`

---

## Q60. What is a demerger?

**Level:** Basic

A demerger is the opposite of a merger: splitting a company into two or more separate entities. Shareholders of the original firm receive shares of the new entities. Demergers unlock value by letting each business focus and be valued on its own.

**Real-life applications:**
- A conglomerate demerges its FMCG and real estate arms.
- Demergers create focused pure-play listed companies.
- Demergers follow a scheme of arrangement in India.
- Tax-neutral demergers are common under the Income Tax Act.

**Key concepts:** `Demerger`, `Spin-off`, `Focus`, `Value unlock`, `Scheme`

---

## Q61. What is a spin-off?

**Level:** Basic

A spin-off is a type of demerger where a parent company distributes shares of a subsidiary to its own shareholders, creating a new independent company. The parent keeps no stake (or a small stake) in the spin-off. Shareholders get shares in both firms.

**Real-life applications:**
- A tech giant spins off its semiconductor unit to shareholders.
- Listed spin-offs trade separately from day one.
- Spin-offs let investors pick which business they want.
- Spin-offs are tax-efficient in many regimes.

**Key concepts:** `Spin-off`, `Independence`, `Shareholder distribution`, `Focus`, `Listing`

---

## Q62. What is a cross-border M&A?

**Level:** Basic

A cross-border M&A is a deal where the buyer and target are in different countries. It adds foreign investment rules, tax structuring, currency, and cultural layers to the usual complexity. Many large deals today are cross-border.

**Real-life applications:**
- An Indian pharma buys a US biotech for new markets.
- FDI rules limit foreign buyers in certain sectors.
- Currency hedging protects the buyer between signing and close.
- Local advisors are needed in both countries.

**Key concepts:** `Cross-border`, `Foreign investment`, `Currency`, `Tax`, `Culture`

---

## Q63. What is a white knight?

**Level:** Basic

A white knight is a friendly third-party buyer that rescues a target from a hostile bidder. The target board prefers the white knight's offer. White knights are a common defensive tactic in hostile deals.

**Real-life applications:**
- A target finds a rival to outbid a hostile raider.
- White knights may get favoured terms in return.
- The original hostile bidder may raise its price or walk away.
- White-knight deals are often announced in days.

**Key concepts:** `White knight`, `Hostile defense`, `Rival bidder`, `Rescue`, `Board support`

---

## Q64. What is a poison pill?

**Level:** Basic

A poison pill is a defensive move that makes a hostile takeover very expensive, usually by giving existing shareholders the right to buy more shares cheaply when a hostile bidder crosses a threshold. This dilutes the bidder. Poison pills buy time for boards to negotiate or find alternatives.

**Real-life applications:**
- A listed firm adopts a poison pill when a raider crosses 10%.
- Poison pills are common in US deals, less so in India.
- Courts sometimes strike down poison pills as unfair.
- Poison pills usually lapse after a set period.

**Key concepts:** `Poison pill`, `Defense`, `Dilution`, `Threshold`, `Shareholders`

---

## Q65. What is a tender offer?

**Level:** Basic

A tender offer is a public offer to buy shares directly from shareholders of a listed company at a set price. It is used in hostile and friendly deals. The offer price is usually higher than the current market price.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer makes a tender offer at a 25% premium.
- Tender offers go to all public shareholders equally.
- SEBI regulates tender offers in India through SAST.
- Tender offers close within a fixed window.

**Key concepts:** `Tender offer`, `Public offer`, `Premium`, `Shareholders`, `Regulation`

---

## Q66. What is goodwill in accounting?

**Level:** Basic

Goodwill is the amount a buyer pays above the fair value of a target's net assets. It reflects brand, relationships, synergies, and future growth. Goodwill sits on the buyer's balance sheet and is tested annually for impairment.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer pays Rs 500 crore for Rs 300 crore of net assets, creating Rs 200 crore goodwill.
- Goodwill impairment hits the P&L in bad years.
- Ind AS requires annual goodwill impairment tests.
- Large goodwill write-downs can shake investor confidence.

**Key concepts:** `Goodwill`, `Purchase price allocation`, `Impairment`, `Intangible`, `Accounting`

---

## Q67. What is purchase price allocation (PPA)?

**Level:** Basic

PPA is the accounting exercise of splitting the purchase price across the target's identifiable assets (tangible and intangible) and liabilities, with any excess recognised as goodwill. PPA drives post-deal depreciation, amortisation, and impairment tests. It is done under Ind AS or IFRS rules.

**Real-life applications:**
- A valuer identifies Rs 100 crore of customer relationships.
- PPA creates intangible assets like brand and technology.
- Amortisation of intangibles reduces future reported profit.
- Auditors check PPA assumptions at year-end.

**Key concepts:** `PPA`, `Allocation`, `Intangible assets`, `Goodwill`, `Amortisation`

---

## Q68. What is a financial buyer's exit strategy?

**Level:** Basic

An exit strategy is how a PE or financial buyer plans to sell its stake and realise returns. Common exits include sale to another PE, sale to a strategic buyer, or IPO. The exit plan is set at the time of buying.

**Real-life applications:**
- A PE fund exits via IPO 5 years after acquisition.
- Secondary buyouts sell to another PE fund.
- Trade sales to strategics often bring the highest price.
- Exit returns are measured in IRR and MOIC.

**Key concepts:** `Exit`, `IPO`, `Trade sale`, `Secondary buyout`, `Returns`

---

## Q69. What is an IRR in a deal?

**Level:** Basic

IRR (Internal Rate of Return) is the annualised return a PE fund or investor earns on a deal from entry to exit. It considers the time value of money. PE firms usually target IRRs of 20% or more.

**Real-life applications:**
- A PE fund reports 22% IRR on a deal exited in 5 years.
- LPs compare funds using IRR and MOIC together.
- Higher leverage tends to boost IRR.
- Delay in exit reduces IRR significantly.

**Key concepts:** `IRR`, `Returns`, `Exit`, `Time value`, `Performance`

---

## Q70. What is a MOIC?

**Level:** Basic

MOIC (Multiple on Invested Capital) is the total money returned divided by the money invested, without considering time. A MOIC of 2.5x means every rupee invested returned 2.50 rupees. MOIC complements IRR as a measure of deal success.

**Real-life applications:**
- A PE deal returns 3x MOIC over 6 years.
- Investors look for both high MOIC and IRR.
- MOIC does not reward fast exits; IRR does.
- Fund performance tables show MOIC across deals.

**Key concepts:** `MOIC`, `Multiple`, `Returns`, `Invested capital`, `Exit`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### 3.1 M&A Lifecycle
Purpose: sequence the end-to-end deal flow so a junior resource can place any question in context.
```
Strategy -> Target Screening -> Term Sheet/LOI -> Due Diligence
   -> SPA Negotiation -> Signing -> Regulatory/CPs -> Close
      -> Day One -> 100-Day Plan -> Integration -> Performance Review
```
Components: M&A thesis, longlist/shortlist, NBO/BO, FDD/LDD/TDD/CDD, SPA schedules, CCI/SEBI filings, escrow/W&I, IMO charter, synergy scorecard.
Example: An IT-services strategic buys a 150-person AI consultancy — BizOps maps each step to a workstream owner.
Trigger: use whenever an interviewer says "walk me through an M&A deal".

### 3.2 Deal Structure Matrix

| Structure | Tax (buyer) | Risk carry-over | Speed | Typical use |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-------|-------------|
| Asset deal | Step-up, amortisable goodwill | Low (cherry-pick) | Slow (consents) | Carve-out of AI product line |
| Share deal | No step-up; inherit basis | High (all history) | Fast | Whole-company acquisition |
| Merger (scheme) | Tax-neutral if qualifying | Medium | Slowest (court/NCLT) | Two listed IT firms combining |

Trigger: used to answer "which structure would you recommend and why".

### 3.3 Synergy Classification with Haircuts
```
                Gross $    Haircut   Net $   Confidence
Cost synergies    100        20%       80       High
Revenue synergies  80        50%       40       Low
Tax synergies      20        30%       14       Medium
Financial          10        40%        6       Medium
                ----------------------------
Net to PV              =  140 * (1-t) -> discount at WACC
```
Example: Acquirer of an AI-SaaS target projects $100m cost + $80m revenue synergies; post-haircut and tax, PV falls to ~$350m, informing walk-away.
Trigger: any synergy-quantification question.

### 3.4 Valuation Triangle / Football Field
```
         DCF (intrinsic)
            /\
           /  \
          /    \
   Comps /______\ Precedents
   (trading)   (transaction)
```
Football field stacks ranges; overlap zone = defensible value. Example: For an AI copilot target, DCF says $500–600m, comps $450–700m, precedents $600–800m — overlap $600–700m becomes negotiation anchor.
Trigger: "what's it worth?" questions.

## 4. Formulas

1. **EV/EBITDA** = Enterprise Value / EBITDA
   - Threshold: mature IT-services 8–12x; AI-SaaS growth 20–40x on forward.
   - Example: Target EBITDA $50m, peer multiple 10x -> EV $500m.

2. **Accretion/Dilution** = (Combined NI - New Interest After-Tax) / (Acquirer shares + New shares issued) - Standalone EPS
   - Threshold: >0 accretive; <0 dilutive; boards typically need Year-1 or Year-2 accretion.
   - Example: Acquirer EPS $2.00 standalone -> $2.15 combined = 7.5% accretive.

3. **Synergy PV** = Σ [Gross_t × (1 − haircut) × (1 − tax)] / (1 + WACC)^t − CTA (costs-to-achieve)
   - Example: $100m gross cost synergies, 20% haircut, 25% tax, 10% WACC over 5 yrs, $30m CTA -> PV ~ $197m.

4. **Walk-Away Value** = Standalone DCF + Net Synergy PV
   - Example: Standalone $500m + net synergies $150m = $650m ceiling; anything above destroys value.

5. **Control Premium** = (Offer Price − Unaffected Price) / Unaffected Price
   - Threshold: typically 20–40% for listed targets.
   - Example: Unaffected $100 → offer $130 = 30% premium.

6. **EV/ARR** = EV / Annual Recurring Revenue (SaaS shortcut)
   - Threshold: AI-SaaS 8–20x ARR depending on growth + margin.
   - Example: $30m ARR × 15x = $450m EV.

## 5. Do vs Don't (Basic)

| # | Do | Don't |
|---|----|-------|
| 1 | Distinguish merger (legal combo) from acquisition (control purchase) | Don't use the terms interchangeably in an interview |
| 2 | Sign a term sheet / LOI with exclusivity before deep DD spend | Don't run full DD without exclusivity |
| 3 | Bake in CPs (regulatory, key-customer consents) before signing | Don't take over without CPs — you own every pre-close risk |
| 4 | Haircut revenue synergies heavily (≥50%) and phase them | Don't price 100% of revenue synergies into offer |
| 5 | Check SEBI open-offer trigger (≥25% / creeping 5%) on Indian listed targets | Don't ignore Indian SAST obligations |
| 6 | Stand up IMO + 100-day plan pre-close | Don't treat integration as a post-close afterthought |
| 7 | Decide W&I insurance vs escrow explicitly | Don't conflate the two — they cover different risks |
| 8 | Normalise EBITDA via QoE before applying multiples | Don't value off reported EBITDA without adjustments |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios

### Scenario 1: BizOps analyst walks M&A lifecycle for AI-SaaS acquisition
An IT-services buyer is evaluating a $200m AI-SaaS target. Analyst maps: Strategy (AI capability gap) → Screening (5 targets) → Term Sheet (90-day exclusivity, $180m indicative) → DD (FDD/LDD/TDD/CDD + AI-specific model/IP/data DD) → SPA (locked-box price, 15% cap, W&I) → CCI filing → Close → Day One (identity, email, comms) → 100-day plan (cross-sell to top-20 accounts, infra consolidation) → synergy tracker. Tools: CapIQ for comps, Mergermarket for precedents, DealRoom VDR.

### Scenario 2: PE associate vs strategic buyer comparison
Same target. Strategic buyer: pays with partial stock, underwrites $40m net synergies, tolerates 11x EBITDA, integrates fully. Financial (PE): all-cash LBO, 60% debt, no operating synergies, underwrites 25% IRR via margin expansion + multiple arbitrage, tolerates 9x entry. Output: strategic wins at higher price; PE wins at structure-flexibility and speed. Tools: Excel LBO model, PitchBook for sponsor benchmarks.

### Scenario 3 (ANTI-EXAMPLE): Candidate conflates EV and Equity value
In a consulting case, candidate is told EV = $500m, debt $100m, cash $20m. They quote "walk-away = $500m + synergies $150m = $650m equity offer". Correct: Equity = EV − Debt + Cash = $420m; walk-away-equity = $420m + $150m = $570m. Error: $80m overpayment on a $420m base = 19% value destruction; interviewer scores the case as "fail on numeracy"; candidate drops from "hire" to "no-hire" bucket — interview-scoring impact quantified as rejection from the deal-team track (6-month delay in getting staffed).

Tools across scenarios: CapIQ, PitchBook, Mergermarket, Excel LBO/merger model, DealRoom VDR, Bloomberg for unaffected price.

## 9. Quick-Recall Card

- M&A = combine (merger) or buy control (acquisition); strategic vs financial buyer drives everything.
- Valuation = DCF + comps + precedents; reconcile in a football field; control premium 20–40%.
- Synergies = Cost (easy) + Revenue (hard, 50% haircut) + Tax + Financial; PV them and net CTA.
- Deal = Term Sheet → DD → SPA (reps/warranties/indemnities, CPs, MAC) → CCI/SEBI → Close → 100-day.
- Structure: Asset (step-up, slow), Share (fast, inherited risk), Merger (scheme, slowest).
- As a PM/Consultant/AI Lead, the one question to answer with this framework is: "Is the combined walk-away value (standalone DCF + net synergy PV) greater than the offered price, after haircuts, tax, CTA and integration risk — and have we closed every CP and governance gate before Day One?"
