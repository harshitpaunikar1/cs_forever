# Practice Q&A — Intermediate

Applied questions on analysis, comparison, and reasoning for M&A. Q71 – Q140

---

## Q71. How do strategic and financial buyers differ in pricing?

**Level:** Intermediate

Strategic buyers can often pay more because they see synergies with their existing business and do not need to sell the firm again. Financial buyers pay based on stand-alone cash flows and target returns, so they cannot overpay without hurting IRR. This is why bidding wars often end with a strategic buyer winning.

**Real-life applications:**
- A PE fund loses to a strategic buyer offering a 15% synergy premium.
- Auction processes mix both buyer types to drive price.
- Sellers pitch to both types to maximise competition.
- Strategic bidders model synergies into their offer price.

**Key concepts:** `Strategic buyer`, `Financial buyer`, `Synergy premium`, `IRR`, `Auction`

## Q72. Why is EV/EBITDA preferred to P/E in M&A?

**Level:** Intermediate

EV/EBITDA compares total business value to operating profit, ignoring differences in capital structure and tax positions. P/E only looks at equity and is distorted by debt, interest, and tax. For M&A, where buyers replace the existing capital structure, EV/EBITDA is far more comparable across targets.

**Real-life applications:**
- Bankers quote deals using EV/EBITDA multiples.
- EV/EBITDA allows cross-border peer comparison.
- Debt-heavy firms look cheap on P/E but not on EV/EBITDA.
- Adjusted EBITDA is used to strip out one-time items.

**Key concepts:** `EV/EBITDA`, `P/E`, `Capital structure`, `Comparability`, `Multiples`

## Q73. What drives the discount rate (WACC) in a DCF?

**Level:** Intermediate

WACC combines the cost of equity (return expected by shareholders) and the after-tax cost of debt, weighted by the firm's capital mix. Cost of equity uses the risk-free rate, equity risk premium, and beta. A higher perceived risk raises WACC, which lowers the DCF value.

**Real-life applications:**
- Analysts test valuation sensitivity at WACC +/- 1%.
- Emerging-market targets attract a country risk premium.
- A small, private firm has a higher WACC than a listed giant.
- WACC is updated as debt ratios and markets change.

**Key concepts:** `WACC`, `Cost of equity`, `Cost of debt`, `Beta`, `Risk premium`

---

## Q74. Why is terminal value such a large part of DCF?

**Level:** Intermediate

Terminal value captures all cash flows beyond the forecast period, which is usually 5 to 10 years. Because growth continues indefinitely, the terminal value often makes up 60% to 80% of total DCF value. This makes the terminal growth rate and exit multiple critical assumptions.

**Real-life applications:**
- A 1% change in terminal growth can swing value 15%.
- Analysts cap terminal growth at long-run GDP growth.
- Exit-multiple terminal values are sanity-checked vs. market multiples.
- Buyers disclose terminal assumptions in board memos.

**Key concepts:** `Terminal value`, `DCF`, `Growth rate`, `Exit multiple`, `Sensitivity`

---

## Q75. How do buyers select comparable companies for multiples?

**Level:** Intermediate

Comparables should match on industry, size, geography, growth rate, and profitability. Too few or too different comparables make the multiple unreliable. Deal teams usually screen 8 to 15 peers and then narrow to 5 closest matches.

**Real-life applications:**
- A mid-cap Indian retailer is benchmarked against 6 listed peers.
- Size adjustments handle large vs. small peer differences.
- Bankers use transaction multiples for the closest deals.
- Peer set is presented in every fairness opinion.

**Key concepts:** `Comparables`, `Peer set`, `Industry`, `Size`, `Benchmark`

## Q76. What is a control premium?

**Level:** Intermediate

A control premium is the extra price a buyer pays to get control of a company, beyond minority trading prices. Control lets the buyer set strategy, appoint management, and capture synergies. Empirical data shows control premiums of 20% to 40% in listed-company deals.

**Real-life applications:**
- Public deals quote premiums above the last month's VWAP.
- Control premium differs from synergy premium.
- Minority discounts mirror control premiums in reverse.
- Fairness opinions cite control-premium studies.

**Key concepts:** `Control premium`, `Minority discount`, `Offer price`, `VWAP`, `Control`

## Q77. How should buyers value synergies?

**Level:** Intermediate

Synergies are valued using an incremental DCF of the extra cash flows, discounted at a risk-appropriate rate. Buyers separate cost synergies (safer, higher probability) from revenue synergies (riskier) and haircut revenue figures. Only a portion of the NPV of synergies should flow into the offer price.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer shares 30% of synergy NPV with the seller via price.
- Cost synergies are valued at WACC; revenue at a higher rate.
- Synergy models are stress-tested under downside cases.
- Boards require clear synergy cases before approving deals.

**Key concepts:** `Synergy valuation`, `Incremental DCF`, `Risk adjustment`, `Offer price`, `NPV`

## Q78. When does a buyer use stock instead of cash?

**Level:** Intermediate

A buyer pays in stock when it wants to conserve cash, align the seller with future performance, or is worth more on paper than in cash reserves. Stock is common in mergers of equals or when the target wants upside in the combined firm. The swap ratio depends on the relative valuations of both companies.

**Real-life applications:**
- A tech merger exchanges 1.2 buyer shares for 1 target share.
- Sellers accept stock when they believe in buyer upside.
- Stock deals avoid draining buyer cash balance.
- Dilution is a major consideration in stock deals.

**Key concepts:** `Stock consideration`, `Swap ratio`, `Cash deal`, `Dilution`, `Alignment`

## Q79. What is dilution in a stock deal?

**Level:** Intermediate

Dilution happens when the buyer issues new shares to pay for a target, reducing existing shareholders' stake and possibly their earnings per share. A deal is "accretive" if post-deal EPS rises and "dilutive" if it falls. Boards evaluate accretion/dilution for the first year and beyond.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer models year-1 EPS impact to test accretion.
- High-multiple buyers dilute less when buying low-multiple targets.
- Synergies convert dilution into accretion over time.
- Analysts react to accretive or dilutive deal announcements.

**Key concepts:** `Dilution`, `Accretion`, `EPS`, `Issued shares`, `Deal math`

---

## Q80. How does cross-border M&A add complexity?

**Level:** Intermediate

Cross-border deals bring foreign exchange risk, tax structuring, different accounting rules, different legal systems, cultural gaps, and country-specific regulators. A misread in any area can delay or kill the deal. Experienced local advisors are essential.

**Real-life applications:**
- Currency hedges protect a Japanese buyer of an Indian target.
- FDI rules limit foreign stakes in defence or media sectors.
- US CFIUS or Indian PN3 reviews can block deals.
- Language barriers slow diligence and integration.

**Key concepts:** `Cross-border`, `FX risk`, `Tax`, `Regulation`, `Culture`

## Q81. What is FDI and how does it affect Indian M&A?

**Level:** Intermediate

Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) rules limit or allow foreign ownership in Indian firms by sector. Some sectors are under automatic route (no approval needed) and others need government approval. The "PN3" rule requires prior approval for investments from neighbouring countries.

**Real-life applications:**
- A Singapore firm buys 100% of an IT target under automatic route.
- Defence sector FDI is capped with conditions.
- PN3 delays deals from Chinese investors by months.
- FDI compliance is a key CP in cross-border deals.

**Key concepts:** `FDI`, `Automatic route`, `Government approval`, `PN3`, `Sectoral cap`

## Q82. How do you value a loss-making target?

**Level:** Intermediate

A pure DCF may not work if profits are negative or unstable. Buyers use scenario-based DCFs, revenue multiples, or cost-to-replicate methods. For tech startups, investors often value users, contracts, or strategic optionality.

**Real-life applications:**
- An EV startup is valued at 10x revenue during growth.
- A pharma target is valued on probability-weighted drug pipelines.
- Buyers use "synergy rescue" models for distressed targets.
- Cost-to-build is a floor for data or IP-heavy firms.

**Key concepts:** `Loss-making`, `Revenue multiple`, `Scenario`, `Optionality`, `Replacement value`

## Q83. What is a quality of earnings (QoE) review?

**Level:** Intermediate

QoE is a diligence exercise to test how real and repeatable the target's reported EBITDA is. It removes one-off gains, normalises expenses, and flags accounting aggressiveness. A buyer relies on QoE-adjusted EBITDA for offer price and multiples.

**Real-life applications:**
- QoE finds Rs 20 crore of one-time gains and cuts EBITDA.
- Aggressive revenue recognition is flagged in QoE.
- PE buyers always run QoE before signing.
- QoE reports go to lenders to size debt.

**Key concepts:** `QoE`, `Adjusted EBITDA`, `Normalisation`, `Accounting`, `Diligence`

## Q84. Why do tax considerations matter in M&A?

**Level:** Intermediate

Tax can change the effective price by billions. Structure choices affect capital gains, stamp duty, GST, transfer pricing, and carry-forward losses. A wrong structure can trigger taxes that kill the deal's return.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer chooses a scheme to preserve Rs 200 crore of losses.
- Asset vs. share deal changes stamp duty significantly.
- Cross-border deals use holding companies in tax-treaty countries.
- Tax diligence flags pending demands and exposure.

**Key concepts:** `Tax structuring`, `Capital gains`, `Stamp duty`, `Losses`, `Treaty`

## Q85. How is working capital adjusted at closing?

**Level:** Intermediate

Buyers and sellers fix a target NWC (often the 12-month average) in the SPA. At closing, if actual NWC is below target, the purchase price is reduced; if above, it is increased. This protects the buyer from receiving a cash-starved business.

**Real-life applications:**
- Closing accounts show NWC Rs 10 crore below target; price cuts Rs 10 crore.
- Auditors prepare closing statements for both sides.
- Disputes go to an expert or arbitration.
- Sellers try to time collections before closing to reduce receivables.

**Key concepts:** `NWC`, `Target`, `Adjustment`, `Closing accounts`, `Dispute`

## Q86. What are locked-box leakage protections?

**Level:** Intermediate

Because price is fixed on an old balance sheet in a locked-box, the buyer must prevent value leaving the target until closing. Leakage clauses ban dividends, bonuses, related-party payments, and any other extraction, with permitted items clearly listed. Breach triggers a pound-for-pound recovery.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer recovers Rs 3 crore of unauthorised bonuses as leakage.
- "Permitted leakage" covers pre-agreed items.
- Leakage clauses run until closing day.
- Sellers warrant no leakage has happened.

**Key concepts:** `Locked-box`, `Leakage`, `Permitted leakage`, `Warranty`, `Recovery`

---

## Q87. When should a buyer insist on an earnout?

**Level:** Intermediate

Earnouts make sense when the buyer and seller disagree on future performance, when the target is a recent or volatile business, or when the founders will keep running it. They align incentives but bring measurement and governance challenges. Badly drafted earnouts lead to post-closing disputes.

**Real-life applications:**
- A tech acquirer pays $30M upfront plus $20M over 3 years on revenue.
- Earnout covenants limit the buyer's integration speed.
- Disputes over earnout accounting are common in courts.
- Minority protection provisions protect sellers during earnout.

**Key concepts:** `Earnout`, `Alignment`, `Measurement`, `Dispute`, `Governance`

---

## Q88. How does a scheme of arrangement differ from a share purchase?

**Level:** Intermediate

A scheme is a court-approved process that transfers all shares, assets, or divisions by operation of law, binding every shareholder. A share purchase is a contract between a buyer and specific sellers. Schemes are needed for listed-company mergers, full squeeze-outs, and complex restructurings.

**Real-life applications:**
- A listed merger uses a scheme; a private deal uses a share purchase.
- Scheme consents are by shareholder majority; purchase needs each seller.
- Schemes take longer due to NCLT timelines.
- Squeeze-outs of minority shareholders use schemes.

**Key concepts:** `Scheme`, `Share purchase`, `Court approval`, `Squeeze-out`, `Listed deal`

---

## Q89. How does the SEBI Takeover Code protect minority shareholders?

**Level:** Intermediate

SAST requires an open offer to minority shareholders once an acquirer crosses 25% voting rights or obtains control. The offer price follows prescribed formulas based on recent trading and past deals. This gives minorities a fair exit route and caps abuse.

**Real-life applications:**
- Minorities can tender at the open offer price and exit.
- Offer prices use VWAP of last 60 days trading.
- Escrow backs at least 25% of offer value.
- Merchant bankers run the open offer process.

**Key concepts:** `SAST`, `Open offer`, `Minority protection`, `Formula price`, `Escrow`

---

## Q90. What is the 25% and 26% rule in India?

**Level:** Intermediate

An acquirer crossing 25% voting rights in a listed Indian firm must make an open offer for at least 26% more. Together the buyer can reach up to 51% or more through a combination of SPA and open offer. Crossing 75% usually triggers delisting or forced tendering.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer at 20% adds 5% and triggers a 26% open offer.
- Promoters at 55% use creeping rules to reach 60%.
- Post-acquisition stakes over 75% often lead to delisting.
- Escrow and bank guarantees back the open offer commitment.

**Key concepts:** `25% threshold`, `Open offer`, `Creeping acquisition`, `Delisting`, `SAST`

---

## Q91. What is insider trading in M&A context?

**Level:** Intermediate

Insider trading is buying or selling listed shares based on unpublished price-sensitive information (UPSI) about a deal. M&A processes create UPSI long before announcement. Strict controls limit who has access, log their trades, and prevent leaks.

**Real-life applications:**
- A banker is suspended after suspicious trades before deal news.
- Trading windows are closed for insiders before announcement.
- Compliance teams maintain UPSI and "insider lists."
- SEBI pursues insider trading cases with steep penalties.

**Key concepts:** `Insider trading`, `UPSI`, `Compliance`, `Leak`, `Penalty`

## Q92. What is a fairness opinion?

**Level:** Intermediate

A fairness opinion is a banker's letter saying the consideration in a deal is fair to a specific group (like minority shareholders) from a financial point of view. It protects boards from lawsuits by giving an independent view. Fairness opinions rely on multiple valuation methods.

**Real-life applications:**
- An independent banker issues a fairness opinion to the target board.
- Boards reference fairness opinions in shareholder notices.
- Fees for fairness opinions are separate from deal success fees.
- Courts give weight to fairness opinions in disputes.

**Key concepts:** `Fairness opinion`, `Banker`, `Board protection`, `Minorities`, `Valuation`

## Q93. What are the main buyer risks in diligence?

**Level:** Intermediate

Key risks include overpaying, hidden tax liabilities, pending litigation, weak IP title, key-customer concentration, poor culture, and IT gaps. Each risk needs a tailored response: price cut, indemnity, CP, or walk-away. A structured red-flag report prioritises them by impact.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer cuts price 10% after finding hidden tax exposure.
- Key-customer contracts trigger retention CPs.
- IT diligence flags cyber risk needing insurance.
- Legal DD covers all material contracts and consents.

**Key concepts:** `Red flags`, `Risk allocation`, `Indemnity`, `CP`, `Walk-away`

## Q94. How do sellers prepare for diligence?

**Level:** Intermediate

Strong sellers run "vendor diligence" or a "sell-side QoE" before the auction. They organise the data room, fix obvious issues, and prepare clear answers to likely buyer questions. Good preparation keeps the process fast and maximises price.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seller engages a Big 4 firm for vendor QoE.
- Data room is indexed by function for quick access.
- Management presentations are rehearsed before buyer visits.
- Pre-cleared issues reduce buyer leverage on price.

**Key concepts:** `Vendor diligence`, `Sell-side`, `Preparation`, `Data room`, `Process`

## Q95. What is a clean team in M&A diligence?

**Level:** Intermediate

A clean team is a small, neutral group (often outside advisors) that sees competitively sensitive information (like pricing or customers) that cannot be shared with the buyer's commercial teams before closing. This satisfies antitrust rules while still allowing analysis. The clean team reports only aggregated insights.

**Real-life applications:**
- Two rival airlines use a clean team for route-level data.
- Antitrust lawyers set clean team protocols.
- Clean team outputs are sanitised summaries only.
- Clean team expires once the deal closes.

**Key concepts:** `Clean team`, `Antitrust`, `Sensitive data`, `Competition law`, `Protocol`

---

## Q96. Why does antitrust or CCI approval matter?

**Level:** Intermediate

Competition regulators check if a deal will reduce competition or create a dominant player. In India, the CCI must approve deals above certain size thresholds. Large horizontal deals are the most scrutinised.

**Real-life applications:**
- CCI imposes divestiture conditions on a merger.
- Global deals may need EU, US, and Indian clearances.
- Approval can take 3-9 months for complex deals.
- CCI filings include market-share and overlap data.

**Key concepts:** `CCI`, `Antitrust`, `Competition`, `Dominance`, `Clearance`

## Q97. How do buyers manage risk in emerging markets?

**Level:** Intermediate

Buyers use local advisors, thorough diligence on compliance and licences, escrows, W&I insurance, and political-risk cover. Contracts include governing law and arbitration clauses in neutral venues. Integration starts with ring-fencing the target to manage cultural and operational risks.

**Real-life applications:**
- A Japanese buyer uses Singapore arbitration for its India deal.
- Political-risk insurance covers government action risks.
- Escrows hold 10-20% of price for 2 years.
- Ring-fencing protects the target in the early integration period.

**Key concepts:** `Emerging markets`, `Arbitration`, `Escrow`, `Political risk`, `Ring-fencing`

## Q98. What is a representation and warranty insurance claim cycle?

**Level:** Intermediate

After a warranty breach, the buyer notifies the insurer within a policy window. The insurer reviews proof and underlying documents, then pays within policy limits and retention (basket). Most W&I claims are settled out of court, often within 12 months.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer recovers $5M from a W&I policy in 9 months.
- Policy exclusions cover known issues and fraud.
- Retention is typically 0.5% of deal value.
- Insurers investigate claims via their own teams.

**Key concepts:** `W&I claim`, `Retention`, `Exclusion`, `Notification`, `Settlement`

## Q99. How do earnout disputes usually arise?

**Level:** Intermediate

Disputes come from disagreements on EBITDA definitions, accounting treatment, buyer actions that reduce performance, or allocation of costs. Sellers often feel the buyer has "starved" the business of resources. Clear definitions and ring-fencing help but do not eliminate disputes.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seller sues because the buyer cut R&D, reducing revenue.
- Earnout accounting follows pre-agreed principles.
- Expert determination resolves accounting fights faster than courts.
- Protection clauses require buyer to run business "ordinary course."

**Key concepts:** `Earnout dispute`, `Accounting definition`, `Good faith`, `Expert`, `Ring-fencing`

## Q100. What is a pre-conditional offer?

**Level:** Intermediate

A pre-conditional offer is a bid made public only after certain pre-conditions (like diligence or financing) are met. It lets the buyer signal interest without full commitment. Targets and regulators sometimes allow them to avoid premature disclosure.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer announces a pre-conditional bid pending diligence.
- Pre-conditions include antitrust filings or board approval.
- Clearing pre-conditions triggers a firm offer.
- Stock exchanges set disclosure rules for pre-conditional offers.

**Key concepts:** `Pre-conditional offer`, `Announcement`, `Disclosure`, `Firm offer`, `Process`

## Q101. How do boards evaluate a takeover offer?

**Level:** Intermediate

Boards consider price, form of consideration, deal certainty, strategic fit, treatment of employees, and regulatory risks. They rely on advisors, fairness opinions, and independent director views. The board's recommendation influences shareholders' votes and informs any rival bids.

**Real-life applications:**
- A target board rejects a low offer and invites competing bids.
- Independent directors lead the evaluation in related-party deals.
- Boards document their reasoning in offer-response circulars.
- Shareholders sue boards for insufficient consideration.

**Key concepts:** `Board evaluation`, `Fiduciary duty`, `Fairness opinion`, `Recommendation`, `Shareholders`

## Q102. How do buyers structure financing for large deals?

**Level:** Intermediate

Buyers use a mix of own cash, new debt, new equity, bridge loans, and sometimes deferred consideration. Financing is committed at signing through underwriting letters. Bridge loans are refinanced after closing with permanent debt and equity issues.

**Real-life applications:**
- A global buyer raises $10B of bridge financing to sign a deal.
- Bridge is refinanced with bonds and a rights issue 6 months later.
- Loan covenants restrict post-closing capital actions.
- Deal certainty depends on financing being committed.

**Key concepts:** `Financing`, `Bridge loan`, `Underwriting`, `Covenants`, `Certainty`

---

## Q103. What is a squeeze-out?

**Level:** Intermediate

A squeeze-out forces remaining minority shareholders to sell their shares once the buyer reaches a high ownership threshold (like 90%). It gives the buyer 100% control and simplifies governance. Squeeze-outs are regulated to ensure fair prices for minorities.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer at 92% squeezes out minorities at the open offer price.
- Indian law allows squeeze-out via a scheme of arrangement.
- Minority shareholders can challenge the price in court.
- Squeeze-outs remove listed-company obligations.

**Key concepts:** `Squeeze-out`, `Minority`, `Threshold`, `Fair value`, `Delisting`

---

## Q104. How do buyers retain key talent after closing?

**Level:** Intermediate

Buyers use retention bonuses, clear roles, compelling vision, and non-compete protection. They identify top 20-50 critical people before signing and build tailored packages. Losing key talent in the first year can destroy most of the deal's value.

**Real-life applications:**
- A critical CTO gets a 2-year retention vest worth 30% of package.
- Buyers meet key staff in the first week of closing.
- New owners clarify reporting lines early.
- Performance equity rolls over from the target.

**Key concepts:** `Retention`, `Key talent`, `Vision`, `Equity rollover`, `Non-compete`

## Q106. How do synergies get tracked?

**Level:** Intermediate

Each synergy becomes a project with an owner, value target, start date, and go-live date. A tracker records committed vs. in-P&L savings. The CFO reports monthly to the steering committee and publishes quarterly progress.

**Real-life applications:**
- A synergy tracker lists 80 projects totalling Rs 500 crore.
- A "run-rate vs. in-year" view monitors actual impact.
- Projects are categorised by risk and dependency.
- CFO links tracker to the financial forecast.

**Key concepts:** `Synergy tracker`, `Run-rate`, `Ownership`, `Reporting`, `Governance`

## Q107. How do buyers handle customer concerns after closing?

They run proactive communication: CEO letters, account-manager visits, FAQs, and roadmap briefings. Top customers get direct outreach within the first 30 days. Service continuity is protected and contract rights respected to avoid triggering any change-of-control clauses.

**Real-life applications:**
- Top 50 accounts get in-person visits in month 1.
- CEO sends open letters to all customers on day one.
- Contracts with change-of-control clauses are renewed early.
- Churn risk is tracked in weekly scorecards.

**Key concepts:** `Customer retention`, `Change-of-control`, `Communication`, `Continuity`, `Outreach`

**Level:** Intermediate

A post-merger operating model defines how the combined firm will operate: structure, decision rights, processes, systems, and locations. It aligns the people, technology, and operating footprint to the deal's strategy. Getting the model right drives both synergy and morale.

**Real-life applications:**
- The new model centralises back-office in one hub.
- Two sales forces combine into one territorial structure.
- Tech platforms are consolidated to one ERP.

**Key concepts:** `Operating model`, `Structure`, `Decision rights`, `Processes`, `Footprint`

## Q109. What is Day 1 vs. Day 100 planning?

**Level:** Intermediate

Day 1 planning ensures the combined firm can legally and practically operate on closing day (payroll, banking, authorisations). Day 100 planning focuses on quick wins, stabilising operations, and setting direction for long-term integration. Both need to be locked before closing.

**Real-life applications:**
- Day 1 payroll runs cleanly across both entities.
- Day 100 plan delivers Rs 50 crore of early synergies.
- Customer pricing is unified by day 100.
- Leadership confirms go-forward org by day 100.

**Key concepts:** `Day 1`, `Day 100`, `Readiness`, `Quick wins`, `Stabilisation`

## Q110. What are common integration failures?

**Level:** Intermediate

Common failures include poor culture fit, lost key talent, delayed synergy capture, customer churn, IT integration issues, and weak governance. These usually trace back to rushed planning or under-resourced IMOs. Early warning signs are missed milestones and rising attrition.

**Real-life applications:**
- Delayed IT merger causes customer downtime.
- High attrition erodes the value of an acquihire.
- Weak governance leads to slow decisions.
- Synergy tracker goes red on 30% of projects.

**Real-life applications:**

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer flags 12 top accounts at risk post-merger.

## Q114. How is IT integration staged?

**Level:** Intermediate

IT integration has phases: day-1 connectivity (emails, access, networks), infrastructure consolidation (data centres, cloud), and application rationalisation (one ERP, one CRM). Each phase has risk, so buyers prioritise by business impact. Full integration can take 18-36 months.

**Real-life applications:**
- Day 1 delivers email forwarding and shared access.
- Phase 2 consolidates ERPs over 12 months.
- Cloud lift-and-shift speeds up data centre consolidation.
- Data migration risks are pilot-tested before cutover.

**Key concepts:** `IT integration`, `Phases`, `Rationalisation`, `Migration`, `Risk`

## Q115. What is a change-of-control clause?

**Level:** Intermediate

A change-of-control clause lets a counterparty (customer, lender, licensor) terminate or renegotiate a contract when ownership of the other side changes. Diligence teams identify all such clauses so buyers can plan waivers, consents, or re-papering. Missing one can cause revenue or licence loss after closing.

**Real-life applications:**
- A large customer demands renegotiation after change of control.
- Banks require consent for loan covenants on change of control.
- Licensors tighten terms in response to M&A.
- Consents are a common CP in the SPA.

**Key concepts:** `Change of control`, `Consent`, `Termination`, `Diligence`, `Contracts`

**Real-life applications:**
- A raider uses a $20B bridge loan to fund a hostile bid.
- Activist funds publicly support the raider's case.
- Bank commitments signal seriousness to target shareholders.

## Q117. What defences do targets use against hostile bids?

**Level:** Intermediate

Defences include poison pills, staggered boards, supermajority rules, white knights, golden parachutes, and public campaigns. The right defence depends on the jurisdiction and shareholder base. In India, SEBI rules limit some defences that are common in the US.

**Real-life applications:**
- A US target adopts a poison pill to slow a raider.
- Boards hold firm with long-term investors' backing.
- A target finds a white knight in 48 hours.
- Public campaigns sway retail shareholders.

**Key concepts:** `Poison pill`, `White knight`, `Staggered board`, `Parachute`, `Defence`

---

## Q118. How does goodwill impairment hit financials?

**Level:** Intermediate

If the acquired business underperforms vs. the deal case, annual impairment tests can force a non-cash write-down of goodwill. This reduces reported profit and equity but not cash. Large impairments often signal that the deal did not deliver expected value.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer writes down Rs 1,200 crore of goodwill after 3 years.
- Auditors require detailed impairment models each year.
- Impairment does not reverse under Ind AS.
- Share prices fall on impairment announcements.

**Key concepts:** `Goodwill impairment`, `Write-down`, `Ind AS`, `Non-cash`, `Investor impact`

---

## Q119. How do buyers choose between cash and stock deals?

**Level:** Intermediate

They weigh balance-sheet strength, share price, tax impact for sellers, earnings dilution, and strategic alignment. Cash is quick and clean but drains reserves; stock preserves cash but dilutes and carries market risk. Boards sometimes offer a mix to balance the trade-offs.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer with overvalued shares uses stock to pay.
- Sellers prefer cash for tax simplicity.
- A mixed deal balances flexibility and alignment.
- Board models both options on EPS and leverage.

**Key concepts:** `Cash vs. stock`, `Dilution`, `Leverage`, `Seller tax`, `Alignment`

---

## Q120. What is deal certainty?

**Level:** Intermediate

Deal certainty is the probability that a signed deal will actually close on time. It depends on financing commitments, regulatory risks, shareholder support, and contract conditions. Sellers prefer high-certainty buyers even at slightly lower prices.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seller picks a lower-price offer with firmer financing.
- Antitrust risk reduces certainty for horizontal deals.
- Shareholder vote thresholds affect certainty.
- Break fees compensate sellers for low-certainty bids.

**Key concepts:** `Deal certainty`, `Financing`, `Regulation`, `Breakage`, `Signalling`

---

## Q121. How do buyers test synergy feasibility?

**Level:** Intermediate

Synergy feasibility comes from benchmarking similar deals, bottom-up project plans, and sensitivity analysis. Each synergy is assigned owners and a probability-weighted value. Buyers publish conservative targets to manage investor expectations.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer presents bottom-up synergy plans to the board.
- Synergies are risk-adjusted by 20% in the deal model.
- External advisors benchmark historical synergy capture rates.
- Milestone-based releases tie synergies to deliverables.

**Key concepts:** `Synergy feasibility`, `Benchmarking`, `Bottom-up`, `Risk adjustment`, `Deliverables`

---

## Q122. What is a reverse merger?

**Level:** Intermediate

A reverse merger is when a private company merges into a listed shell company to become public without a traditional IPO. The private firm becomes the main business; the listed firm becomes the parent. Reverse mergers offer faster access to public markets but carry reputational risks.

**Real-life applications:**
- A tech company uses a reverse merger to list quickly.
- SPACs use a reverse merger concept to list operating businesses.
- Regulators scrutinise shell-company track records.
- Reverse mergers can enable listing despite IPO slowdowns.

**Key concepts:** `Reverse merger`, `Shell company`, `Listing`, `SPAC`, `Disclosure`

---

## Q123. What is a SPAC?

**Level:** Intermediate

A Special Purpose Acquisition Company (SPAC) is a blank-cheque listed company that raises cash from investors to later merge with a private target, effectively listing it. It offers a faster and more flexible route to public markets than a traditional IPO. SPAC mergers need diligence and disclosure like any M&A deal.

**Real-life applications:**
- A SPAC acquires a green-energy startup and takes it public.
- Investors can redeem shares if they dislike the target.
- Regulators tightened SPAC disclosure rules.
- SPAC popularity rose in 2020-21, then cooled.

**Key concepts:** `SPAC`, `Blank cheque`, `De-SPAC`, `Disclosure`, `Listing`

---

## Q124. What is a tender offer vs. open offer?

**Level:** Intermediate

A tender offer is a public offer to buy listed shares, used in both friendly and hostile deals worldwide. An open offer is the Indian regulatory term for a SEBI-mandated offer to minority shareholders triggered by thresholds. All open offers are tender offers, but not all tender offers are open offers.

**Real-life applications:**
- A US hostile tender offer bypasses the target board.
- An Indian buyer at 25% makes a SAST-mandated open offer.
- Open offer pricing follows SEBI's formula.
- Tender offers use regulatory timelines.

**Key concepts:** `Tender offer`, `Open offer`, `SAST`, `Regulation`, `Minorities`

---

## Q125. How is a joint venture different from an acquisition?

**Level:** Intermediate

A JV is a new entity formed by two or more partners to run a shared business, while each partner keeps its own firm. An acquisition buys an existing company. JVs share risk and resources; acquisitions give full control and faster decision-making.

**Real-life applications:**
- An Indian firm forms a JV with a Japanese partner to sell cars.
- Acquisitions suit situations needing speed and control.
- JVs are used to test markets with lower risk.
- JV governance agreements mirror SHA principles.

**Key concepts:** `JV`, `Acquisition`, `Partnership`, `Control`, `Risk sharing`

---

## Q126. When is a carve-out used?

**Level:** Intermediate

A carve-out is the sale of a specific division, unit, or subsidiary from a larger parent. Buyers see focus; sellers raise cash and sharpen strategy. Carve-outs are complex because shared services (IT, HR, Finance) need to be separated, often via transition service agreements.

**Real-life applications:**
- A conglomerate carves out its shipping unit for sale.
- Transition service agreements support the new buyer for 12 months.
- Carve-outs need audited stand-alone financials.
- Shared contracts need assignment or novation.

**Key concepts:** `Carve-out`, `TSA`, `Stand-alone accounts`, `Shared services`, `Novation`

---

## Q127. What is a transition service agreement (TSA)?

**Level:** Intermediate

A TSA is a contract where the seller provides services (IT, payroll, procurement) to the buyer for a defined period after a carve-out. It bridges the gap while the buyer stands up its own operations. TSAs include scope, fees, SLAs, and exit plans.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer uses a 12-month TSA for IT services.
- TSAs are priced at cost-plus or market rates.
- Exit from TSA is milestone-based.
- Penalties apply for SLA breach.

**Key concepts:** `TSA`, `Services`, `Cost recovery`, `SLA`, `Exit`

---

## Q128. What is purchase price allocation (PPA) under Ind AS?

**Level:** Intermediate

PPA allocates the total deal consideration across the identifiable net assets of the target at their fair values, with any excess going to goodwill. Intangibles like brand, customer relationships, and technology are recognised separately. Future P&L carries their amortisation.

**Real-life applications:**
- A PPA recognises Rs 80 crore of brand and Rs 120 crore of customer intangibles.
- Goodwill is tested annually; intangibles amortised over useful life.
- Auditors review PPA models with valuer input.
- PPA changes tax depreciation and deferred tax.

**Key concepts:** `PPA`, `Ind AS`, `Intangibles`, `Goodwill`, `Amortisation`

---

## Q129. How do earnouts affect accounting?

**Level:** Intermediate

Earnouts are recorded as contingent consideration at fair value on closing and revalued each period. Changes in fair value often flow through P&L. Heavy earnout volatility can complicate financial reporting and investor communication.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer books Rs 50 crore of contingent consideration on day 1.
- Earnout revaluation creates P&L swings.
- Analysts strip out earnout accounting noise.
- CFOs flag earnout impacts in guidance.

**Key concepts:** `Contingent consideration`, `Fair value`, `Earnout`, `Ind AS 103`, `Volatility`

---

## Q130. What is a material adverse change (MAC) in practice?

**Level:** Intermediate

MACs are rarely triggered because courts interpret them narrowly. A true MAC usually needs a long-lasting, serious change in the business, not a normal downturn. Specific MAC definitions (naming events or thresholds) are more enforceable than general wording.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer fails to trigger MAC during an industry-wide slump.
- Specific MAC clauses cover disease outbreaks or regulatory actions.
- Courts require high proof of material, long-term impact.
- MAC carve-outs exclude market and industry-wide events.

**Key concepts:** `MAC`, `Walk-away`, `Court interpretation`, `Specific carve-out`, `Enforceability`

---

## Q131. What is a reverse break fee?

**Level:** Intermediate

A reverse break fee is paid by the buyer to the seller if the deal fails due to buyer-side reasons, like financing failure or antitrust blockade. It protects the seller from buyer-caused breakage. Reverse break fees are common in cross-border deals with regulatory risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer pays $500M reverse break fee after antitrust blockade.
- Sizes range from 2% to 6% of deal value.
- Seller uses reverse break fee to recover costs and walk away.
- They are heavily negotiated with financing banks.

**Key concepts:** `Reverse break fee`, `Buyer risk`, `Financing`, `Antitrust`, `Compensation`

---

## Q132. What is a drag-along right?

**Level:** Intermediate

A drag-along right lets majority shareholders force minority shareholders to sell their shares in a deal on the same terms. It protects exits by ensuring the buyer can get 100%. Drag-alongs are common in private equity and VC investments.

**Real-life applications:**
- A PE firm drags minorities in a sale to a strategic buyer.
- Drag-alongs speed up auctions.
- Minority thresholds (like 60% majority) trigger drag.
- Drag-along and tag-along often sit together in SHAs.

**Key concepts:** `Drag-along`, `Minority`, `Exit`, `SHA`, `100% sale`

---

## Q133. What is a tag-along right?

**Level:** Intermediate

A tag-along lets minority shareholders sell their shares on the same terms if a majority sells. It protects minorities from being left behind with a new, unknown owner. Tag-alongs and drag-alongs are frequently paired.

**Real-life applications:**
- A founder sells to a new investor; co-founders tag along.
- Tag-along ensures equal exit rights across shareholders.
- Protects minorities when majority changes hands.
- Enforced via the SHA clauses.

**Key concepts:** `Tag-along`, `Minority`, `Exit parity`, `SHA`, `Protection`

---

## Q134. What is a right of first refusal (ROFR)?

**Level:** Intermediate

ROFR gives a shareholder the right to buy shares at the price a third party has offered, before the seller can sell to that third party. It allows existing owners to block unwanted new owners. ROFRs can slow auctions and deter outside bidders.

**Real-life applications:**
- A JV partner exercises ROFR to buy out the other side.
- Auctioneers flag ROFRs early to manage bidder process.
- ROFR terms include timelines and matching conditions.
- Some SHAs use ROFR to preserve balance of control.

**Key concepts:** `ROFR`, `Match right`, `SHA`, `Transfer restriction`, `Control`

---

## Q135. How do buyers use escrow accounts?

**Level:** Intermediate

Buyers hold back part of the price (10-20%) in an escrow account to secure warranty and indemnity claims after closing. The escrow is released over time if no claims arise. Escrows are common in deals without W&I insurance.

**Real-life applications:**
- A 10% escrow is held for 18 months to cover claims.
- Escrows release in tranches (50% at 12 months, 50% at 24 months).
- Escrow agents are neutral banks with clear release rules.
- Buyers claim against escrow before suing sellers directly.

**Key concepts:** `Escrow`, `Hold-back`, `Claim protection`, `Release`, `Bank`

---

## Q136. What is a holdback in M&A?

**Level:** Intermediate

A holdback is similar to escrow, but the money stays with the buyer as a deferred payment due to the seller. It protects the buyer from warranty breaches while giving the seller a claim on it over time. Holdbacks are common in smaller deals without full escrows.

**Real-life applications:**
- A 15% holdback is released in 3 equal tranches over 18 months.
- Holdbacks save escrow agent fees.
- Payment of holdback is conditional on no pending claims.
- Interest accrues on holdbacks to compensate seller.

**Key concepts:** `Holdback`, `Deferred payment`, `Protection`, `Tranches`, `Interest`

---

## Q137. What is an NDA in M&A?

**Level:** Intermediate

A Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA) is signed early in deal talks to protect both sides' confidential information. NDAs cover purpose, permitted recipients, period, and remedies. They often include non-solicitation of staff and customers.

**Real-life applications:**
- A buyer signs an NDA before accessing the data room.
- NDAs last 2 to 5 years from signing.
- Breach leads to injunction and damages.
- NDAs often include stand-still clauses in large deals.

**Key concepts:** `NDA`, `Confidentiality`, `Duration`, `Stand-still`, `Breach`

---

## Q138. What is a representations bring-down?

**Level:** Intermediate

A bring-down is confirmation at closing that the seller's representations are still true. Material breach between signing and closing can let the buyer walk away. The bring-down certificate is signed and delivered at closing.

**Real-life applications:**
- The seller signs a bring-down certificate at closing.
- Breach at closing can trigger MAC or walk-away.
- Bring-downs reduce post-closing surprise risk.
- Scope of bring-down is negotiated tightly.

**Key concepts:** `Bring-down`, `Closing`, `Reps`, `MAC`, `Certificate`

---

## Q139. What is reverse due diligence?

**Level:** Intermediate

Reverse diligence is when the seller or target checks the buyer, usually in stock deals or JVs. It confirms the buyer's financial strength, governance, and ability to close. Reverse diligence is common when sellers take stock consideration or stay on as partners.

**Real-life applications:**
- A seller reviews the buyer's audited accounts in a stock deal.
- JV partners run mutual reverse diligence.
- Reverse diligence checks antitrust risk on the buyer's side.
- A founder reviews the buyer's culture before accepting equity.

**Key concepts:** `Reverse diligence`, `Seller review`, `Buyer risk`, `Stock deal`, `JV`

---

## Q140. How should boards balance speed and diligence?

**Level:** Intermediate

Boards want speed to lock in a deal before competitors or market shifts, but rushing diligence risks hidden liabilities. Good boards insist on clear diligence milestones, tiered approvals, and walk-away rights. They set time limits on issues that matter most, not every line item.

**Real-life applications:**
- A board approves a deal in 60 days with weekly updates.
- Risk-based diligence focuses on top 10 issues.
- Standing committees make fast, informed decisions.
- External advisors accelerate specialist reviews.

**Key concepts:** `Board speed`, `Diligence depth`, `Risk focus`, `Approvals`, `Milestones`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### 3.1 SPA Decision Stack (Commercial x Legal x Protection)
**Purpose:** sequence SPA negotiation priorities.
```
+------------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 1 COMMERCIAL: Price | Earnout | Working-Capital Peg |
+------------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 2 LEGAL: CPs | MAC+Scrape | Termination | Break-Fee |
+------------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 3 PROTECTION: Reps | Survival | Cap | Basket | W&I  |
+------------------------------------------------------------+
| LAYER 4 POST-CLOSING: Indemnity | Escrow | Set-off Rights |
+------------------------------------------------------------+
```
**Components:** Commercial intent → CP gates → rep-based protection → post-close remedies.
**Worked example - IT-services carve-out:** $200M price, 15% cap ($30M), $200K de-minimis, $2M tipping basket, 18-month survival on business reps, 6-year on fundamental/IP, 1.2% RWI premium, 2% break fee.
**Trigger:** use on every >$50M deal; re-run when valuation shifts >10%.

### 3.2 Open Offer Price Formula (SAST)
**Purpose:** compute mandatory open-offer floor under SEBI SAST.
```
           +-----------------------+
           | Trigger: 25% or ctrl  |
           +-----------------------+
                      |
     +----------------+----------------+
     |         |          |            |
  Negotiated  60-day   2-week      26-week
  per-share   VWAP     VWAP        High
     |         |          |            |
     +---------+----+-----+------------+
                    |
                 TAKE MAX  -> Open Offer Price
```
**Components:** negotiated SPA price, 60-day VWAP, 2-week VWAP, 26-week high; frequently-traded test applies.
**Worked example - listed IT-services target:** SPA Rs.410, 60d VWAP Rs.395, 2w VWAP Rs.420, 26w high Rs.435 → open-offer Rs.435 for 26% of public float.
**Trigger:** any acquisition of voting rights in a listed Indian company crossing 25% or change-of-control.

### 3.3 Haspeslagh-Jemison 2x2 (Integration Archetypes)
**Purpose:** pick integration operating model.
```
                   Need for Strategic Interdependence
                        LOW                 HIGH
                   +-------------+----------------+
Need for     HIGH  | PRESERVATION| SYMBIOTIC      |
Organ.             | keep brand, | co-exist,      |
Autonomy           | light touch | selective merge|
                   +-------------+----------------+
             LOW   | HOLDING     | ABSORPTION     |
                   | portfolio   | full integrate |
                   +-------------+----------------+
```
**Components:** strategic interdependence (synergy need) vs autonomy (cultural/talent).
**Worked example - AI-SaaS acquisition:** high tech synergy + talent-sensitive → Symbiotic; preserve founder team 24 months, integrate data-plane and GTM by month 12.
**Trigger:** choose within 30 days of signing; anchors TSA scope and Day-1/100/365 plan.

PURCHASE PRICE ($500M)
        |
        v
+-------+-------+-----+--------+------------+
|       |       |     |        |            |
Tangible Tech   Cust  Brand    Non-compete  Goodwill

$40M    $120M  $90M  $30M      $10M         $210M

## 4. Formulas

### 4.1 Open Offer Price
`Offer Price = max(Negotiated, 60d-VWAP, 2w-VWAP, 26w-High, Highest Acquisition in prior 52w)`
**Threshold:** minimum 26% of public shareholding for open offer size.
**Example:** max(410, 395, 420, 435, 425) = Rs.435; offer for 26% of 40M public shares = 10.4M shares x 435 = Rs.452.4cr.

### 4.2 Basket Math (Tipping vs Deductible)
- **Tipping:** loss ≥ threshold → recover from dollar one.
- **Deductible:** loss ≥ threshold → recover only above threshold.
**Example:** $2M basket, $3M loss.
- Tipping: recover $3M.
- Deductible: recover $1M.

### 4.3 PPA Goodwill
`Goodwill = Purchase Price − (Tangible Assets + Identifiable Intangibles − Assumed Liabilities) + DTL`
**Example:** $500M − ($40M + $250M − $60M) + $62.5M DTL = $232.5M goodwill.

### 4.4 Synergy NPV (Risk-Adjusted)
`NPV = Σ [(Gross Synergy_t × (1 − haircut) × (1 − t_tax)) − CTA_t] / (1+WACC)^t`
**Thresholds:** haircut 20-40% (McKinsey 2024); CTA ~1x run-rate synergy; WACC = pre-deal WACC + 100-200bps integration risk.
**Example:** $50M/yr gross, 30% haircut, 25% tax, $40M CTA year 1, 11% WACC, 5-year: ~$100M NPV after CTA.

### 4.5 Post-Deal ROIC
`ROIC_post = NOPAT_post / (Pre-Deal Invested Capital + Purchase Price + Integration Capex)`
**Threshold:** ROIC_post ≥ WACC + 200bps within 3 years.
**Example:** NOPAT $85M / Invested Capital $680M = 12.5%; vs WACC 10% → 250bps spread — acceptable.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Scrub MAC with industry/pandemic/war carve-outs and materiality scrape | Don't rely on bare MAC — courts read narrowly (Akorn/Fresenius) |
| Keep CP list to 5-8 closing conditions | Don't build 50-item CP list — signals distrust, delays closing |
| Use RWI to bridge gaps in diligence coverage | Don't let RWI substitute for diligence — insurers exclude known issues |
| Use median + regression for precedent multiples | Don't use arithmetic mean of precedents — outliers skew |
| Include PPA forecast in IC memo | Don't omit PPA — surprises post-close goodwill plan |
| Check FEMA/FDI sectoral caps pre-LOI | Don't forget FEMA triggers — defence/telecom/insurance need approval route |
| Establish clean team for overlapping product data | Don't share competitive data pre-closing — HSR/EUMR gun-jumping risk |
| Fund escrow at SAST 25%/10% minimums in cash or BG | Don't under-escrow — SEBI rejection delays open offer 60+ days |
| Model 20-40% synergy haircut | Don't take seller's synergy deck at face value |
| Include earnout governance (board/audit/veto) | Don't structure earnout with zero protection — post-close disputes spike |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios

### Scenario 1 - SEBI Open-Offer for Listed IT-Services Target
Buyer signs SPA at Rs.410/share for 26% promoter stake in listed mid-cap IT-services firm. 60d-VWAP Rs.395; 2w-VWAP Rs.420; 26w-high Rs.435. Open-offer price = Rs.435. Offer size: 26% of 40M public shares = 10.4M shares. Total offer value Rs.452.4cr. Escrow: 25% on first Rs.500cr = Rs.113cr via bank guarantee. Timeline: PA Day 0 → DPS Day 5 → DLoF Day 14 → SEBI observations Day 21 → LoF dispatch Day 46 → Offer open 10 working days → closing ~ Day 70.
**Tools:** Bloomberg SAST price calculator, CapIQ, SEBI SCORES.

### Scenario 2 - PPA for AI-SaaS Acquisition ($500M)
Target: vertical AI-SaaS, $60M ARR, 80% gross margin, $40M tangibles, $60M assumed liabilities. Allocation:
- Tangible: $40M.
- Tech IP (core model + platform): $120M, 7-yr amortisation.
- Customer relationships (enterprise contracts): $90M, 10-yr.
- Brand: $30M, 15-yr.
- Non-compete (founders): $10M, 3-yr.
- DTL (25% x $250M intangible step-up): $62.5M.
- Goodwill (residual): $500M − ($40M + $250M − $60M) + $62.5M = $232.5M.
Annual amortisation ~$33M P&L hit. Impairment risk: if churn spikes >15% or model becomes commoditised (open-source LLM commoditisation triggered $42B impairment wave in 2023-2024 per Duff & Phelps tracking), goodwill CGU could write down $80-120M.
**Tools:** Anaplan PPA module, Duff & Phelps benchmarking, Big-4 valuation teams.

### Scenario 3 - ANTI-EXAMPLE: Loose CPs + No MAC Scrape

Outcome: 14-month Delaware litigation, buyer wins partial closing at 15% discount but:
- Break-fee exposure: $8M.
- Legal fees: $6M each side.
- 14-month delay: lost $30M synergy NPV.
- Reputational impact: corp-dev head fired; two follow-on deals dropped.
**Tools that would have prevented:** Ironclad CLM (MAC template library), DealRoom VDR with CP-tracker, ABA Model SPA commentary.

**Tool stack across scenarios:** CapIQ, Bloomberg (SAST price calc), Anaplan (PPA/integration/synergy model), DealRoom VDR, Ironclad CLM, CCI Green Channel portal, Duff & Phelps valuation benchmarks.
