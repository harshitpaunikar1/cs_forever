
# Strategic Dissonance & Strategy Review

## Overview

Strategic dissonance happens when what leaders planned and what is happening on the ground don’t match. A strategy review is a structured check to decide: continue, adjust, or stop.

## Why It Matters

Companies often keep executing outdated plans. Regular reviews help catch problems early and adapt before losses grow.

## Key Principles

- Listen to frontline signals and market shifts
- Track performance vs assumptions
- Encourage honest reporting (no fear culture)
- Decide fast: fix, pivot, or exit

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Strategic dissonance** | Mismatch between strategy and reality |
| **Assumptions** | Beliefs the strategy depends on |
| **Review cadence** | How often strategy is reviewed |
| **Pivot** | A significant change in direction |

## Use Case

A company revisiting strategy after a new competitor changes the market.

## Scenario

> A consulting firm sees profits drop despite higher sales. Review finds delivery costs rising due to too many customized projects. They tighten project selection rules.

## Examples

- A food brand reviews strategy after customers shift to healthier options.
- A software firm reviews after a new pricing model disrupts the market.

# Strategy Reformulation & Measuring Success

## Overview

Strategy reformulation means changing the strategy based on learning. Measuring success means tracking the right indicators to know if the strategy is working.

## Why It Matters

Without measurement, strategy becomes guesswork. Reformulation keeps the company relevant when conditions change.

## Key Principles

- Update strategy when assumptions change
- Use both financial and non-financial measures
- Set clear targets and owners
- Avoid vanity metrics; track what drives outcomes

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Reformulation** | Redesigning strategy |
| **KPIs** | Key performance indicators |
| **Leading indicators** | Early signals (e.g., trial users) |
| **Lagging indicators** | Final results (e.g., profit) |

## Use Case

Adjusting a growth strategy when customer acquisition costs rise.

## Scenario

> An edtech firm planned growth through ads, but ad costs doubled. They reformulate to partnerships with schools and track retention + learning outcomes.

## Examples

- A retailer shifts from expansion to improving same-store sales and tracks repeat visits.
- A startup moves from B2C to B2B and tracks deal cycle time + churn.

# Exploring Options: Blue Ocean Strategy

## Overview

Blue Ocean Strategy is about creating a new market space where competition is low, instead of fighting rivals in the same crowded market (red ocean).

## Why It Matters

Competing head-to-head often leads to price wars and low profits. Blue oceans can unlock new demand and stronger margins.

## Key Principles

- Create value in a new way (not just “better”)
- Reduce/Remove features customers don’t value
- Add/Increase features customers truly value
- Target non-customers, not just existing customers

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Red ocean** | Crowded market with intense competition |
| **Blue ocean** | New market space with little competition |
| **Value innovation** | Increasing value while reducing cost |
| **Non-customers** | People not currently buying in the market |

## Use Case

A company redesigning its offering to attract new customer groups.

## Scenario

> A cinema chain creates a new “family experience” with play zones and kid-friendly timing, attracting parents who avoided cinemas earlier.

## Examples

- A gym launches 20-minute express workouts for busy office workers.
- A bank creates a simple, app-only account for first-time young earners.

# Innovation Strategy (Corporate Venturing)

## Overview

Innovation strategy is how a company creates and supports new ideas that can become future businesses. Corporate venturing means investing in or building new ventures inside/outside the firm.

## Why It Matters

Markets change quickly. Innovation creates future growth engines so the company doesn’t rely only on today’s products.

## Key Principles

- Separate “new venture” work from daily operations
- Provide funding, freedom, and clear goals
- Test fast, learn fast, scale what works
- Protect the core while exploring the new

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Corporate venturing** | Building/investing in new businesses |
| **Incubator/accelerator** | Program supporting new ideas |
| **Pilot** | Small test before full launch |
| **Ambidexterity** | Running core + innovation together |

## Use Case

A manufacturing firm creating a new EV-components startup unit.

## Scenario

> An auto company creates a small team to develop connected-car services. They test with one model, learn, then expand across the lineup.

## Examples

- A retailer launches a digital marketplace as a new venture.
- A telecom invests in a startup offering AI customer support tools.

# Knowledge Management (Creating & Sharing Know-How)

## Overview

Knowledge management means capturing what employees learn and making it easy for others to use—so the company doesn’t “forget” lessons.

## Why It Matters

When knowledge stays in people’s heads, it gets lost when they leave. Good knowledge sharing improves speed, quality, and consistency.

## Key Principles

- Convert experience into reusable guides and tools
- Encourage sharing through culture and incentives
- Use systems (databases, playbooks, communities)
- Balance documented knowledge with mentoring

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Tacit knowledge** | Know-how in people’s heads |
| **Explicit knowledge** | Documented knowledge (manuals, SOPs) |
| **Codification** | Turning experience into documents/tools |
| **Knowledge repository** | Central storage for learnings |

## Use Case

A consulting firm storing project learnings for future teams.

## Scenario

> A hospital creates checklists and case notes from senior doctors’ experience, reducing mistakes and training time for new staff.

## Examples

- A software team creates a “bug playbook” to fix recurring issues faster.
- A sales team records winning pitches and shares templates.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Framework 1: Strategic Dissonance Detection Loop
**Purpose:** Turn "something feels off" into a structured, named gap that triggers either fix, pivot, or exit.

**Text Diagram:**
```
  ┌──────────────────┐
  │ 1. ASSUMPTIONS    │ ← make them explicit in strategy wiki
  │    LIST           │
  └────────┬─────────┘
           │
           ▼
  ┌──────────────────┐
  │ 2. SIGNAL         │ ← leading indicators, qualitative signals
  │    COLLECTION     │
  └────────┬─────────┘
           │
           ▼
  ┌──────────────────┐
  │ 3. ASSUMPTION     │ ← compare actuals to assumed values
  │    HEALTH CHECK   │
  └────────┬─────────┘
           │
           ▼
  ┌──────────────────┐
  │ 4. DECISION GATE  │ ── FIX ── Incremental adjustment
  │    (Fix / Pivot / │ ── PIVOT ── Significant reformulation
  │     Exit)         │ ── EXIT ── Wind down initiative
  └──────────────────┘
```

Components:
- **Assumption List:** every strategy contains 5–15 named assumptions (market size, CAC, competitive response, pricing tolerance). Make them explicit at launch.
- **Signal Collection:** leading indicators (sign-up rate, win-rate, churn signals) + qualitative (customer interview themes, frontline-sales notes)
- **Health Check:** actual ÷ assumed for each assumption; colour-coded green/yellow/red
- **Decision Gate:** cumulative red-assumption count triggers fix/pivot/exit

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** An AI coding-assistant startup assumes (a) mid-market developer budgets approve $50/seat, (b) 40% signup-to-paid conversion, (c) 25% annual churn. After Q2: actual $35/seat approvable, 18% conversion, 38% churn. Health check: 3/3 red. Decision gate fires → **Pivot** from mid-market PLG to enterprise top-down motion.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Quarterly strategy reviews; when any senior leader says "I feel like something's wrong but can't quite name it."

### Framework 2: Three Horizons of Growth (McKinsey)
**Purpose:** Allocate capital and attention across time horizons so current business funds future growth without starving either.

**Text Diagram:**
```
       Value
         │                                       ___
         │                                    __/
         │                                 __/
         │                              __/          ← H3
  H3     │                           __/            (Transformational, 5–10 yr)
  ──────│───────────────────────__/────────────────────
         │                    __/
         │                 __/
         │              __/              ← H2
  H2     │           __/                (Adjacent, 2–5 yr)
  ──────│────────__/─────────────────────────────────
         │     __/
         │   _/                    ← H1
  H1     │ _/                     (Core, 0–2 yr)
  ──────│/───────────────────────────────────────────
         └────────────────────────────────────────────► Time

   Typical capital allocation: 70% H1 | 20% H2 | 10% H3
```

Components:
- **H1 (Core):** extend and defend today's business; maintain margin
- **H2 (Adjacent):** emerging businesses that extend the core in adjacent markets/products
- **H3 (Transformational):** options / bets that may or may not become future core

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A $200M ARR SaaS allocates:
- H1: Core observability product → 70% of $60M R&D = $42M
- H2: Security-data-lake adjacency (launching Q2) → 20% = $12M
- H3: Autonomous-agent platform (research stage) → 10% = $6M

Capital-allocation health check: if H2+H3 < 25%, company is short on future engines; if >40%, core is being starved. 30/70 is the typical red-line.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Annual planning; when the CFO pushes to cut "non-core" spend; when Board asks "what's next."

### Framework 3: Ambidextrous Organisation Structure (Tushman–O'Reilly)
**Purpose:** Design the org so exploration (new ventures) doesn't get crushed by exploitation (core operations), while still sharing where useful.

**Text Diagram:**
```
              ┌───────────── CEO / CORPORATE ─────────────┐
              │                                            │
              │                                            │
      ┌──────▼────────┐                      ┌────────────▼─────────┐
      │  EXPLOIT UNIT  │                      │  EXPLORE UNIT        │
      │  (Core biz)    │                      │  (Corp-venture,      │
      │                │     Share what       │   CVC, incubator)    │
      │  - Efficiency  │  helps (brand,       │  - Experimentation   │
      │  - Predictable │   customers,         │  - Flexibility       │
      │  - Scale       │   capital)           │  - Tolerance for     │
      │  - KPIs        │ ◄──────────────────► │    failure           │
      │                │                      │                      │
      │  Metrics:      │                      │  Metrics:            │
      │  Revenue/cost  │                      │  Learning/validation │
      └────────────────┘                      └──────────────────────┘
```

Components:
- **Exploit unit:** optimised for efficiency; standard OKRs; predictable budgets
- **Explore unit:** optimised for learning; stage-gate funding; different metrics (learning velocity, not revenue)
- **Corporate layer:** shares what helps (brand, customers, capital) while shielding explore from core's efficiency culture

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A large enterprise SaaS sets up an AI-agents explore unit.
- Explore unit: separate P&L, separate hiring budget, OKRs based on design-partner wins and model benchmarks, not ARR
- Exploit unit: standard SaaS metrics (NRR, ARR growth, CAC payback)
- Shared: brand, enterprise-customer relationships, cloud infrastructure
- Protected: separate engineering pipeline, separate product-marketing narrative

After 18 months, Explore unit reaches product-market fit and graduates to H1 via a re-merger or stays separate as H2. Dedicated structure prevented core's efficiency pressure from killing the exploration early.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When an innovation programme stalls; when core-unit leaders complain about "wasted spend" on explore; when explore-team attrition spikes because of integration pressure.

### Framework 4: SECI Knowledge Conversion Cycle (Nonaka)
**Purpose:** Operationalise how tacit ↔ explicit knowledge flows so the firm actually learns instead of losing knowledge on attrition.

**Text Diagram:**
```
            TO → TACIT            TO → EXPLICIT
         ┌─────────────────┬─────────────────────┐
 FROM    │                  │                      │
 TACIT   │  SOCIALIZATION   │  EXTERNALIZATION     │
         │  (apprenticeship,│  (writing, recording,│
         │   shadowing)     │   diagrams)          │
         ├─────────────────┼─────────────────────┤
 FROM    │                  │                      │
 EXPLICIT│  INTERNALIZATION │  COMBINATION         │
         │  (learning-by-   │  (docs combined into │
         │   doing, drills) │   playbooks, wikis)  │
         └─────────────────┴─────────────────────┘
```

Components:
- **Socialization (T→T):** apprenticeship, pairing — sharing tacit directly
- **Externalization (T→E):** the hardest mode — turning experience into articulated docs
- **Combination (E→E):** merging docs into richer playbooks, courses
- **Internalization (E→T):** practising docs until they become intuitive

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A consulting firm with a new AI-transformation practice.
- Socialization: pair junior consultants with senior leads on design-partner engagements
- Externalization: after every engagement, senior must author a 4-page "pattern brief" (mandatory)
- Combination: quarterly, the knowledge-management team merges pattern briefs into a living playbook
- Internalization: mandatory playbook practice in new-hire bootcamps; knowledge embedded back into tacit judgement

KPI: pattern-brief count per senior per quarter (target 2); playbook version number; time-to-productive-deployment for new hires (target 90 days).

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Knowledge-management initiatives; consulting firm practice-area reviews; any time senior attrition is leaving a capability gap.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Assumption Health Check
**Formula:** `Health(assumption) = Actual Value / Assumed Value`

**Variables:**
- Assumed Value = the number in the plan (CAC, win rate, market size, pricing tolerance)
- Actual Value = measured result today

**Why this formula exists:** Strategy is a set of bets on assumptions. If an assumption is wildly off, the strategy built on top of it is broken, regardless of how well you executed.

**How to interpret the output:**
- Ratio 0.85–1.15 → Green (within tolerance)
- 0.65–0.85 or 1.15–1.35 → Yellow (investigate)
- < 0.65 or > 1.35 → Red (assumption broken; reformulate)

**Worked example:** AI SaaS's assumptions Q2:
- CAC: Assumed $800 / Actual $1,400 → ratio 1.75 → Red (severe)
- Win-rate: Assumed 22% / Actual 19% → ratio 0.86 → Yellow
- Churn: Assumed 8% / Actual 14% → ratio 1.75 → Red (severe)

Three assumptions broken → strategy reformulation triggered.

**Data source:** CAC from marketing analytics (HubSpot, Salesforce, Mixpanel ROI dashboards). Win rate from CRM. Churn from Amplitude / ChartMogul.

---

### Formula 2: Three-Horizons Capital Allocation Test
**Formula:** `Horizon Balance Score = |H1% − 70| + |H2% − 20| + |H3% − 10|`

**Variables:**
- Percentages of R&D or growth-capex allocated to each horizon
- Target: 70 / 20 / 10 (benchmark; adjust to industry)

**Why this formula exists:** Over-investment in H1 leaves no future engine; over-investment in H3 starves the present.

**How to interpret the output:**
- Score < 10 → well-balanced portfolio
- 10–25 → minor imbalance; tune
- \> 25 → material imbalance; re-plan

**Worked example:** A SaaS company allocates 92% to H1 (core product maintenance), 5% to H2, 3% to H3.
- Balance Score = |92−70| + |5−20| + |3−10| = 22 + 15 + 7 = **44** → heavy imbalance.

Decision: Shift $10M of H1 maintenance budget toward H2 (security adjacency) and H3 (agentic workflows) research. Target new split: 75 / 17 / 8 (score = 12).

**Data source:** R&D budget breakdown from finance; innovation-portfolio categorisation from corporate strategy. Review quarterly.

---

### Formula 3: Innovation Portfolio Expected Value
**Formula:** `EV(portfolio) = Σᵢ Pᵢ × Vᵢ − Cᵢ`, where for each venture i: Pᵢ = probability of success, Vᵢ = NPV if successful, Cᵢ = current cost

**Variables:**
- Pᵢ = success probability (calibrated; typical: H1 = 0.8, H2 = 0.4, H3 = 0.1)
- Vᵢ = NPV on success
- Cᵢ = current capital committed

**Why this formula exists:** Lets you compare a safe H1 improvement against a moonshot H3 on risk-adjusted terms.

**How to interpret the output:**
- EV > 0 → keep in portfolio
- EV < 0 but strategic optionality high → may still keep (real options)
- EV < 0 AND no optionality → kill

**Worked example:** Three ventures in a CVC portfolio:
- Agentic SaaS (H3): P=0.10, V=$500M, C=$20M → EV = 50 − 20 = $30M
- Security adjacency (H2): P=0.40, V=$80M, C=$15M → EV = 32 − 15 = $17M
- Core modernisation (H1): P=0.85, V=$30M, C=$10M → EV = 25.5 − 10 = $15.5M

Top-EV bet is H3 despite low probability. Keep all three; prioritise attention on H3 milestone gates.

**Data source:** Success probabilities calibrated with Tetlock-style forecasting reviews (quarterly). NPVs from FP&A model; costs from committed budget.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Run annual strategy offsite then silence for 11 months | Run quarterly assumption-health reviews; red flag ≥ 2 broken assumptions triggers formal reformulation |
| Track only lagging indicators | Pair every lagging KPI with 1–2 leading indicators with known lead times |
| Bury "dissonance" feedback as cultural noise | Have frontline escalation channels: red-flag email alias, monthly-town-hall signal item |
| Fund H3 innovation with H1 efficiency KPIs | Dedicated explore-unit P&L with learning-velocity metrics (not ARR) |
| Put innovation inside the core business unit | Separate explore unit with different budget, metrics, and hiring profile |
| Rely on tribal knowledge | Mandate pattern briefs after major engagements; quarterly playbook updates |
| Build knowledge-management wiki no one reads | Measure usage (searches, edits, reuse); retire unused content; gamify contribution |
| Define "pivot" retroactively | Name the assumption that broke and show evidence before announcing the pivot |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: SaaS Company Detecting a Strategic Inflection Point
**Situation:** A $150M ARR SaaS for customer support discovers its main competitor launched an AI-agent product that resolves 60% of tickets without humans. The firm's strategy ("better human agent tooling") is suddenly dissonant.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Strategic Dissonance Loop + Assumption Health Check.

**Analysis:**
Assumption audit:
- "Human agent headcount grows with ticket volume" — ratio 0.7 (customers reducing heads) → Red
- "Ticket volume grows 15% YoY" — ratio 0.8 → Yellow
- "AI automation is complementary, not substitution" — ratio 0.3 → Deep Red

Three assumptions broken including the core one. Decision gate fires → **Pivot**.

**Decision rule:** When the *core* strategic assumption hits Red, it's a Strategic Inflection Point — reformulate strategy, don't adjust.

**Action (Monday morning):** Convene 2-week strategy reformulation sprint. Propose new positioning: "AI-native customer operations platform" (agent + automation unified). Announce H3 program to research agentic products. Signal board within 30 days.

### Scenario 2: Consulting Firm Building Knowledge Capability
**Situation:** A 400-consultant firm loses 3 senior partners; onboarding of 50 new consultants is stalling; client-satisfaction scores dropping.

**Applicable framework/metric:** SECI + Community of Practice.

**Analysis:**
- Current state: Socialization happening informally. No externalization pipeline. Playbooks outdated (last updated 2 years ago). Internalization assumed.
- KPI gap: new-hire time-to-productive = 6 months vs target 3 months. Senior-partner knowledge transfer informal.

SECI interventions:
- Socialization: structured apprenticeship pairing; mandated shadow hours
- Externalization: pattern-brief mandate (2 per quarter per partner)
- Combination: dedicated knowledge-management lead; quarterly playbook sprint
- Internalization: new-hire bootcamp redesign around playbook; drills

**Decision rule:** If new-hire time-to-productive > 4 months, SECI cycle is broken; target the weakest mode.

**Action:** Appoint KM lead; launch pattern-brief system; run first playbook sprint in Q3 with 10 senior partners. Target: new-hire productive in 3 months by Q2 next year; senior-partner attrition risk contained via codification.

---

### Scenario 3 (Anti-example): Innovation Strangled by Core-Unit Metrics
**Situation:** An enterprise software company's AI explore unit sits inside the core product org, reports to the SVP of Product, and is evaluated on quarterly ARR contribution.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Ambidextrous Organisation Structure + Three Horizons.

**Analysis (what goes wrong):**
- Explore unit measured on ARR → product team skips long-horizon research in favour of tactical features
- Core-unit hiring processes pick incumbents' profiles, not research-oriented talent
- Quarterly review pressure compresses the timeline → no experiments run to completion
- After 18 months, "innovation unit" has produced 2 features, no new venture, and the best researchers have left

**Cost of this mistake:** 18 months lost; $14M burned; 6 top-tier researchers lost; competitor launches a category-defining product.

**Decision rule:** H3 work must NOT be inside core P&L; separate unit with learning metrics.

**Action:** Restructure: move explore unit to a separate reporting line; change metrics to learning-velocity (design-partner wins, model benchmarks); allow 24-month horizon. Guard against core-unit poaching.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. **Maintain an assumption list in the strategy wiki** — every strategy doc ends with 5–15 explicit assumptions, each with owner, measurement method, and health check cadence.
2. **Set a quarterly strategy-review cadence** — 2-hour exec session; agenda: assumption health, leading indicators, decisions (fix/pivot/exit).
3. **Build a Three-Horizons capital-allocation dashboard** — Tableau view showing R&D / growth-capex split across H1/H2/H3; target range highlighted.
4. **Stand up an explore unit with its own P&L** — separate hiring budget, separate metrics (learning-velocity, not ARR), dedicated leadership.
5. **Institutionalise pattern briefs** — mandatory after every senior-led engagement; 3–4 pages; submitted to knowledge-management system within 2 weeks.
6. **Run quarterly playbook sprints** — pull pattern briefs + raw learnings, consolidate into versioned playbooks, publish via Notion/Confluence.
7. **Build a communities of practice network** — 4–6 CoPs across key capabilities; quarterly show-and-tells; measure participation.
8. **Wire leading indicators into the KPI dashboard** — every lagging metric has an annotation showing the leading indicator that precedes it and by how long.
