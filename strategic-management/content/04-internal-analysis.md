
# Internal Analysis: RBV and Core Competencies

## Overview

RBV says a company wins because of its resources and capabilities (what it has and what it can do well). Core competencies are the few strengths that truly make it special.

## Why It Matters

When you know your strongest capabilities, you can build strategy around them instead of copying competitors.

## Key Principles

- Focus on strengths that are valuable and rare
- Build skills that are hard to copy
- Combine resources into strong capabilities
- Invest in learning and improvement

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Resource** | Asset (money, brand, patents, people) |
| **Capability** | Ability to use resources well |
| **Core Competence** | Key strength that drives success |
| **VRIO** | Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Organized (test for advantage) |

## Use Case

A company identifies its core competence in logistics and expands into fast delivery services.

## Scenario

> A dairy brand realizes its core strength is cold-chain distribution. It enters premium ice cream because it can deliver better than rivals.

## Examples

- A firm with strong data science capability wins in personalized recommendations.
- A company with a trusted brand launches new products more easily.

# Value Chain Analysis

## Overview

A value chain breaks a business into steps (like buying materials, making product, marketing, delivery, service) to see where value is created and where costs are high.

## Why It Matters

It shows exactly where a company can reduce cost or improve customer value. Small improvements in key steps can create big advantage.

## Key Principles

- Map each activity from start to after-sales
- Find cost drivers and value drivers
- Strengthen activities that matter most to customers
- Outsource activities that are not critical (if sensible)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Primary Activities** | Inbound, operations, outbound, marketing, service |
| **Support Activities** | HR, tech, procurement, infrastructure |
| **Cost Driver** | What increases cost in an activity |
| **Margin** | Value created minus total cost |

## Use Case

A manufacturing firm redesigns operations to reduce defects and improve delivery time.

## Scenario

> An e-commerce firm finds last-mile delivery is expensive. It creates neighborhood pickup points to reduce cost and improve reliability.

## Examples

- A restaurant improves value by speeding up kitchen operations.
- A smartphone brand improves service centers to increase loyalty.

# Competitor Analysis

## Overview

Competitor analysis means understanding rivals: what they want, what they can do, and how they may react to your moves.

## Why It Matters

Many strategies fail because companies ignore competitor reactions. Knowing rivals helps you choose smarter moves and avoid price wars.

## Key Principles

- Identify direct and indirect competitors
- Study competitor goals, strengths, and weak points
- Predict likely responses to your strategy
- Track competitor signals (pricing, hiring, ads, partnerships)

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Strategic Group** | Firms with similar strategies in an industry |
| **Benchmarking** | Comparing with competitors’ performance |
| **Competitor Response** | How rivals react to your move |
| **Market Share** | Portion of total sales in a market |

## Use Case

A new smartphone brand predicts competitor price cuts and prepares alternative bundles.

## Scenario

> A biscuit company plans to enter a new region. Competitor analysis shows the local leader wins on distribution, so the new firm partners with strong distributors first.

## Examples

- A ride-hailing app anticipates discount wars and instead focuses on driver loyalty.
- A bank watches competitor branch closures and shifts to digital-first growth.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Three interlocking lenses on the firm itself: Resource-Based View (RBV) + VRIO tests which internal capabilities can sustain advantage; Value Chain decomposes operations to find where margin is won or lost; Competitor Analysis maps rivals' goals, capabilities, and likely reactions. For an IT/AI/Product/Consulting leader this is how you decide which engineering capabilities to invest in, which activities to outsource, and how a competitor will react when you ship. Decision it helps make: *"Given our resources and our rivals' likely moves, where do we invest, where do we outsource, and where do we pick a fight?"*

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Framework 1: VRIO Decision Flow
**Purpose:** Test whether a resource/capability delivers *sustained* competitive advantage — not just parity.

**Text Diagram:**
```
  Start with resource/capability
             │
             ▼
     ┌───────────────┐  No ─► Competitive Disadvantage
     │  VALUABLE?    │
     └───────┬───────┘
         Yes │
             ▼
     ┌───────────────┐  No ─► Competitive Parity
     │  RARE?        │
     └───────┬───────┘
         Yes │
             ▼
     ┌───────────────┐  No ─► Temporary Advantage
     │  INIMITABLE?  │
     └───────┬───────┘
         Yes │
             ▼
     ┌───────────────┐  No ─► Unexploited Advantage (wasted)
     │  ORGANISED?   │
     └───────┬───────┘
         Yes │
             ▼
     SUSTAINED COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE
```

Components:
- **Valuable:** helps exploit opportunity / neutralise threat
- **Rare:** few competitors have it
- **Inimitable:** blocked by history, causal ambiguity, social complexity, legal protection
- **Organised:** processes, incentives, and structure in place to extract the value

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** An enterprise-AI startup has: (a) a proprietary evaluation dataset built from 3 years of design-partner work, (b) a generic fine-tuning pipeline available to anyone on open-source.
- Dataset: Valuable Y, Rare Y, Inimitable Y (accumulated over time with exclusive contracts), Organised Y → **sustained advantage**.
- Pipeline: Valuable Y, Rare N → parity. Don't market it as a differentiator.

Decision: invest in dataset expansion (add 10 new design partners with exclusivity clauses); do not invest in pipeline as a differentiator.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Any time someone names a "competitive strength." Force them through VRIO before letting it anchor a strategy.

### Framework 2: Porter's Value Chain
**Purpose:** Decompose the firm into activities; locate where margin is won and where it leaks.

**Text Diagram:**
```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    FIRM INFRASTRUCTURE                              │
│                    (finance, legal, strategy)                       │  S
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤  u
│                    HUMAN RESOURCE MANAGEMENT                        │  p
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤  p
│                    TECHNOLOGY DEVELOPMENT (R&D, IT, AI)             │  o
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤  r
│                    PROCUREMENT                                      │  t
├───────────┬───────────┬───────────┬───────────────┬─────────────────┤──────
│  Inbound  │Operations │ Outbound  │  Marketing &  │     Service     │ M
│ Logistics │           │ Logistics │     Sales     │                 │ A
│           │           │           │               │                 │ R
│  (data    │  (model   │  (API /   │  (PLG,        │  (customer      │ G
│   ingest) │   train)  │   app)    │   sales)      │   success)      │ I
│           │           │           │               │                 │ N
└───────────┴───────────┴───────────┴───────────────┴─────────────────┴──────
                     ────── PRIMARY ACTIVITIES ──────
```

Components:
- **Primary (5):** Inbound logistics, Operations, Outbound logistics, Marketing & sales, Service
- **Support (4):** Firm infrastructure, HRM, Technology development, Procurement
- For each: cost drivers (volume, scale, learning, linkages) and value drivers (differentiation levers)

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** An AI SaaS product maps its value chain:
- Inbound logistics → data ingestion pipeline. Cost: 8% of revenue.
- Operations → model training & inference. Cost: 35% of revenue. Primary cost driver = GPU utilisation.
- Outbound logistics → API delivery. Cost: 6%.
- Marketing & sales → PLG + inside sales. Cost: 30%.
- Service → customer success. Cost: 8%.
- Support (HR, tech, infra, procurement): 10%.
- Margin: 3%.

Diagnosis: Operations (GPU spend) and Marketing (CAC) together are 65% of cost. Action: (a) move to spot GPU capacity + distilled smaller models → cut ops by 30%; (b) double PLG funnel conversion via product activations → cut S&M payback period by 40%.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Margin compression review, unit-economics debate, buy-vs-build decisions.

### Framework 3: Strategic Group Map
**Purpose:** Cluster competitors on two strategy dimensions to visualise who truly competes with whom and where white spaces exist.

**Text Diagram:**
```
         BREADTH OF OFFERING
               WIDE
          ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
          │                                  │
   HIGH   │  [Microsoft]  [Google]           │
          │                                  │
  PRICE/  │  [Salesforce]                    │
  POSITION│                                  │
          │                                  │
          │     [Zoho]                       │
          │                 [HubSpot]        │
   LOW    │  [Mom-and-pop SaaS]              │
          │                                  │
          └──────────────────────────────────┘
               NARROW              BREADTH
```

Components:
- **Axes:** pick two strategic dimensions (price, breadth, integration depth, geography, customer segment, tech sophistication)
- **Clusters:** firms that compete most directly sit in the same region
- **White space:** an unoccupied region of the map = potential positioning

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A vertical-AI founder maps GenAI coding assistants on axes (model sophistication × IDE integration depth). Incumbents cluster in high-soph / high-integration (GitHub Copilot, Cursor). White space: high-soph / low-integration CLI-first tool for DevOps engineers. Strategy: enter the white space; don't go head-on vs Copilot.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Before defining positioning or picking which competitors to benchmark against. Forces honest identification of "who actually takes our deals."

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: VRIO Composite Score
**Formula:** `VRIO Score = V × R × I × O`, each dimension 0 or 1 (or 0–1 scaled for nuance).

**Variables:**
- V = Valuable (1 if enables revenue or blocks threat, else 0)
- R = Rare (1 if fewer than ~5 peers hold equivalent resource, else 0)
- I = Inimitable (1 if time-to-replicate > 3 years or legal/contractual block, else 0)
- O = Organised (1 if processes, incentives, governance actively exploit it, else 0)

**Why this formula exists:** Multiplicative structure forces all four — missing any dimension zeros out the advantage. Additive averages hide failures (0 R with 1,1,1 others averages 0.75; VRIO product correctly = 0).

**How to interpret the output:**
- Product = 0 → not an advantage (fix the missing dimension or deprioritise)
- Product = 1 (all Y) → sustained advantage candidate — protect, invest, and don't disclose unnecessarily
- Scaled variant (0–1 continuous) → any dimension < 0.5 signals a fix target

**Worked example:** Strong ML team at a B2B SaaS:
- V = 1 (drives retention via personalisation)
- R = 0 (every competitor has an ML team)
- I = 0 (talent is mobile)
- O = 1

Product = 0 → **competitive parity only**. Don't market "our ML team" as a moat. Redirect storytelling to something that scores 1×1×1×1 (say, 6 years of proprietary customer-interaction data).

**Data source:** Resource inventory from internal audit (HR for people, IP/Legal for patents, CTO for data/IP, CFO for financial assets). VRIO scoring via calibrated exec panel (3-person minimum). Stored in a "Capability Register" Notion database or Airtable.

---

### Formula 2: Activity Margin Contribution
**Formula:** `Activity Margin Contribution = (Value Added by activity) − (Cost of activity)`, as % of firm revenue.

**Variables:**
- Value Added = customer willingness-to-pay attributable to the activity (estimated via conjoint, ablation, or customer survey)
- Cost of activity = fully loaded cost including allocated overhead

**Why this formula exists:** Tells you *which activity* to invest in (high value add, low cost) or kill/outsource (low value add, high cost). Classic make-vs-buy input.

**How to interpret the output:**
- Contribution > 10% of revenue → core; invest, protect, in-source
- Contribution 3–10% → supporting; optimise cost
- Contribution < 3% → candidate for outsourcing/automation
- Contribution < 0 → activity destroys value; fix or kill

**Worked example:** AI-infra firm analyses its "on-prem deployment" activity.
- Value added: Enterprise customers pay $200k/yr premium for on-prem option → $4M revenue from 20 enterprise accounts attributable.
- Cost: Dedicated deployment team of 8 engineers, infra tooling, travel: $3.2M/yr.
- Contribution = +$0.8M, ~2% of revenue.

Decision: contribution is low and trend is flat. Action: convert on-prem into a self-serve Kubernetes operator (one-time $1M investment) to reduce activity cost to $0.8M/yr → contribution rises to $3.2M (8% of revenue).

**Data source:** Costs from finance ERP (NetSuite, SAP). Revenue attribution from CRM (Salesforce) tagged by feature usage. Value added via customer research (Gong, UserVoice) + conjoint studies.

---

### Formula 3: Competitor Response Probability
**Formula:** `P(response) = f(Commitment, Awareness, Motivation, Capability)` — Chen's AMC framework.

**Variables (each scored 0–1):**
- Awareness = does competitor notice our move?
- Motivation = does the move threaten something they value?
- Capability = can they respond credibly in time?
- Commitment = our move's visibility / size / irreversibility (we weight this)

**Why this formula exists:** Unseen competitor reactions ruin strategies. This forces explicit prediction before execution.

**How to interpret the output:**
- P < 0.3 → low response risk; proceed
- P 0.3–0.6 → moderate; prepare one counter-move
- P > 0.6 → high; pre-empt with decoys, timing, or bundling

**Worked example:** A mid-sized IT consulting firm plans a 20% discount on AI transformation retainers to win share from Accenture.
- Awareness = 0.9 (public pricing page)
- Motivation = 0.6 (Accenture won't fight for small deals, but will flag repeat ones)
- Capability = 0.9 (Accenture can price-match easily)
- Commitment (our side) = 0.7 (public, hard to roll back)

Back-of-envelope: if any of A/M/C is high, response is likely. Product ~0.49 → moderate. Action: retitle offer as "AI transformation sprint" (not a pricing discount); make it time-bound; avoid triggering a public response.

**Data source:** Competitor moves tracked via Crayon, Klue, SimilarWeb, job-board scraping, earnings transcripts. Stored in a competitive-intel wiki.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| List strengths with no VRIO filter | Every claimed strength passes VRIO before entering the strategy doc |
| Celebrate "our great team" as a moat | Identify the *specific isolating mechanism* (social complexity, causal ambiguity, contract, IP) |
| Map the value chain once and file it | Refresh quarterly with updated cost and value-add figures per activity |
| Outsource based on cost alone | Outsource only if the activity's value-add is low AND the activity is not a moat input |
| Benchmark against every competitor | Benchmark against the strategic group you actually compete in |
| Launch moves without modelling competitor response | Run AMC on every major move >$500k before shipping |
| Confuse market share with advantage | Market share without ROIC > WACC is vanity; pair the metrics |
| Treat capability audits as HR exercises | Run VRIO in a joint strategy+HR+CTO session with data, not opinions |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: AI Product Company Deciding What to Open-Source
**Situation:** A 3-year-old AI platform company must decide whether to open-source its orchestration framework to grow developer mindshare, or keep it closed as a moat.

**Applicable framework/metric:** VRIO scoring + Activity Margin Contribution + Competitor Response (AMC).

**Analysis:**
Orchestration framework VRIO:
- V = 1 (enables enterprise customers)
- R = 0.4 (LangChain, LlamaIndex exist)
- I = 0.3 (approach is replicable within 6–9 months)
- O = 1 (we run it in production at scale)

Product = 0.12 → weak moat. Not a sustained advantage.

Proprietary evaluation dataset VRIO:
- V = 1, R = 1 (design-partner exclusive), I = 1 (3 years of accumulation + contracts), O = 1.
- Product = 1.0 → sustained advantage.

**Decision rule:** Open-source components with VRIO product < 0.3; retain components ≥ 0.7.

**Action (Monday morning):** Announce open-source of orchestration framework (capture developer mindshare, signal to market). Keep eval dataset and fine-tuned models closed. Update positioning: "open framework, closed data moat."

---

### Scenario 2: Consulting Firm Deciding to In-Source Design
**Situation:** A 500-person IT consulting firm currently outsources UX design to boutique agencies. Partners debate in-sourcing.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Value Chain Activity Margin + VRIO.

**Analysis:**
Current state:
- Design activity cost (outsourced): $8M/yr; value added via uplift in deal win-rate estimated $6M.
- Contribution = -$2M.

In-source hypothesis:
- In-house team cost: $5M/yr; value added: $9M (tighter coordination, faster iteration on pitches, RFP win-rate +8 pts).
- Contribution = +$4M.

VRIO on in-house design capability:
- V = 1 (drives win rate)
- R = 0.5 (some competitors have in-house design)
- I = 0.6 (hard to replicate organisational memory across consulting projects)
- O = 1 (can integrate into existing engagement model)

**Decision rule:** In-source activities where contribution flips from negative to ≥$3M AND VRIO product > 0.2.

**Action:** Approve in-sourcing. Hire 15 designers in Q3. Phase out boutique contracts over 6 months. Track monthly: design spend, deal-win rate, pitch cycle time.

### Scenario 3 (Anti-example): Naming Culture as a Moat Without VRIO
**Situation:** A late-stage startup's board deck lists "our unique culture" as a top-3 competitive advantage, justifying a high talent acquisition spend.

**Applicable framework/metric:** VRIO + Causal Ambiguity test.

**Analysis (what goes wrong):**
- V = 1 (culture arguably drives retention and output)
- R = 0.3 (many startups claim similar culture)
- I = 0.5 (hard to copy but not impossible; Spotify, Netflix have published playbooks)
- O = uncertain (culture is often the *least* measured/managed capability)

Product ≈ 0.15. Weak moat claim. But $20M was spent on talent acquisition justified partly by "culture."

**Cost of this mistake:** 18 months later, three key leaders leave, culture "erodes," and the "moat" evaporates. Board mark-down, missed Series D projections.

**Decision rule:** No capability enters the moat section of the deck without VRIO ≥ 0.5.

**Action:** Remove "culture" as a standalone moat claim. Rewrite as "engineering-specific onboarding + on-call rotation that converts mid-level hires to senior operators 40% faster than peer companies — measured, documented, repeatable." That is specific enough to test.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. **Build a Capability Register in Notion or Airtable** — one row per capability, columns for V/R/I/O scores, evidence, owner, next review date. Rows < 0.5 product are de-emphasised in external messaging.
2. **Map your value chain on a whiteboard, then digitise** — activity by activity, cost % of revenue, value-add % of revenue, owner, cost driver, value driver. Review quarterly in Ops QBR.
3. **Run a joint VRIO session once a year** — Strategy lead + CTO + Head of HR + Head of Product. Requires evidence, not opinion. Two-hour block. Capture results in strategy wiki.
4. **Publish a "Moat Log"** — every claim of competitive advantage must cite the VRIO row. No citation = claim removed from deck.
5. **Build a competitor tracker with AMC fields** — one row per competitor, one row per anticipated move of yours. Use Crayon/Klue feeds. Review monthly.
6. **Run war-games before moves >$500k** — 90-minute session with a "Red Team" playing the competitor. Predict response probability and cost of counter-moves.
7. **Strategic group map poster** — 2-axis map on the wall (or Miro board). Update whenever a new entrant exceeds $5M ARR or a competitor repositions.
8. **Link value-chain activity outsourcing decisions to the capability register** — never outsource an activity whose underlying capability scores VRIO ≥ 0.5.
