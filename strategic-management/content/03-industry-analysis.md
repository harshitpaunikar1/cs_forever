
# Industry Analysis: Porter's Five Forces

## Overview

Porter’s Five Forces helps understand how tough an industry is by checking five pressures: rivals, new entrants, substitutes, supplier power, and buyer power.

## Why It Matters

Even a great company struggles in a very harsh industry. This tool helps decide where profits are likely and what risks exist.

## Key Principles

- Industry structure shapes long-term profitability
- Look beyond direct competitors
- Power relationships matter (buyers/suppliers)
- Use the analysis to choose positioning

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Rivalry** | Competition intensity among existing firms |
| **New Entrants** | New players entering the market |
| **Substitutes** | Different products solving the same need |
| **Buyer Power** | Customers’ ability to force low prices |
| **Supplier Power** | Suppliers’ ability to raise prices |

## Use Case

An entrepreneur checks Five Forces before opening a new restaurant chain.

## Scenario

> A new bottled water brand finds high rivalry, many substitutes (soft drinks, filters), strong retail buyer power, and moderate supplier power—so it decides to focus on niche health positioning.

## Examples

- Airlines: high rivalry + high buyer power + substitutes (trains) → tough profitability.
- Luxury cosmetics: stronger branding reduces buyer switching → better profitability.

# Applying Five Forces (Industry Understanding)

## Overview

This is the practical step: you use Five Forces on a specific industry and then decide what strategy makes sense.

## Why It Matters

Frameworks are useful only when they lead to better decisions—pricing, differentiation, partnerships, or market entry.

## Key Principles

- Define the industry clearly (what is included/excluded)
- Use real evidence (prices, switching costs, number of rivals)
- Identify the strongest forces first
- Decide strategic actions to reduce pressures

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Industry Boundaries** | What products/markets you include |
| **Switching Cost** | Difficulty of changing brands |
| **Complementors** | Players who increase your product value (e.g., app developers for phones) |
| **Strategic Action** | A move to improve position |

## Use Case

A cola company studies rivalry and substitutes to decide whether to push health drinks.

## Scenario

> A streaming service finds strong rivalry and many substitutes. It responds by locking exclusive content and improving personalization to reduce switching.

## Examples

- Telecom firm bundles services to reduce buyer switching.
- Food delivery app forms restaurant partnerships to limit rivalry impact.

## 1. Topic Snapshot
Porter's Five Forces diagnoses *why* an industry is profitable (or not) by measuring five structural pressures: rivalry among incumbents, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, buyer power, and supplier power. A sixth force — complementors — is often added for platform/ecosystem contexts. For an IT/AI/Product/Consulting leader, this is the tool that decides whether to enter a vertical, how to price, and where to build moats. Decision it helps make: *"Is this industry structurally attractive to compete in, and which force do I need to neutralise first?"*

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Framework 1: Porter's Five Forces Diagram
**Purpose:** Map the five structural pressures on an industry; score each; act on the strongest.

**Text Diagram:**
```
                        ┌──────────────────────┐
                        │  THREAT OF NEW       │
                        │  ENTRANTS            │
                        │  (Entry Barriers)    │
                        └──────────┬───────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼
    ┌───────────────┐      ┌──────────────────────┐      ┌───────────────┐
    │  SUPPLIER     │────►│   INDUSTRY            │◄────│    BUYER      │
    │  POWER        │      │   RIVALRY            │      │    POWER      │
    │               │      │   (core)             │      │               │
    └───────────────┘      └──────────┬───────────┘      └───────────────┘
                                      │
                                      ▼
                        ┌──────────────────────┐
                        │  THREAT OF           │
                        │  SUBSTITUTES         │
                        └──────────────────────┘
```

Components (each scored 1–5, where 5 = maximum pressure):
- **Rivalry:** # of comparable competitors, growth rate, product differentiation, exit barriers, fixed-cost intensity
- **New Entrants:** capital intensity, scale economies, regulation, incumbent retaliation credibility
- **Substitutes:** price-performance of alternatives, switching friction, buyer propensity to switch
- **Buyer Power:** concentration, price sensitivity, backward-integration threat, switching cost to buyer
- **Supplier Power:** concentration, switching cost to you, forward-integration threat, uniqueness

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** An AI-wrapper SaaS building on OpenAI's API:
- Rivalry: 5 (100+ similar tools; low differentiation)
- New Entrants: 5 (two engineers + API key = MVP in a weekend)
- Substitutes: 4 (open-source models, direct API use)
- Buyer Power: 3 (SMB buyers, low concentration)
- **Supplier Power: 5** (OpenAI controls pricing, rate limits, T&Cs)
- Composite intensity: 22/25 → structurally unattractive without moats.

Decision: neutralise the strongest force (supplier dependency) by building a model-agnostic abstraction layer + fine-tuned proprietary models, or exit to a vertical where switching costs can be built.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Market-entry decisions, pricing-strategy reviews, any time the team asks "should we build in this space?"

```
                     └──────────┬───────────┘
                                │
                    ┌───────────┴───────────┐
                    ▼                       ▼
             [ Five Forces continue as before ]
```

Components:
- **Complementors:** third parties whose offering increases your value proposition (app developers for iOS; data vendors for an analytics platform; system integrators for enterprise software)
- Evaluated on: ecosystem size, engagement, lock-in to your platform, bargaining power

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A low-code platform strategist. Traditional Five Forces shows rivalry is extreme. But complementor count (template builders, SI partners, marketplace apps) is growing 40% YoY. The complementor flywheel becomes the primary moat — the strategy becomes "invest in developer relations and marketplace revenue share."

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Platform strategy, marketplace design, developer-ecosystem investment decisions.

### Framework 3: Force-Intensity Scoring Matrix
**Purpose:** Move from a qualitative "five boxes" to a quantified, prioritised heatmap that drives action.

**Text Diagram:**
```
┌───────────────┬─────┬─────────────────────────────────┬────────────┐
│ Force         │Score│ Evidence                        │ Action     │
├───────────────┼─────┼─────────────────────────────────┼────────────┤
│ Rivalry       │ 5   │ 100+ tools; 30% price erosion/yr│ Vertical   │
│ New Entrants  │ 5   │ $50M seed in AI wrappers 2025  │ Network    │
│ Substitutes   │ 4   │ OSS Llama variants available    │ effects    │
│ Buyer Power   │ 3   │ Fragmented SMB buyer base       │ Lock-in via│
│ Supplier Power│ 5   │ OpenAI rate limits + T&C changes│ abstraction│
└───────────────┴─────┴─────────────────────────────────┴────────────┘
  Score 1=benign, 5=existential. Composite = sum/25 = industry attractiveness inverse.
```

Components:
- **Score (1–5):** intensity of the force
- **Evidence:** concrete data points (not opinion)
- **Action:** strategic move mapped to that force

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** Same AI-wrapper case. Heatmap reveals supplier and rivalry are both 5. Action plan: (a) supplier → build abstraction layer and test open-source fallback within 2 quarters; (b) rivalry → pick a vertical (say, healthcare coding) and go deep rather than horizontal.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Any Porter analysis that produced narrative but no action. This forces the translation step.

## 4. Formulas

### Formula 1: Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) — measure of rivalry/concentration
**Formula:** `HHI = Σ (sᵢ)²` where sᵢ is market share of firm i, in percent.

**Variables:**
- sᵢ = market share of firm i (as a percentage: 20, not 0.20)
- i = 1, 2, ..., n (all firms in the industry)

**Why this formula exists:** Rivalry as a "feeling" is useless. HHI gives a single number that antitrust regulators, investors, and strategy teams can compare across markets.

**How to interpret the output:**
- HHI < 1,500 → unconcentrated → likely high rivalry, low pricing power
- HHI 1,500–2,500 → moderately concentrated → some pricing power
- HHI > 2,500 → highly concentrated → oligopoly behaviour, better margins possible
- HHI > 5,000 → near-monopoly; regulatory scrutiny likely

(DOJ/FTC thresholds, standard in antitrust reviews.)

**Worked example:** Global LLM API providers (rough 2026 shares): OpenAI 40, Anthropic 20, Google 20, Meta-open-source 10, Others 10.
HHI = 40² + 20² + 20² + 10² + 10² = 1600 + 400 + 400 + 100 + 100 = **2,600** → moderately-to-highly concentrated → rivalry moderate; supplier power from buyer's perspective high.

Decision: if you are a consumer of LLM APIs, HHI 2,600 says suppliers have pricing power. Plan for price increases; budget for multi-vendor abstraction.

**Data source:** Market share data from Gartner, IDC, Statista, CB Insights, or company filings (10-K for public firms). Store in a Google Sheet refreshed quarterly.

---

### Formula 2: Composite Force Intensity Score
**Formula:** `Industry Pressure = Σ Forceᵢ × Weightᵢ`, where Forceᵢ is scored 1–5 and Weightᵢ is the force's relative importance (sum of weights = 1).

**Variables:**
- Forceᵢ = intensity of force i, 1 (benign) to 5 (existential)
- Weightᵢ = importance of force i for *this* industry (AI wrapper? Consulting? M&A?) — not all forces are equal

**Why this formula exists:** Equal-weighted averages hide the fact that one dominant force can sink an industry. Weighting forces to the industry context avoids this.

**How to interpret the output:**
- Score < 2.5 → attractive industry, expect above-average profitability
- Score 2.5–3.5 → average profitability; requires deliberate positioning
- Score > 3.5 → structurally hostile; enter only with a named moat or exit strategy

**Worked example:** A management consulting firm analysing "generative AI for mid-market banks" vertical.
| Force | Score | Weight | Weighted |
|-------|-------|--------|----------|
| Rivalry | 4 | 0.25 | 1.00 |
| New Entrants | 5 | 0.20 | 1.00 |
| Substitutes | 3 | 0.15 | 0.45 |
| Buyer Power | 2 | 0.15 | 0.30 |
| Supplier Power | 5 | 0.25 | 1.25 |
| **Total** | | **1.00** | **4.00** |

Score 4.0 → structurally hostile. Decision rule fires: enter only with explicit moat. Moat candidate: proprietary compliance-tuned models + long-term MSAs with mid-market banks = high switching cost and lower supplier dependency through custom fine-tunes.

**Data source:** Scoring comes from expert panels (internal or external); evidence list anchored to Gartner/Forrester reports, financial filings, and primary customer interviews. Stored in Airtable or Notion with a "Five Forces" table per target industry.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Draw the five arrows and call it a strategy | Quantify each force, show evidence, and map each to a strategic action |
| Define the industry loosely ("tech") | Define scope explicitly: product category × geography × buyer segment |
| Treat all five forces as equally important | Weight forces by industry context; a dominant force gets 30–40% weight |
| Forget complementors for platform businesses | Use the Six Forces variant for platforms, marketplaces, and ecosystems |
| Analyse once and shelve | Refresh annually minimum; after any major shock (new entrant Series A > $100M; regulatory change) |
| Confuse "it depends" with absence of analysis | Replace "it depends" with "this force scores X out of 5 because of evidence Y" |
| Treat Five Forces as a macro tool | Porter is industry-level; PESTEL is macro; don't substitute |
| Skip the action-translation step | Every force score > 3 must produce a named initiative with owner + deadline |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: AI Consulting Firm Choosing Between BFSI and Retail Verticals
**Situation:** A 200-person AI consultancy must pick one vertical to dominate next. Internal debate: BFSI (regulated, slow) vs Retail (fast-moving, fragmented).

**Applicable framework/metric:** Composite Force Intensity with industry-specific weighting.

**Analysis:**
| Force | BFSI Score | Retail Score |
|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Rivalry | 3 (few specialised players) | 5 (fragmented) |
| New Entrants | 2 (high regulation = entry barrier) | 5 (low barrier) |
| Substitutes | 2 (niche expertise) | 4 (DIY tooling) |
| Buyer Power | 3 (large but few buyers) | 4 (many, price-sensitive) |
| Supplier Power | 3 (talent) | 3 (talent) |
| **Weighted total** | **2.6** | **4.2** |

**Decision rule:** Pick industry with pressure score ≤ 3.0 and a clear moat. BFSI wins on both counts (regulatory barrier IS the moat).

**Action (Monday morning):** Draft go-to-market plan for BFSI: hire a former banking CTO as industry head, get SOC-2/ISO 27001 in the pipeline, deprioritise retail client outreach this quarter.

---

### Scenario 2: SaaS PM Responding to New Entrant Wave
**Situation:** A project-management SaaS (20% market share, HHI 1,800) sees 12 new entrants launch in the last year, each raising $20–50M.

**Applicable framework/metric:** HHI trajectory + Rivalry + Entry barrier analysis.

**Analysis:**
- HHI 1,800 today → likely drops to 1,400 as new entrants take 10–15% collective share → industry becomes more fragmented.
- Rivalry score moves from 3 to 4.
- Implication: margin compression unless differentiation or switching cost rises.

**Decision rule:** If HHI is projected to drop >300 points in 18 months, invest in switching-cost-raising features before the pricing war starts.

**Action:** Ship a deep-integration layer (API + SDK + data export frictions), expand security certifications (SSO, SAML, SCIM), and launch annual-billing pricing tier with discount. Target: raise effective switching cost measured in integration-hours from 4 to 40+.

---

### Scenario 3 (Anti-example): Five Forces with the Wrong Boundary
**Situation:** A product team analysing "the CRM market." They conclude rivalry is extreme (Salesforce, HubSpot, Zoho, 50+ others) and exit the space.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Industry Boundaries + Five Forces.

**Analysis (what goes wrong):**
- Wrong boundary: "CRM" is too broad.
- Correct boundary: "CRM for independent solar installers with 5–20 field technicians."
- In the narrow boundary: rivalry is 2 (only one generic competitor), buyer power is 3, substitutes 2, entrants 3, suppliers 2. Weighted score ≈ 2.3 → attractive niche.

**Cost of this mistake:** Team abandons a profitable niche. Lost opportunity: ~$40M TAM with 25% margins.

**Decision rule:** If Five Forces conclusion is "market is bad," first challenge the boundary definition. Re-run with tighter boundaries (vertical × geography × size segment) before accepting the conclusion.

**Action:** Rewrite the industry scope. Re-run the scoring. Present both wide and narrow boundaries to exec team; explicit recommendation on which boundary to compete in.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. **Build a Five Forces scorecard template in Notion or Airtable** — columns: force, score (1–5), weight, evidence, owner, action. Clone per target industry or vertical.
2. **Scope the industry explicitly before scoring** — write a one-line definition: `[product category] × [geography] × [buyer segment]`. Get exec sign-off on scope before data collection starts.
3. **Collect evidence per force, not opinions** — for supplier power, pull contract terms and concentration data; for rivalry, pull market-share data from Gartner/IDC and price trends from public pricing pages.
4. **Compute HHI in a Google Sheet** — share list of top-10 firms; formula `=SUMPRODUCT(share_range^2)`. Update quarterly.
5. **Assign one force to one exec sponsor** — Supplier → COO/Procurement, Rivalry → Head of Strategy, Buyer → CRO, Entrants → Chief of Staff (competitive intel), Substitutes → CTO, Complementors (if platform) → VP Partnerships.
6. **For every force scoring ≥ 4, create a named initiative in the OKR system** — linked to a Jira epic or a strategy initiative tracker. No score-4-without-owner.
7. **Review annually; refresh on trigger events** — any new entrant raising > $50M; regulatory change; supplier merger; M&A among top-5 incumbents.
8. **Link the Five Forces doc to the strategy wiki and OKR cascade** — make it impossible to set quarterly OKRs without referencing the current Five Forces state.
