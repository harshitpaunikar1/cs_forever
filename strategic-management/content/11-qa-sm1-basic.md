# Practice Q&A — SM-I Basic

Foundational questions covering Strategic Management I concepts. Q1 – Q70

---

## Q1. What is strategic management in simple terms?

**Level:** Basic

Strategic management is the process of deciding a company’s long-term direction and making choices about how it will succeed in its market. It includes setting goals, analyzing the business situation, choosing strategies, and taking actions to achieve results. It helps a firm decide what to prioritize and how to use its resources wisely over time.

**Real-life applications:**

- Strategic management guides a school in improving student outcomes over years.
- It helps a hospital decide where to invest for better patient care.
- Businesses use it to choose which products to grow or stop.
- Governments use it to plan development goals and allocate budgets.
- Individuals also use it informally for career planning and skill-building.

**Key concepts:** `Strategic management` `Strategy` `Long-term direction` `Implementation` `Control`

---

## Q2. Why do organizations need strategy instead of only day-to-day planning?

**Level:** Basic

Organizations need strategy because day-to-day planning focuses on immediate tasks, while strategy focuses on long-term success. Strategy helps a firm make clear choices about where to compete and how to win, so daily actions support a bigger goal. Without strategy, a company may waste resources, react too late to changes, or copy competitors without building its own advantage.

**Real-life applications:**

- A retailer uses strategy to decide online vs. offline growth.
- A university uses strategy to choose new courses based on future jobs.
- A nonprofit uses strategy to focus on the most impactful communities.
- A city uses strategy to balance transport, housing, and jobs.
- Even families use strategy for long-term savings and education plans.

**Key concepts:** `Strategy` `Long-term focus` `Prioritization` `Alignment` `Resource allocation`

## Q3. What is the difference between a plan and a strategy?

**Level:** Basic

A plan is a set of steps to achieve a task, often short-term and detailed. Strategy is a broader set of choices about goals, scope, and competitive approach, usually long-term. Strategy answers “where will we compete and how will we win,” while a plan explains “what actions will we take and when.” A plan can change often, but strategy should be stable enough to guide decisions.

**Real-life applications:**

- A café’s strategy is premium coffee; its plan is a monthly marketing calendar.
- A company’s strategy is cost leadership; its plan is reducing waste this quarter.
- A school’s strategy is digital learning; its plan is teacher training sessions.
- A hospital’s strategy is patient experience; its plan is new feedback systems.
- A sports team’s strategy is youth talent; its plan is weekly practice schedules.

**Key concepts:** `Strategy` `Planning` `Scope` `Competitive approach` `Long-term direction`

## Q6. What are goals and objectives, and how are they different?

**Level:** Basic

Goals are broad results an organization wants to achieve, such as “become a market leader.” Objectives are specific, measurable targets with time limits, such as “increase market share by 5% in 12 months.” Goals provide direction, while objectives make progress trackable and easier to manage.

**Real-life applications:**

- A school goal is better learning; an objective is higher test scores by year-end.
- A firm’s goal is growth; an objective is launching three new products this year.
- A city goal is cleaner air; an objective is reducing emissions by a set percent.
- A hospital goal is quality; an objective is reducing waiting time to 20 minutes.
- Personal goals become effective when turned into measurable objectives.

**Key concepts:** `Goals` `Objectives` `Measurement` `Time-bound targets` `Performance`

Core purpose is the deeper reason an organization exists beyond making money. It reflects what the organization tries to contribute to customers or society and often stays stable even when products or markets change. Core purpose helps build identity and guides decisions during uncertainty.

**Real-life applications:**

- A school’s core purpose is developing responsible citizens.

## Q9. What is competitive advantage?

**Level:** Basic

Competitive advantage is an edge that helps a company perform better than competitors. This edge may come from lower costs, better quality, stronger brand, faster delivery, or unique features. It matters because it helps the firm attract customers, earn profits, and survive competition over time.

**Real-life applications:**

- A low-cost airline gains customers through cheaper fares.
- A premium smartphone brand wins through quality and brand trust.
- A university builds advantage through strong placements and faculty.
- A clinic gains advantage through faster service and better patient care.
- Even individuals build advantage through rare skills and strong networks.

**Key concepts:** `Competitive advantage` `Differentiation` `Cost advantage` `Value` `Performance`

## Q10. What does “value proposition” mean?

**Level:** Basic

- A ride app offers “fast pickup and safe rides” to build trust.

## Q11. What is meant by “sustainable competitive advantage”?

**Level:** Basic

Sustainable competitive advantage is an advantage that lasts for a long time because it is difficult for competitors to copy. It may come from strong brand trust, unique capabilities, patents, network effects, or efficient systems. Sustainability matters because competitors often imitate successful ideas quickly.

**Real-life applications:**

- A strong brand retains customers even when rivals discount prices.
- A firm with superior logistics delivers faster at lower cost over years.
- A platform benefits when more users attract even more users.
- Universities with strong research reputation attract funding repeatedly.
- Countries build sustainable strengths through education and innovation systems.

**Key concepts:** `Sustainable advantage` `Imitation barriers` `Capabilities` `Brand` `Network effects`

## Q15. What are Porter’s Five Forces in simple terms?

**Level:** Basic

Porter’s Five Forces is a framework used to understand how competitive an industry is. It looks at five pressures: rivalry among competitors, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of buyers, and bargaining power of suppliers. Together, these forces influence prices, costs, and profitability.

**Real-life applications:**

- A new business uses Five Forces before entering a market.
- Companies use it to understand why profits are high or low in an industry.
- It helps firms choose whether to compete on cost or differentiation.
- Regulators can use it to understand market power and fairness.
- Students use it to analyze real industries like telecom, airlines, or retail.

**Key concepts:** `Five Forces` `Rivalry` `New entrants` `Substitutes` `Buyer power` `Supplier power`

## Q17. What is the Resource-Based View (RBV) of a firm?

**Level:** Basic

RBV is the idea that a company succeeds because of the resources and capabilities it owns or develops, such as strong people, brand, technology, or efficient systems. If these resources are valuable and hard to copy, the company can gain a long-term advantage.

**Real-life applications:**

- A brand with loyal customers can launch new products faster.
- A firm with strong R&D can innovate more consistently than rivals.
- A university with expert faculty attracts better students and funding.
- A sports team with strong training system produces talent regularly.
- Countries with skilled workforce gain advantage in global industries.

**Key concepts:** `RBV` `Resources` `Capabilities` `Advantage` `Imitation difficulty`

## Q22. What is cost leadership strategy?

**Level:** Basic

Cost leadership is a strategy where a firm aims to become the lowest-cost producer in its industry. By keeping costs low, it can charge lower prices to win customers or maintain normal prices and earn higher profit.

**Real-life applications:**

- Budget airlines simplify services to reduce operating costs.
- Retail chains use bulk buying to lower product costs.
- Universities reduce costs through shared digital learning resources.
- Governments reduce service costs by digitizing citizen processes.
- Households practice cost leadership by minimizing waste and bulk purchasing.

**Key concepts:** `Cost leadership` `Efficiency` `Low cost` `Economies of scale` `Price advantage`

- A hospital differentiates through advanced treatment and patient care.

- A retailer opens new stores in nearby cities gradually.
- A school expands by adding new grades and hiring teachers.

## Q29. What is entry mode in international business?

**Level:** Basic

Entry mode is the method a firm chooses to enter a foreign market, such as exporting, licensing, franchising, joint venture, or wholly owned subsidiary. The choice depends on desired control, risk level, investment capacity, and local market conditions.

**Real-life applications:**

- A restaurant brand uses franchising to expand abroad quickly.
- A small firm exports first to test demand without big investment.
- A company enters via joint venture to use local partner knowledge.
- A tech firm licenses software to local distributors.
- Governments guide entry modes in regulated industries like telecom or defense.

**Key concepts:** `Entry mode` `Exporting` `Licensing` `Franchising` `Joint venture` `Subsidiary`

## Q30. What is the difference between exporting and franchising?

**Level:** Basic

Exporting means selling products to another country from the home country, often through distributors, with relatively low investment. Franchising means giving another party the right to use your brand and business model in return for fees and rules, commonly used in services like food and retail. Exporting is product-focused, while franchising is business-model-focused.

**Real-life applications:**

- A tea company exports packaged tea to foreign stores.
- A fast-food brand uses franchising to open outlets in new countries.
- Exporting suits firms with strong production but limited foreign presence.
- Franchising suits firms that can standardize operations and brand experience.
- Both help scale but differ in control and responsibility.

**Key concepts:** `Exporting` `Franchising` `International expansion` `Control` `Licensing fees`

## Q35. What is a “trade-off” in strategy?

**Level:** Basic

A trade-off means choosing one option and giving up another. In strategy, trade-offs matter because resources are limited and trying to do everything often leads to weak performance. Clear trade-offs help create a focused position and reduce confusion in execution.

**Real-life applications:**

- A brand chooses premium quality and accepts higher prices.
- A budget airline chooses low cost and avoids expensive add-on services.
- A school chooses specialized programs instead of offering every course.
- A government chooses to invest more in health vs. other sectors due to limited budgets.
- Individuals choose time for study over entertainment to reach goals.

**Key concepts:** `Trade-off` `Focus` `Choice` `Positioning` `Resource limits`

## Q36. What is “industry rivalry” in Porter’s Five Forces?

**Level:** Basic

Industry rivalry refers to how strongly existing firms compete with each other. Rivalry is high when many competitors offer similar products, when growth is slow, or when price competition is intense. High rivalry usually reduces profits.

**Real-life applications:**

- Telecom firms compete heavily on price and data plans.
- Online retail rivalry increases during festival discounts.
- Coaching institutes compete through results and marketing claims.
- Hospitals compete through services, doctors, and patient experience.
- High rivalry pushes firms to innovate or differentiate to survive.

**Key concepts:** `Rivalry` `Competition intensity` `Price competition` `Market share` `Profit pressure`

---

## Q37. What is the “threat of new entrants” in an industry?

**Level:** Basic

Threat of new entrants means how easily new companies can enter an industry and compete. Entry is easier when startup costs are low, regulations are light, and customer switching is simple. When entry is easy, existing firms face more competition and lower profits.

**Real-life applications:**

- Small food brands can enter local markets easily.
- Banking is harder to enter due to regulation and high trust needs.
- E-commerce can attract new sellers quickly, increasing competition.
- Education coaching centers face frequent new entrants in many cities.
- Firms build barriers like brand loyalty to reduce this threat.

**Key concepts:** `New entrants` `Entry barriers` `Regulation` `Switching cost` `Competition`

---

## Q38. What does “threat of substitutes” mean?

**Level:** Basic

Substitutes are different products or services that satisfy the same need. The threat of substitutes is high when customers can easily switch to alternatives that are cheaper or more convenient. High substitute threat limits how much firms can charge.

**Real-life applications:**

- Tea and coffee can substitute for each other for caffeine needs.
- Streaming services substitute for movie theaters for entertainment.
- Online courses substitute for some classroom coaching.
- Public transport substitutes for ride-hailing in many cities.
- Firms reduce substitute threat by improving unique value and experience.

**Key concepts:** `Substitutes` `Alternatives` `Switching` `Price pressure` `Customer choice`

---

## Q39. What is “buyer power” in Porter’s Five Forces?

**Level:** Basic

Buyer power is the ability of customers to influence price and terms. Buyers have more power when there are many sellers, products are similar, and switching is easy. Strong buyer power forces firms to lower prices or improve value.

**Real-life applications:**

- Large retailers can demand discounts from suppliers.
- Customers compare online prices and switch quickly.
- Corporate clients negotiate strongly with service providers.
- Students choose colleges based on fees and placements, influencing institutions.
- Hospitals face buyer power through insurers and large employer groups.

**Key concepts:** `Buyer power` `Negotiation` `Switching cost` `Price sensitivity` `Customer leverage`

---

## Q40. What is “supplier power” in Porter’s Five Forces?

**Level:** Basic

Supplier power is the ability of suppliers to raise prices or reduce quality. Supplier power is high when there are few suppliers, inputs are specialized, or switching suppliers is difficult. Strong supplier power increases costs and reduces profitability for firms.

**Real-life applications:**

- Chip suppliers can influence smartphone pricing due to limited supply.
- Specialized medicine suppliers affect hospital procurement costs.
- Skilled faculty availability can influence coaching institute quality and cost.
- Construction material shortages increase real estate project costs.
- Companies manage supplier power through diversification and long-term contracts.

**Key concepts:** `Supplier power` `Input cost` `Switching difficulty` `Supplier concentration` `Profit impact`

## Q41. What is scenario planning?

**Level:** Basic

Scenario planning is a method of preparing for different possible futures. Instead of predicting one outcome, organizations develop a few realistic scenarios and decide how they would respond to each. This improves readiness when uncertainty is high.

**Real-life applications:**

- A business prepares for high inflation vs. stable prices.
- A university plans for more online learning vs. campus demand.
- A hospital prepares for disease outbreaks vs. normal seasonal patterns.
- Governments prepare disaster response scenarios for floods or droughts.
- Individuals plan career scenarios based on skills and industry changes.

**Key concepts:** `Scenario planning` `Uncertainty` `Futures` `Preparedness` `Contingency`

**Level:** Basic

A strategic framework is a structured tool that helps analyze business situations and make decisions systematically. Managers use frameworks to simplify complex problems, compare options, and ensure important factors are not ignored. Frameworks do not replace thinking; they support better thinking.

**Real-life applications:**

- Five Forces helps managers understand industry competition.

**Key concepts:** `Framework` `Analysis tool` `Decision support` `Structure` `Strategic thinking`

## Q45. What is “strategic positioning”?

**Level:** Basic

Strategic positioning is the place a company chooses to occupy in customers’ minds and in the market compared to competitors. It includes decisions about who to serve, what value to offer, and how to deliver it. Good positioning makes the company’s offering clear and attractive.

**Real-life applications:**

- A brand positions as “affordable and reliable” for mass customers.
- A premium brand positions as “high quality and status.”A college positions as “industry-ready” through placements.
- A clinic positions as “family healthcare with fast service.”Individuals position themselves in interviews as problem-solvers or specialists.

**Key concepts:** `Positioning` `Target customers` `Value proposition` `Differentiation` `Market perception`

## Q46. What does “customer value” mean in strategy?

**Level:** Basic

Customer value is the benefit customers feel they receive compared to what they pay (money, time, effort). Value can come from quality, convenience, service, status, or savings. Strategy aims to create strong customer value because it drives demand and loyalty.

**Real-life applications:**

- Fast delivery increases value in e-commerce for busy customers.
- Reliable public services increase citizen value and trust.
- A school increases value through better teaching and counseling support.
- Hospitals increase value with accurate diagnosis and compassionate care.
- People choose careers that create value for employers through results.

**Key concepts:** `Customer value` `Benefits` `Costs` `Satisfaction` `Loyalty`

## Q47. What is the meaning of “strategic alignment” inside an organization?

**Level:** Basic

Strategic alignment means ensuring that different parts of the organization—people, departments, budgets, and processes—work toward the same overall strategy. When alignment is strong, daily work supports long-term goals. When alignment is weak, teams move in different directions and waste resources.

**Real-life applications:**

- Sales and marketing align on the same target customer segment.
- A school aligns curriculum, assessments, and teacher training to learning goals.
- A hospital aligns patient-care standards across departments.
- A government aligns departments to deliver a common development program.
- Families align spending with long-term goals like education and home ownership.

**Key concepts:** `Alignment` `Coordination` `Shared goals` `Consistency` `Execution`

## Q48. What is meant by “strategic intent”?

**Level:** Basic

Strategic intent is a strong, long-term ambition that pushes an organization to aim higher than its current position. It creates focus and energy by stating what the organization wants to achieve over time, even if resources are limited today. It encourages learning and innovation.

**Real-life applications:**

- A startup aims to become a leader in digital payments over 10 years.
- A university sets intent to become a top research institution.
- A hospital sets intent to be the best trauma center in the region.
- Governments set intent like becoming a high-income economy.
- Individuals set intent like becoming a specialist in a chosen field.

**Key concepts:** `Strategic intent` `Ambition` `Long-term goals` `Focus` `Capability building`

## Q49. What is “organizational culture” and how does it affect strategy?

**Level:** Basic

Organizational culture is the shared way people behave, communicate, and make decisions in an organization. Culture affects strategy because even strong strategies can fail if the culture resists change or does not support execution. A supportive culture improves teamwork, discipline, and learning.

**Real-life applications:**

- A customer-focused culture improves service quality in retail.
- A safety culture reduces accidents in hospitals and factories.
- A learning culture helps schools and universities improve continuously.
- Ethical culture reduces corruption and builds trust in public offices.
- Individuals thrive in cultures that reward growth and responsibility.

**Key concepts:** `Culture` `Behavior norms` `Execution` `Change readiness` `Values`

## Q50. What does “SWOT” stand for, and what is it used for?

**Level:** Basic

SWOT stands for Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats. It is used to summarize internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats. SWOT helps organizations understand their situation and decide strategies that use strengths to capture opportunities while managing weaknesses and threats.

**Real-life applications:**

- A small business uses SWOT before expanding to a new area.
- A school uses SWOT to improve academic and extracurricular programs.
- A hospital uses SWOT to plan service upgrades and staffing.
- Governments use SWOT-like thinking for development planning.
- Individuals use SWOT to plan careers and skill development.

**Key concepts:** `SWOT` `Strengths` `Weaknesses` `Opportunities` `Threats`

## Q51. What is “strategy evaluation”?

**Level:** Basic

Strategy evaluation is checking whether a strategy is producing the expected results and still makes sense in the current environment. It includes reviewing performance data, market changes, and internal progress. If results are weak or conditions change, managers may adjust goals, actions, or the strategy itself.

**Real-life applications:**

- A company reviews a new product strategy after six months of sales data.
- A school evaluates a new teaching method based on learning outcomes.
- A hospital evaluates patient satisfaction after service improvements.
- Governments evaluate policies using public feedback and outcome data.
- Individuals evaluate study plans and adjust based on exam performance.

**Key concepts:** `Evaluation` `Performance review` `Feedback` `Adaptation` `Strategic control`

## Q52. What is meant by “strategic risk”?

**Level:** Basic

Strategic risk is the possibility that a strategy may fail due to wrong assumptions, poor execution, or unexpected changes in the environment. Strategic risk increases when uncertainty is high or when large investments are made. Managing risk involves preparation, monitoring, and flexibility.

**Real-life applications:**

- A firm faces strategic risk when entering a new country with unknown regulations.
- Schools face risk when changing curriculum without teacher readiness.
- Hospitals face risk when adopting new technology without training.
- Governments face risk when launching large schemes without planning.
- Individuals face risk when choosing careers without understanding market demand.

**Key concepts:** `Strategic risk` `Uncertainty` `Assumptions` `Monitoring` `Flexibility`

## Q53. What is “market segmentation” and why does it matter for strategy?

**Level:** Basic

Market segmentation is dividing customers into groups based on similar needs, behavior, or characteristics. It matters because different customers value different things. Segmentation helps a firm choose the right target customers and build offerings that fit them well.

**Real-life applications:**

- A telecom firm creates plans for students, families, and businesses.
- A school offers different support programs for different learner needs.
- A hospital offers separate packages for maternity, chronic care, and emergencies.
- Governments segment citizens for targeted welfare schemes.
- Individuals tailor resumes differently for different job roles.

**Key concepts:** `Segmentation` `Targeting` `Customer needs` `Positioning` `Value proposition`

## Q54. What is “strategic focus” and why is it useful?

**Level:** Basic

Strategic focus means concentrating efforts on a clear set of priorities instead of trying to do everything. It is useful because resources like money, time, and attention are limited. With focus, an organization can build stronger capabilities, deliver better value, and reduce confusion in execution.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A company focuses on one customer segment to improve product fit.A school focuses on foundational learning before adding advanced programs.A hospital focuses on one specialty to build strong expertise and reputation.Governments focus on key reforms to improve outcomes effectively.Individuals focus on one skill at a time to build mastery.

**Key concepts:** `Focus` `Priorities` `Resource allocation` `Capability building` `Trade-offs`

## Q55. What is meant by “first-mover advantage”?

**Level:** Basic

First-mover advantage is the benefit a company may gain by entering a market or adopting an innovation before competitors. It can help build brand recognition, customer loyalty, and early control of resources. However, first movers also face risks like high costs and uncertain demand.

**Real-life applications:**

- Early smartphone brands gained strong customer loyalty.
- Early e-commerce platforms built seller and buyer networks faster.
- Schools that adopt modern teaching early attract more students.
- Governments that digitize services early improve citizen satisfaction sooner.
- Individuals who learn emerging skills early gain better job opportunities.

## Q56. What is “brand reputation” and why is it strategic?

**Level:** Basic

Brand reputation is how customers and society perceive a company’s trustworthiness, quality, and behavior. It is strategic because a strong reputation attracts customers, reduces marketing effort, supports premium pricing, and builds resilience during crises. Reputation takes time to build and can be damaged quickly.

**Real-life applications:**

- Trusted hospitals attract more patients through word-of-mouth.
- Reputed universities attract better students and industry partnerships.
- Brands with good service recover faster from small mistakes.
- Governments build trust through consistent, transparent service delivery.
- Individuals build reputation through reliability and ethics at work.

**Key concepts:** `Brand reputation` `Trust` `Loyalty` `Perceived quality` `Stakeholder perception`

## Q57. What is a “strategic alliance”?

**Level:** Basic

A strategic alliance is a partnership between two or more organizations to achieve common goals while remaining separate entities. Alliances can help share resources, reduce risk, enter new markets, or combine capabilities. They work best when roles, benefits, and responsibilities are clearly defined.

**Real-life applications:**

## Q58. What is “economies of scale”?

**Level:** Basic

Economies of scale occur when the cost per unit decreases as a company produces more. Large production volumes spread fixed costs like machinery, rent, and management across more units. Economies of scale support cost leadership because they help reduce overall costs and improve price competitiveness.

**Real-life applications:**

- Large retailers buy in bulk and get lower supplier prices.
- Big factories produce at lower per-unit cost than small workshops.
- Large universities can share digital platforms across many students.
- Governments reduce cost per service through centralized digital systems.
- Families reduce cost per item by buying certain goods in bulk.

**Key concepts:** `Economies of scale` `Cost per unit` `Fixed costs` `Volume` `Cost advantage`

## Q59. What is “learning curve” in business strategy?

**Level:** Basic

## Q60. What is “switching cost” and why does it matter?

**Level:** Basic

Switching cost is the effort, money, time, or risk a customer faces when changing from one provider to another. High switching costs can reduce customer churn and strengthen a firm’s competitive position. Firms often increase switching costs through loyalty programs, ecosystems, and long-term contracts.

**Real-life applications:**

- Telecom contracts make it harder to switch quickly.
- Software subscriptions lock users through data and training needs.
- Banks increase switching cost through linked services and auto-payments.
- Students switching schools face adjustment and academic differences.
- Governments reduce switching costs by standardizing services across regions.

**Key concepts:** `Switching cost` `Customer retention` `Churn` `Lock-in` `Loyalty`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Strategic Management Process
**Purpose:** Turn analysis into a closed loop of choice, execution, and review.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
FORMULATE -> IMPLEMENT -> EVALUATE
     ^                           |
     |___________________________|
```
Components explained:
- Formulate: read the environment, define scope, pick trade-offs.
- Implement: assign owners, budgets, processes, and timelines.
- Evaluate: compare results against targets and reset course.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A product team decides to move from SMB to enterprise. Formulation chooses the segment and compliance bar; implementation adds SSO, SOC 2, and enterprise support; evaluation checks win rate, sales cycle length, and churn after two quarters.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the team is treating strategy as a slide deck instead of an operating loop.

### Porter’s Five Forces
**Purpose:** Judge how hard it will be to earn profit in an industry.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
          New entrants
               ^
Supplier <---- Industry ----> Buyer
 power         rivalry        power
               v
          Substitutes
```
Axes / Components explained:
- Rivalry: how aggressive current competitors are.
- New entrants: how easy it is for fresh competitors to enter.
- Substitutes: how easily customers can solve the same need another way.
- Buyer power: how much customers can force price or service changes.
- Supplier power: how much input providers can raise cost or lower quality.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A vertical AI SaaS seller sees high buyer power from enterprise procurement, moderate supplier power from cloud and model providers, and rising substitutes from in-house copilots. Decision: do not compete on generic features; build workflow lock-in and industry-specific data assets.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** Before entering a new market or pricing an offer.

### PEST
**Purpose:** Scan macro forces that can change demand, cost, or compliance.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Political | Economic
----------+---------
Social    | Technological
```
Axes / Components explained:
- Political: regulation, policy, trade, public spending.
- Economic: inflation, growth, rates, purchasing power.
- Social: demographics, habits, trust, culture.
- Technological: automation, platforms, digital channels, AI.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A health-tech startup launching in two countries finds very different regulatory load and adoption speed. The PEST read says to sequence the rollout: launch in the lighter-regulation market first, then localize for the stricter one.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the issue is external uncertainty rather than internal capability.

### Resource-Based View (RBV)
**Purpose:** Explain advantage from what the firm owns or can do repeatedly.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Resources -> Capabilities -> Advantage -> Performance
```
Axes / Components explained:
- Resources: people, data, brand, capital, technology.
- Capabilities: how well the resources are combined and used.
- Advantage: whether the capability creates an edge versus rivals.
- Performance: the business result that follows.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A consulting firm has scarce domain experts plus a reusable assessment library. The RBV lens says the advantage is not "consulting" in general; it is the combination of expertise and reusable IP that cuts delivery time and raises win rate.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the question is "what can we do better than others?"

### Value Chain Analysis
**Purpose:** Find where value is created, leaked, or delayed.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Inbound -> Ops -> Outbound -> Sales/Marketing -> Service
   \            Support Activities (HR, IT, Procurement)   /
```
Axes / Components explained:
- Primary activities: the direct steps that deliver value.
- Support activities: the systems that enable the chain.
- Cost: where money is consumed.
- Value creation: where differentiation or quality is built.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A SaaS company traces slow onboarding to a manual provisioning step in operations. Fixing that step cuts time-to-value and improves retention faster than adding new features.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When margins or customer experience are broken but the cause is unclear.

### SWOT
**Purpose:** Combine internal and external facts into one decision snapshot.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
            External
          O           T
        -------------------
Internal S |           | W |
        -------------------
```
Axes / Components explained:
- Strengths: what you do well internally.
- Weaknesses: what is hard or missing internally.
- Opportunities: attractive outside openings.
- Threats: external risks or pressure points.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A product-led startup has a strong data asset and weak enterprise support. SWOT suggests pairing the strength with an enterprise-ready opportunity only after fixing the support gap.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When leaders need a compact view before choosing priorities.

### Scenario Planning
**Purpose:** Prepare for several plausible futures instead of one forecast.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Today -> Scenario A
      -> Scenario B
      -> Scenario C
```
Axes / Components explained:
- Uncertainty: the variable that could change the outcome.
- Futures: the few futures worth planning for.
- Preparedness: what actions work in each future.
- Contingency: the trigger-based response plan.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A GenAI platform plans for cheap models, expensive models, and regulation-heavy models. The firm pre-approves roadmap moves for each case so product and sales do not freeze when the market shifts.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the future path is unclear and the cost of delay is high.

### Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning (STP)
**Purpose:** Choose which customer group to serve and how to be seen.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Segment -> Target -> Position
```
Axes / Components explained:
- Segmentation: split the market by need or behavior.
- Targeting: pick the segment(s) worth serving.
- Positioning: claim a clear place in the target’s mind.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A cloud analytics vendor segments by company size, then targets regulated mid-market firms, and positions as "fast compliance-ready analytics" rather than "cheap dashboards."

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When a product or service feels too broad to sell clearly.

### Platform Strategy
**Purpose:** Build a two-sided or multi-sided system that becomes more valuable as participation rises.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Side A <-> Trust / Rules / Matching <-> Side B
                  |
             Network effects
```
Axes / Components explained:
- Network effects: more users make the platform more useful.
- Trust: payments, ratings, identity, and dispute handling.
- Matching: the mechanism that connects the two sides.
- Liquidity: enough supply and demand to keep the system alive.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A marketplace for AI consultants should not chase raw sign-ups alone. It needs verified experts, buyer trust, and match quality, otherwise the network effect never converts into revenue.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the product connects multiple participant groups.

### Business-Level Strategy Choice Set
**Purpose:** Decide how to compete in one market before adding growth tactics.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
         LOW COST
            ^
            |
FOCUS <-----+-----> DIFFERENTIATION
            |
            v
        PREMIUM VALUE
```
Axes / Components explained:
- Cost leadership: win by operating cheaper.
- Differentiation: win by being meaningfully unique.
- Focus: win in a narrow segment instead of the whole market.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A developer-tool vendor should not promise low price, premium service, and every feature to every customer. The better choice is often a focused differentiation play on one user segment.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When the team has too many competitive promises at once.

### Global Integration vs Local Responsiveness
**Purpose:** Decide how much to standardize across countries versus adapt locally.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                LOCAL RESPONSIVENESS
                       HIGH
                         ^
                         |
LOW  --------------------+--------------------  HIGH
GLOBAL INTEGRATION       |                     STANDARDIZATION
                         |
                         v
                      LOW
```
Axes / Components explained:
- Global integration: keep systems, brand, and processes aligned.
- Local responsiveness: adapt to culture, regulation, and taste.
- Standardization: reuse the same product or process.
- Localization: change the offer to fit the market.

**IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example:** A B2B software firm can keep its core platform standard, but localize billing, language, and compliance features for each market. That gives scale without forcing a one-size-fits-all rollout.

**When to pull this out in a meeting:** When a global expansion is stuck between scale efficiency and local fit.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| Don't | Do |
|-----|-----|
| Treat strategy as a list of everything the team wants to do | Force a trade-off so the team knows what it will not do |
| Use one framework for every problem | Match the lens to the decision: PEST for macro risk, Five Forces for industry pressure, RBV for internal edge |
| Call any growth story a competitive advantage | Name the actual moat: brand, switching cost, network effects, scale, or capability |
| Enter a market because it looks big | Check rivalry, buyer power, supplier power, and entry barriers first |
| Copy a rival's feature set and call it differentiation | Differentiate on outcome, workflow fit, trust, or cost structure |
| Track activity volume and ignore results | Tie strategy to measurable objectives, then review variance regularly |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: AI Copilot That Needs a Moat
**Situation:** A B2B AI copilot has 22% monthly churn in SMB, 6% in enterprise, and a 4.2/10 trust score from security reviews. The team wants to keep adding generic features because competitors are moving fast.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Platform strategy, switching cost, and customer value.

**Analysis:** If churn stays above 15% in the SMB segment, the product is leaking value faster than it is creating it. The current signal says the real problem is not feature count; it is weak lock-in and trust. Increasing integration depth and embedding the copilot into daily workflows should matter more than shipping another surface feature.

**Decision rule:** If churn > 15% and trust < 5/10, prioritize workflow embedding and security proof points. If churn is 8%-15%, keep shipping but add onboarding and retention fixes. If churn < 8%, scale acquisition.

**Action:** Add in-product workflow hooks, publish a security packet for enterprise buyers, and stop promising broad horizontal use cases until retention improves.

### Scenario 2: Consulting Firm Choosing a Vertical
**Situation:** A 40-person consulting firm is debating whether to enter healthcare analytics or stay in fintech. Healthcare looks larger, but buyer power is high, procurement is slow, and the team lacks deep domain references.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Five Forces + RBV.

**Analysis:** Healthcare scores 8/10 on buyer power, 7/10 on rivalry, and only 5/10 on strategic fit because the firm lacks references. Fintech scores 6/10 on rivalry, 4/10 on buyer power, and 9/10 on strategic fit because the team already has reusable playbooks and client trust.

**Decision rule:** If the external pressure score is above 7/10 and strategic fit is below 6/10, do not enter. If fit is 8/10 or higher and the pressure score is below 6.5/10, enter and invest.

**Action:** Keep fintech as the core vertical, build one healthcare pilot only if it comes through a strong partner, and do not hire a full healthcare sales pod yet.

### Scenario 3: Global Expansion With Local Adaptation
**Situation:** A SaaS company wants to expand into two new countries. A standardized launch would take 8 weeks and $120k; a localized launch would take 14 weeks and $145k but is expected to raise conversion by 18%.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Global integration vs local responsiveness + scenario planning.

**Analysis:** Standardization wins on speed and cost, but localization may be worth it if the conversion uplift persists after launch. If the local market is regulation-heavy or language-sensitive, the extra spend is justifiable. If not, standardize the core and localize only billing and support.

**Decision rule:** If conversion uplift > 15% or compliance risk > 7/10, localize. If uplift is 8%-15%, standardize the core and localize only critical surfaces. If uplift < 8%, keep one global product.

**Action:** Launch a standard core, localize the landing page and billing first, and defer deeper product localization until the first cohort proves retention.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build a one-page strategy memo that states where you will play, how you will win, and what you will not do.
2. Run PEST and Five Forces on one priority market, then capture the result in a shared decision log.
3. Map internal strengths with RBV and value chain analysis so the team knows what is genuinely reusable.
4. Translate the strategy into 3-5 measurable objectives with owners, deadlines, and KPI definitions.
5. Add a monthly control cadence that reviews variance, blockers, and competitor moves.
6. Use scenario planning to pre-approve responses for the three most plausible future states.
7. Revisit positioning quarterly and remove any initiative that does not strengthen the chosen edge.
