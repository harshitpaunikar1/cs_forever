# Practice Q&A — SM-II Basic

Foundational questions covering SM-II Corporate Strategy topics. Q1 – Q70

---

## Q1. What is Strategic Management – II mainly about compared to Strategic Management – I?

**Level:** Basic

Strategic Management – II mainly focuses on corporate strategy—choices about what businesses a firm should be in, how it should grow or shrink its scope, and how the corporate center adds value across multiple businesses. Strategic Management – I usually focuses more on business-level competition within one industry. SM–II also emphasizes major strategic moves like alliances, acquisitions, divestitures, and corporate governance.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies like conglomerates decide which industries to enter or exit.
- Headquarters designs policies for shared services across units.
- Firms choose partnerships or acquisitions to grow faster.
- Boards and leaders guide long-term direction and risk control.

**Key concepts:** `Corporate strategy` `Business strategy` `Scope` `Corporate center` `Portfolio`

---

## Q2. Define corporate strategy in simple terms.

**Level:** Basic

Corporate strategy is the set of decisions that determine the overall direction of a company that owns or manages multiple businesses. It answers questions like: What industries should we be in? Should we expand, diversify, or exit? How should the parent company manage and support its different business units so that the whole group performs better together?

**Real-life applications:**

- A large group may run cement, power, and retail businesses.
- It decides where to invest more and where to reduce exposure.
- It sets shared policies like finance controls or talent systems.
- It aims to make the group stronger than separate companies.

**Key concepts:** `Corporate strategy` `Portfolio` `Diversification` `Parenting` `Synergy`

---

## Q3. What is the difference between business strategy and corporate strategy?

**Level:** Basic

Business strategy explains how a single business unit competes in its market (for example, low cost, differentiation, or focus). Corporate strategy is about the company as a whole—what businesses it should own and how those businesses should be managed together. Business strategy aims to win in one industry; corporate strategy aims to create value across industries and business units.

**Real-life applications:**

- A shampoo brand decides pricing and marketing to beat rivals (business).
- A parent company decides whether to own shampoos and foods together (corporate).
- Corporate strategy may share distribution across brands.
- Business strategy focuses on customers and competitors in one market.

**Key concepts:** `Business strategy` `Corporate strategy` `Competitive advantage` `Scope` `Portfolio`

---

## Q4. What is the Strategy Diamond framework?

**Level:** Basic

The Strategy Diamond is a simple tool to describe a complete strategy using five elements: Arenas (where to compete), Vehicles (how to get there), Differentiators (how to win), Staging (speed and sequence), and Economic Logic (how profits will be made). It helps leaders check whether a strategy is coherent and complete.

**Real-life applications:**

- Firms use it to clarify growth plans before investing.
- It ensures entry method matches goals (buy, build, partner).
- It helps communicate strategy consistently to teams.
- It reduces confusion by linking choices to profit logic.

**Key concepts:** `Arenas` `Vehicles` `Differentiators` `Staging` `Economic logic`

---

## Q5. What does “Arenas” mean in the Strategy Diamond?

**Level:** Basic

Arenas describe where a company will compete. This includes the product categories, customer segments, geographic markets, and channels it will focus on. Arenas are about making clear choices so the company does not try to be everywhere at once.

**Real-life applications:**

- A food company may choose health snacks as its arena.
- A retailer may choose Tier-2 cities and online channels.
- A software firm may choose education customers instead of banks.
- Clear arenas guide budgets, hiring, and product decisions.

**Key concepts:** `Arenas` `Market scope` `Customer segment` `Geography` `Channel`

---

## Q6. What does “Vehicles” mean in the Strategy Diamond?

**Level:** Basic

Vehicles describe how a company will enter or expand in its chosen arenas. Common vehicles include organic growth (build), alliances/partnerships, acquisitions, licensing, or joint ventures. The choice depends on speed, risk, control needs, and required capabilities.

**Real-life applications:**

- A company may acquire a competitor to enter quickly.
- It may partner with a local firm to reduce risk in a new country.
- It may build internally when it wants full control.
- Vehicle choice affects cost, integration work, and outcomes.

**Key concepts:** `Vehicles` `Organic growth` `Alliance` `Acquisition` `Joint venture`

---

## Q7. What does “Differentiators” mean in the Strategy Diamond?

**Level:** Basic

Differentiators are the features or capabilities that allow a company to win in its chosen arenas. They explain why customers will choose the firm over competitors. Differentiators can include quality, brand trust, low cost, speed, customization, service, or a unique business model.

**Real-life applications:**

- Airlines may differentiate by low fares or premium service.
- E-commerce firms may differentiate through faster delivery.
- Schools may differentiate through outcomes and placements.
- Clear differentiators guide what to invest in and improve.

**Key concepts:** `Differentiators` `Value proposition` `Competitive advantage` `Positioning` `Choice`

---

## Q8. What does “Staging” mean in the Strategy Diamond?

**Level:** Basic

Staging is about timing—what strategic moves happen first, what happens later, and how fast the company should move. It includes sequencing priorities and deciding the pace of expansion, investment, or capability building so the firm does not overstretch resources.

**Real-life applications:**

- A startup may enter one city first, then expand nationally.
- A firm may test a pilot before large-scale rollout.
- Staging helps manage cash flow and operational capacity.
- It reduces risk by learning early and scaling carefully.

**Key concepts:** `Staging` `Timing` `Sequencing` `Pace` `Strategic roadmap`

---

## Q9. What does “Economic Logic” mean in the Strategy Diamond?

**Level:** Basic

Economic logic explains how the strategy will generate profits. It identifies the main sources of financial returns, such as scale economies, premium pricing, cost efficiencies, higher utilization, or lower acquisition costs. A strategy is incomplete if it cannot clearly explain how money will be made sustainably.

**Real-life applications:**

- Low-cost retailers rely on high volume and low operating cost.
- Luxury brands rely on premium pricing and brand power.
- Platforms may rely on network effects and transaction fees.
- Economic logic guides investment and performance expectations.

**Key concepts:** `Economic logic` `Profit model` `Cost advantage` `Pricing power` `Scale`

---

## Q10. What is a “strategic decision”?

**Level:** Basic

A strategic decision is a high-impact choice that shapes the long-term direction of an organization. It usually involves major resource commitments, affects multiple parts of the organization, and is difficult to reverse. Examples include entering a new industry, acquiring a firm, or changing the business model.

**Real-life applications:**

- Governments make strategic decisions about infrastructure projects.
- Universities decide to launch new schools or close old programs.
- Companies decide to enter new countries or exit industries.
- Strategic decisions require careful analysis and leadership alignment.

**Key concepts:** `Strategic decision` `Long-term impact` `Irreversibility` `Resource commitment` `Direction`

---

## Q11. What is corporate advantage?

**Level:** Basic

Corporate advantage exists when the corporate parent adds value to its individual business units so that the total performance of the group is better than the sum of separate parts. It requires the corporate center to provide benefits like shared capabilities, better governance, stronger resource allocation, or cross-business synergy.

**Real-life applications:**

- A group may centralize procurement to reduce costs.
- It may share brand reputation across multiple products.
- It may provide training systems that improve all units.
- Without corporate advantage, diversification can become wasteful.

**Key concepts:** `Corporate advantage` `Corporate parent` `Added value` `Synergy` `Parenting`

---

## Q12. What is “synergy” in corporate strategy?

**Level:** Basic

Synergy is the extra value created when two businesses work together in a way that produces better results than if they operated separately. Synergies may come from shared resources, shared customers, shared technology, or better bargaining power. True synergy must be specific and measurable, not just a hopeful idea.

**Real-life applications:**

- Two brands share distribution trucks to reduce logistics cost.
- A bank cross-sells insurance to existing customers.
- Shared IT platforms reduce duplicated technology spending.
- Synergy claims should be tested before big investments.

**Key concepts:** `Synergy` `Shared resources` `Cross-selling` `Economies of scope` `Value creation`

---

## Q13. What is an “activity system” in strategy?

**Level:** Basic

An activity system is the set of interconnected activities a company performs to deliver its value proposition. Strategy becomes strong when these activities reinforce each other, creating a consistent way of operating that is hard to copy. It is not one action, but a network of aligned actions.

**Real-life applications:**

- A low-cost airline aligns quick turnarounds, simple service, and standard planes.
- A luxury hotel aligns premium locations, trained staff, and high-touch service.
- Schools align curriculum, assessments, and teaching methods with outcomes.
- Strong activity systems improve consistency and performance.

**Key concepts:** `Activity system` `Fit` `Reinforcement` `Value chain` `Consistency`

---

## Q14. What does “fit” mean in an activity system?

**Level:** Basic

Fit means the company’s activities support and strengthen each other, creating a coherent system. When fit is strong, each activity makes other activities more effective. Fit helps protect strategy because competitors find it hard to copy a whole system compared to copying a single feature.

**Real-life applications:**

- Discount stores combine low rent, bulk buying, and limited variety.
- Hospitals combine protocols, training, and audits to reduce errors.
- E-commerce combines warehouses, data analytics, and last-mile partners.
- Fit reduces internal conflict and improves execution reliability.

**Key concepts:** `Fit` `Alignment` `Reinforcing activities` `Coherence` `System thinking`

---

## Q15. What are strategic trade-offs?

**Level:** Basic

Trade-offs are choices about what a company will not do. They are important because trying to serve everyone usually leads to confusion and weak performance. Trade-offs protect strategy by keeping the company focused and by preventing conflicting activities that damage the chosen position.

**Real-life applications:**

- A premium restaurant avoids discounting to protect brand value.
- A low-cost airline avoids free meals to keep costs low.
- A school may avoid too many electives to focus on core learning.
- Trade-offs make operations simpler and strategy clearer.

**Key concepts:** `Trade-offs` `Focus` `Positioning` `Choice` `Consistency`

---

## Q16. What is a strategic alliance?

**Level:** Basic

A strategic alliance is a cooperative agreement between two or more firms to achieve shared goals while remaining separate companies. Alliances can involve sharing technology, entering new markets, co-developing products, or combining distribution. They are useful when firms want speed and shared risk without full ownership.

**Real-life applications:**

- Pharma firms partner to co-develop a new drug.
- Airlines collaborate through code-share to expand routes.
- A local firm partners with a global brand to enter new markets.
- Alliances require trust, clear roles, and governance.

**Key concepts:** `Strategic alliance` `Cooperation` `Shared risk` `Partnership` `Governance`

---

## Q17. What is a joint venture (JV)?

**Level:** Basic

A joint venture is a type of alliance where partners create a new, separate entity that they jointly own and control. Each partner contributes resources like capital, technology, or market access. JVs are often used when local knowledge and shared investment are needed, especially in new countries or new technologies.

**Real-life applications:**

- Two firms form a JV to build and operate a manufacturing plant.
- A global firm uses a JV to enter a regulated foreign market.
- Universities may create a JV-style institute with industry funding.
- Clear ownership and decision rights reduce JV conflicts.

**Key concepts:** `Joint venture` `Shared ownership` `Control` `Partnership` `Governance`

---

## Q18. What is a merger?

**Level:** Basic

A merger is when two companies combine to form a single organization. The goal may be to increase scale, reduce costs, expand markets, or combine complementary strengths. In practice, one company often has more influence, but the idea is that both businesses become one integrated entity.

**Real-life applications:**

- Two regional banks merge to create a larger network.
- Two manufacturers merge to reduce duplication and strengthen bargaining power.
- Mergers can change culture, jobs, and customer experience.
- They require integration planning to avoid disruption.

**Key concepts:** `Merger` `Integration` `Scale` `Consolidation` `Synergy`

---

## Q19. What is an acquisition?

**Level:** Basic

An acquisition is when one company buys another company and gains control over it. The buyer may acquire to enter a new market, gain technology, reduce competition, or scale faster. Acquisitions require careful post-deal integration to achieve the expected benefits.

**Real-life applications:**

- A tech firm buys a startup to gain a new feature quickly.
- A manufacturer buys a supplier to secure critical inputs.
- A retailer buys a smaller chain to expand into new regions.
- Poor integration can destroy value even after a good deal.

**Key concepts:** `Acquisition` `Control` `Integration` `Growth` `Valuation`

---

## Q20. What is post-merger integration?

**Level:** Basic

Post-merger integration is the process of combining the people, systems, processes, and cultures of two companies after a merger or acquisition. It includes decisions about leadership roles, IT systems, product lines, reporting structure, and customer handling. Good integration turns deal logic into real performance gains.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Integration aligns sales teams to avoid customer confusion.Operations are combined to remove duplicate costs.HR policies are unified to reduce employee uncertainty.Clear communication helps retain key talent and customers.

**Key concepts:** `Integration` `Culture` `Systems` `Synergy realization` `Change management`

---

## Q21. What is a divestiture?

**Level:** Basic

A divestiture is when a company sells, spins off, or closes a business unit or asset. Firms divest to focus on core areas, raise cash, reduce debt, or exit businesses with weak performance or poor strategic fit. Divestiture is a strategic choice to improve overall portfolio health.

**Real-life applications:**

- A company sells a non-core unit to invest in growth areas.
- Governments divest stakes in firms through privatization for fiscal reasons.
- Universities close low-demand programs to strengthen key departments.
- Divestitures can improve focus but need careful transition planning.

**Key concepts:** `Divestiture` `Portfolio cleanup` `Core focus` `Spin-off` `Asset sale`

---

## Q22. What is a spin-off?

**Level:** Basic

A spin-off is a divestiture method where a company creates a new independent firm by separating a business unit and distributing shares to existing shareholders. The parent reduces complexity, and the new company can focus on its own strategy. Spin-offs are used when separation can unlock value.

**Real-life applications:**

- A large firm spins off its consumer unit to focus on enterprise clients.
- Investors can value each business more clearly after separation.
- Employees may get clearer priorities and leadership focus.
- Spin-offs require legal, financial, and operational separation work.

**Key concepts:** `Spin-off` `Separation` `Shareholders` `Focus` `Portfolio restructuring`

---

## Q23. What is strategic dissonance?

**Level:** Basic

Strategic dissonance is a mismatch between the strategy leaders believe they are executing and what the organization is actually doing or experiencing. It can appear as conflicting priorities, poor results despite effort, or frontline feedback that contradicts top management assumptions. Recognizing dissonance helps trigger timely strategy review.

**Real-life applications:**

- A firm claims “premium service” but cuts training budgets.
- A school promises outcomes but ignores student feedback and support needs.
- A government policy targets inclusion but implementation excludes key groups.
- Early detection prevents bigger failure later.

**Key concepts:** `Strategic dissonance` `Assumptions` `Execution gap` `Feedback` `Alignment`

---

## Q24. What is a strategy review?

**Level:** Basic

A strategy review is a structured process to evaluate whether the current strategy is working and still fits the environment. It checks performance, assumptions, competitive changes, and execution issues. The outcome may be to continue, adjust, or redesign strategy based on evidence rather than habit.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies review strategy annually or quarterly in volatile markets.
- Nonprofits review programs to ensure impact and funding alignment.
- Schools review curriculum strategy based on learning outcomes.
- Governments review policies to improve results and reduce waste.

**Key concepts:** `Strategy review` `Evaluation` `Assumptions check` `Performance` `Adaptation`

---

## Q25. What is strategy reformulation?

**Level:** Basic

Strategy reformulation means changing or redesigning a strategy when new information shows the old plan is not working or conditions have changed. It may involve changing target markets, value proposition, growth approach, or resource allocation. Reformulation turns learning into a better strategy.

**Real-life applications:**

- A startup shifts from consumer app to business clients.
- A retailer changes from expansion to profitability focus.
- A city reformulates transport plans after new population patterns emerge.
- Reformulation avoids wasting resources on outdated assumptions.

**Key concepts:** `Reformulation` `Pivot` `Learning` `Adaptation` `Strategic change`

---

## Q26. What are KPIs in strategy?

**Level:** Basic

KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) are measurable signals used to track whether strategic goals are being achieved. Good KPIs connect daily actions to strategic outcomes. They can be financial (profit, cost) or non-financial (retention, quality, on-time delivery).

**Real-life applications:**

- A hospital tracks infection rates to improve care strategy.
- A business tracks customer retention to validate growth strategy.
- A school tracks attendance and learning outcomes to improve programs.
- Governments track service delivery time to improve public satisfaction.

**Key concepts:** `KPI` `Measurement` `Targets` `Performance management` `Outcomes`

---

## Q27. What is Blue Ocean Strategy?

**Level:** Basic

Blue Ocean Strategy is about creating new market space where competition is minimal, instead of fighting rivals in crowded markets. It focuses on “value innovation”—offering higher value to customers while also reducing costs by removing features customers do not value.

**Real-life applications:**

- A gym offers 20-minute express workouts for busy professionals.
- A school creates a new hybrid learning model for working adults.
- A retailer targets non-customers with a simplified shopping experience.
- Blue oceans often come from rethinking who the customer can be.

**Key concepts:** `Blue ocean` `Red ocean` `Value innovation` `Non-customers` `Differentiation`

---

## Q28. What is a “red ocean” in strategy?

**Level:** Basic

A red ocean is a market space where many competitors fight for the same customers using similar offerings. Competition is intense, pricing pressure increases, and profits can shrink. Firms in red oceans often focus on beating rivals rather than changing the market rules.

**Real-life applications:**

- Many telecom firms compete with similar data plans and discounts.
- Exam coaching markets can become red oceans with heavy advertising.
- When products look alike, price wars become common.
- Firms may seek differentiation or a blue ocean to escape.

**Key concepts:** `Red ocean` `Competition` `Price pressure` `Industry rivalry` `Commoditization`

---

## Q29. What is “value innovation”?

**Level:** Basic

Value innovation means creating a leap in value for customers while also reducing the company’s costs. Instead of choosing between differentiation and low cost, value innovation aims to achieve both by eliminating and reducing features that do not matter and adding what customers truly want.

**Real-life applications:**

- A low-cost clinic streamlines processes while improving patient experience.
- An education platform reduces costs using tech while improving access.
- A transport app reduces waiting time while lowering booking friction.
- Value innovation can create a new category and new demand.

**Key concepts:** `Value innovation` `Cost reduction` `Customer value` `Differentiation` `Blue ocean`

---

## Q30. What is innovation strategy?

**Level:** Basic

Innovation strategy is a plan for how an organization will generate, select, and scale new ideas that lead to new products, services, or business models. It clarifies what type of innovation is needed, how resources will be allocated, and how risks will be managed to create future growth.

**Real-life applications:**

- Firms invest in R&D to create next-generation products.
- Universities promote research and entrepreneurship programs.
- Governments design innovation missions like clean energy transitions.
- Innovation strategy balances experimentation with business discipline.

**Key concepts:** `Innovation strategy` `R&D` `Experimentation` `New ventures` `Growth engines`

## Q31. What is corporate venturing?

**Level:** Basic

Corporate venturing is when an established company creates, funds, or partners with new ventures to explore future opportunities. This can happen through internal venture teams, incubators, corporate venture capital, or partnerships with startups. The aim is to build new growth options beyond the current core business.

**Real-life applications:**

- A bank launches an internal fintech venture to test new services.
- A manufacturing firm funds a startup working on smart sensors.
- A retailer incubates a direct-to-consumer brand inside the company.
- Venturing helps large firms stay relevant in changing markets.

**Key concepts:** `Corporate venturing` `Incubator` `Corporate VC` `Experimentation` `Growth options`

## Q32. What is knowledge management?

**Level:** Basic

Knowledge management is the practice of capturing, organizing, sharing, and using knowledge within an organization. It ensures that valuable experience and best practices do not remain only in people’s heads. Good knowledge management improves learning, reduces repeated mistakes, and speeds up problem solving.

**Real-life applications:**

- Consulting firms store project lessons for future teams.
- Hospitals standardize protocols to spread clinical knowledge.
- Schools share teaching resources and successful methods across teachers.
- Public agencies document procedures to improve continuity and transparency.

**Key concepts:** `Knowledge management` `Learning` `Best practices` `Repository` `Sharing`

## Q33. What is tacit knowledge?

**Level:** Basic

Tacit knowledge is know-how that people gain through experience and practice, but it is hard to write down. Examples include negotiation skill, diagnosing problems, or handling customers. Organizations try to share tacit knowledge through mentoring, training, shadowing, and communities of practice.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A senior technician teaches troubleshooting through on-the-job coaching.A teacher shares classroom management skills via observation.A doctor trains interns through rounds and case discussions.Firms create mentorship programs to transfer tacit knowledge.

**Key concepts:** `Tacit knowledge` `Experience` `Mentoring` `Learning-by-doing` `Skills transfer`

---

## Q34. What is explicit knowledge?

**Level:** Basic

Explicit knowledge is knowledge that can be documented and easily shared, such as manuals, procedures, databases, checklists, and reports. It is easier to store and transfer than tacit knowledge. Organizations build repositories and standard operating procedures to spread explicit knowledge consistently.

**Real-life applications:**

- A factory uses SOPs to ensure quality consistency.
- A university documents admission and evaluation processes.
- A call center uses scripts and FAQs for faster service.
- Public services use guidelines to standardize citizen support.

**Key concepts:** `Explicit knowledge` `Documentation` `SOP` `Repository` `Codification`

---

## Q35. What are “perils” in strategy implementation?

**Level:** Basic

Perils in strategy implementation are common problems that cause good strategies to fail during execution. These include unclear priorities, poor coordination across teams, weak accountability, lack of skills, resistance to change, and slow decision-making. Managing execution risks is essential to convert strategy into results.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A transformation fails when teams don’t understand priorities.Projects stall when approvals take too long.Employees resist when communication and training are weak.Clear goals, ownership, and reviews reduce these perils.

**Key concepts:** `Execution risk` `Implementation` `Alignment` `Accountability` `Coordination`

## Q36. What is digital transformation?

Digital transformation is the process of using digital technologies to fundamentally improve how an organization operates and delivers value to customers. It is not just buying software; it involves redesigning processes, building digital skills, and using data to make better decisions. The goal is better speed, quality, and customer experience.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Banks shift onboarding to mobile apps for faster service.Factories use sensors to reduce downtime through predictive maintenance.Schools adopt learning platforms and analytics to support students.Governments digitize services to reduce queues and increase transparency.

## Q37. Why do firms form strategic alliances instead of acquiring?

**Level:** Basic

Firms form alliances instead of acquiring when they want to share risk, move quickly, or access capabilities without paying the full cost of buying a company. Alliances can be more flexible and less complex than acquisitions. They are also useful when full ownership is not possible due to regulation or when both partners need to learn from each other.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Two firms co-develop a product without merging teams fully.A foreign company partners locally to handle regulations.Organizations test collaboration before committing to ownership.Alliances allow faster entry with lower upfront investment.

**Key concepts:** `Alliance` `Acquisition alternative` `Flexibility` `Shared risk` `Control`

## Q38. What is diversification?

**Level:** Basic

Diversification is when a company expands into new products, services, or industries beyond its current business. It can be related (connected to existing capabilities) or unrelated (different industries). Diversification is a corporate strategy choice that aims to grow, reduce risk, or use existing strengths in new areas.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A dairy company enters health beverages (related diversification).A conglomerate enters both retail and power (unrelated diversification).Universities add new departments to attract different student groups.Diversification must be supported by clear corporate advantage.

**Key concepts:** `Diversification` `Scope expansion` `Related diversification` `Unrelated diversification` `Portfolio`

---

## Q39. What is related diversification?

**Level:** Basic

Related diversification is when a company enters businesses that have a logical connection to its existing capabilities, customers, technologies, or supply chain. Because of shared resources, related diversification can create synergies more easily than unrelated diversification. The parent can often add value using existing expertise.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A sportswear firm enters fitness equipment using brand and distribution.A bank enters insurance using customer base and data.A food company enters packaged snacks using supply chain strengths.Related diversification can reduce costs and boost cross-selling.

**Key concepts:** `Related diversification` `Synergy` `Shared capabilities` `Economies of scope` `Fit`

---

## Q40. What is unrelated diversification?

**Level:** Basic

Unrelated diversification is when a company enters businesses with little connection to its existing capabilities or markets. It is harder to create synergy because activities and customers differ. Firms pursue it to spread risk or use financial strengths, but it can fail if the corporate parent cannot add real value.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A group owning cement, hotels, and software may face weak synergy.Management complexity increases across very different industries.Capital allocation becomes the main corporate role.Many firms later divest unrelated units to improve focus.

**Key concepts:** `Unrelated diversification` `Conglomerate` `Complexity` `Parenting challenge` `Portfolio`

---

## Q41. What is corporate governance?

Corporate governance is the system of rules, structures, and processes used to direct and control an organization. It defines how decisions are made, how performance is monitored, and how managers are held accountable. Good governance protects shareholders and stakeholders and reduces unethical or risky behavior.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Boards set oversight policies for risk and compliance.Companies use audits to ensure financial transparency.Universities use governance to maintain academic integrity.Governments use governance systems to reduce corruption and misuse of funds.

**Key concepts:** `Corporate governance` `Accountability` `Oversight` `Transparency` `Risk control`

## Q42. What is the role of the board of directors?

**Level:** Basic

The board of directors provides oversight of top management and ensures the organization is run in the best long-term interest of shareholders and other stakeholders. The board approves major strategic decisions, monitors performance, oversees risk and compliance, and hires or evaluates the CEO. It should challenge management constructively, not micromanage daily operations.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Boards approve big acquisitions and large investments.They review ethical issues and major risk exposures.They set CEO compensation linked to long-term performance.They protect the organization from governance failures and scandals.

**Key concepts:** `Board of directors` `Oversight` `CEO evaluation` `Risk oversight` `Fiduciary duty`

Leadership plays a key role in creating and executing strategy by setting direction, making tough choices, and aligning people around priorities. Leaders communicate the strategy, allocate resources, build culture, and remove obstacles. Without strong leadership, strategy often remains a document instead of becoming real action.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A CEO focuses the company by stopping low-value projects.School leaders align teachers around learning goals and methods.Public leaders drive reforms by building trust and clear accountability.Leaders influence culture, motivation, and execution discipline.

**Key concepts:** `Leadership` `Direction` `Resource allocation` `Culture` `Execution`

Sometimes M&A is used to restructure a portfolio or improve efficiency. However, deals succeed only when strategic fit and integration are managed well.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A company buys a competitor to increase market share quickly.A firm acquires a startup to speed up innovation.A bank acquires branches to expand into new regions.M&A can fail if culture clashes and synergy is overestimated.

## Q46. Why do firms divest businesses?

Firms divest to focus on core strengths, raise cash, reduce debt, simplify operations, or exit businesses with weak performance or poor strategic fit. Divestiture can improve management attention and resource allocation. A well-timed divestiture can strengthen the remaining portfolio and increase overall competitiveness.REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A firm sells a low-growth unit to invest in high-growth areas.A government divests assets to fund public spending and reduce deficits.A school closes under-enrolled programs to strengthen main departments.Divestitures can protect long-term health by reducing complexity.

## Q47. What is internationalization as a corporate strategy move?

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### Strategy Diamond
**Purpose:** Force a full strategy into one coherent set of choices.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
          Arenas
   Vehicles       Differentiators
          Economic Logic
            Staging
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Arenas - where the firm competes, including segments, geography, channel, and product scope.
Component 2: Vehicles - build, buy, ally, license, or joint venture.
Component 3: Differentiators - why the customer picks you, such as speed, cost, trust, or customization.
Component 4: Staging - the sequence and pace of rollout.
Component 5: Economic logic - how profit is created and sustained.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS vendor entering healthcare uses arenas to pick provider systems, vehicles to partner instead of acquire, differentiators to emphasize compliance and integration, staging to pilot in one region, and economic logic to prove expansion margin after onboarding costs.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a growth plan sounds ambitious but the entry path or profit logic is fuzzy.

### Activity System and Fit
**Purpose:** Show how operations reinforce the strategy instead of fighting it.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Activity A -> Activity B -> Activity C
     ^                         |
     |-------------------------|
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Fit - activities reinforce one another.
Component 2: Alignment - the team, tools, and incentives point in the same direction.
Component 3: Coherence - the customer sees one consistent offer.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI consulting practice pairs reusable prompts, data-governance checklists, delivery accelerators, and partner certifications so each engagement makes the next one cheaper and faster.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When teams are optimizing locally and the customer experience is becoming inconsistent.

### Related vs Unrelated Diversification Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether the portfolio should share capabilities or stay separate.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                Strategic fit
             High             Low
Related   |  Shared skills   |  Hard to justify
Unrelated |  Conglomerate    |  Complexity trap
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Related diversification - shared capabilities, cross-selling, and economies of scope.
Component 2: Unrelated diversification - no obvious synergy; the parent must earn its keep through capital allocation or governance.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product company adding analytics modules to its core platform is related; buying an unrelated logistics firm is only rational if the parent has a real portfolio-optimization edge.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone proposes a new business that is not obviously adjacent to the core.

### Build / Buy / Ally / License Matrix
**Purpose:** Choose the fastest and safest vehicle for entering a new arena.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                 Control
            High            Low
Speed  High  Build          Ally
       Low   Buy            License
```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Build - maximum control, slower learning.
Component 2: Buy - fast access to assets, high integration risk.
Component 3: Ally - shared risk, shared governance.
Component 4: License - low commitment, limited control.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team deciding on a new AI feature may build the model layer, buy a data-labeling company, ally with a cloud vendor, or license a model wrapper depending on speed and control needs.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team agrees on the goal but not the route.

### Blue Ocean / Red Ocean Value Curve
**Purpose:** Separate price-war competition from value innovation.

**Text Diagram:**
```text

      /

```
Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: Red ocean - crowded demand, price pressure, commoditization.
Component 2: Blue ocean - new demand, less direct rivalry, value innovation.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A generic analytics dashboard is red-ocean; an AI workflow product that removes a compliance step and sells to non-customers is closer to blue-ocean value innovation.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When differentiation is being discussed as feature creep instead of market creation.

### Strategy Review Loop
**Purpose:** Keep strategy alive after launch.

**Text Diagram:**

## 4. Formulas
The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source]; the source names the concepts but does not state the math explicitly.

Formula: Synergy value = Incremental cash flow from combination - integration cost
Variables:
Incremental cash flow = the extra revenue or cost savings created by combining businesses.
Integration cost = systems, people, process, legal, and change costs needed to realize the deal.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether a merger, acquisition, or alliance creates net value.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 0 -> the combination destroys value -> walk away or redesign the deal.
Value > 0 but small -> value exists but is fragile -> slow down and de-risk execution.
Value strongly positive -> the strategic move has room to justify complexity -> proceed with tight integration control.
Worked example with numbers: If a product acquisition adds $8M annual cash flow and costs $3M to integrate, synergy value is $5M, which supports the deal if the payback period is acceptable.

Formula: Acquisition premium % = ((Offer price - Stand-alone value) / Stand-alone value) x 100
Variables:
Offer price = the price paid for the target.
Stand-alone value = value of the target as an independent firm.
Why this formula exists: It tests whether the buyer is overpaying for expected synergies.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 10% -> disciplined bid range -> still validate integration assumptions.
Value 10%-30% -> common deal zone -> needs clear synergy case.
Value > 30% -> high overpayment risk -> require exceptional strategic fit or walk.
Worked example with numbers: If stand-alone value is $100M and offer price is $125M, premium = 25%, so the buyer needs at least $25M of credible net synergy to break even.

Formula: KPI attainment % = (Actual / Target) x 100
Variables:
Actual = realized outcome.
Target = planned outcome.
Why this formula exists: It shows whether execution is on track.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 80% -> corrective action needed -> stop and diagnose the bottleneck.
Value 80%-99% -> watch zone -> fix the execution gap before it compounds.
Value >= 100% -> on track or ahead -> consider scaling the playbook.
Worked example with numbers: If a launch target is 1,000 active users and actual is 850, KPI attainment is 85%, which says the team should inspect onboarding, retention, and channel quality before expanding spend.

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI product expansion
Situation: A B2B AI product team wants to enter the healthcare vertical. The team estimates $2.4M first-year revenue, $1.1M onboarding and compliance cost, and a 14-month payback if it builds internally.
Applicable framework/metric: Strategy Diamond and KPI attainment.
Analysis: The arena is clear, but the vehicle choice is still open. If a partner channel can cut onboarding cost by 40% and reduce payback below 9 months, the partner route is better than a pure build.
Decision rule: If payback is under 9 months, build or partner is acceptable; if 9-15 months, prefer ally or license; if above 15 months, delay or narrow the scope.
Action: Pilot one regulated workflow, define compliance KPIs, and hold expansion until activation and retention are stable.

Scenario 2: Consulting firm portfolio cleanup
Situation: A consulting-led digital firm runs a low-margin custom build unit and a high-margin AI platform unit. The custom unit contributes 18% of revenue but only 4% of EBITDA, while the platform unit contributes 30% of revenue and 52% of EBITDA.
Applicable framework/metric: Related vs unrelated diversification and corporate parenting.
Analysis: If the custom unit does not create measurable cross-sell or capability spillover, it is a portfolio drag. If management overhead rises faster than combined EBITDA, the firm should simplify.
Decision rule: If unit-level EBITDA margin is below 10% and cross-sell is below 5%, divest or restructure; if cross-sell is above 10% and the unit improves the platform funnel, keep it.
Action: Map shared capabilities, quantify cross-sell, and set an exit trigger for the underperforming unit.

Scenario 3: M&A integration for an IT services firm
Situation: A mid-market IT services company acquires a niche cloud-security firm for $40M. The integration team expects $6M annual synergy, but retention risk could erase $2M if key engineers leave.
Applicable framework/metric: Acquisition premium and post-merger integration risk.
Analysis: Net synergy is $4M before integration cost. If integration will cost $5M and key-engineer attrition rises above 12%, the deal becomes negative value unless the acquirer protects the talent base and sequencing.
Decision rule: If retention stays above 90% and synergy capture exceeds 80% by month 12, continue; if retention falls below 85% or synergy capture stays under 60%, freeze further integration and fix people/process issues.
Action: Lock leadership retention, keep customer-facing systems stable, and sequence process integration after the first 90 days.
