# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational questions covering core marketing research concepts.

---

## Q1. What is marketing research?

**Level:** Basic

Marketing research is the systematic process of collecting and using information to help a business make better marketing decisions. It focuses on understanding customers, competitors, and market conditions so that actions like pricing, product design, advertising, and distribution are based on evidence rather than guesswork.

**Real-life applications:**

- Marketing teams use research before launching new products to reduce risk.
- Retailers use it to understand why sales rise or fall in certain stores.
- Startups use research to check if people actually need their idea.
- Schools use similar research methods to study student preferences and improve services.

**Key concepts:** `Marketing research` `decision-making` `information gathering` `evidence-based marketing`

---

## Q2. Why do companies conduct marketing research before making major decisions?

**Level:** Basic

Companies conduct marketing research to reduce uncertainty and improve the chances of success. Research helps them understand what customers want, how competitors behave, and what factors influence buying. This leads to smarter choices about products, pricing, promotions, and target markets.

**Real-life applications:**

- A company tests demand before investing in a new factory.
- A brand studies customer reactions before changing its logo.
- A service provider researches complaints to fix recurring problems.
- Governments use similar studies to design public awareness campaigns.

**Key concepts:** `risk reduction` `customer insights` `market understanding` `managerial decisions`

---

## Q3. What is the difference between a decision problem and a research problem?

**Level:** Basic

A decision problem is what managers need to decide, such as “Should we lower the price?” A research problem is what information must be collected to support that decision, such as “How price-sensitive are our customers?” The decision problem is action-focused, while the research problem is information-focused.

**Real-life applications:**

- A retailer deciding on home delivery needs research on customer willingness to pay.
- A university planning a new course needs research on student interest.
- A hospital deciding on new services needs research on patient needs.
- Organizations avoid wrong actions by defining both problems clearly.

**Key concepts:** `decision problem` `research problem` `managerial action` `information needs`

---

## Q4. What does “problem formulation” mean in marketing research?

**Level:** Basic

Problem formulation means clearly defining what you want to find out before collecting data. It turns a broad concern like “sales are down” into a specific question such as “Which customer group reduced purchases and why?” A clear problem statement guides the entire research process.

**Real-life applications:**

- Businesses use it to avoid collecting irrelevant data.
- Consultants use it to set clear project goals with clients.
- Nonprofits use it to identify why donations are declining.
- Schools use it to understand why attendance is dropping in a program.

**Key concepts:** `problem formulation` `research objectives` `scope` `clarity`

---

## Q5. What are research objectives?

**Level:** Basic

Research objectives are clear statements describing what a study aims to achieve. They specify the key information needed, such as measuring awareness, understanding preferences, or identifying reasons for dissatisfaction. Objectives keep the research focused and help evaluate whether the study was successful.

**Real-life applications:**

- A brand sets objectives to measure awareness after a campaign.
- A restaurant sets objectives to understand why repeat visits are declining.
- An app sets objectives to identify the most confusing part of onboarding.
- Public agencies set objectives to assess citizen satisfaction with services.

**Key concepts:** `research objectives` `focus` `information needs` `evaluation`

---

## Q6. What is a hypothesis in marketing research?

**Level:** Basic

A hypothesis is a testable statement that predicts a relationship between variables. For example, “Lower prices will increase sales among students.” Researchers use hypotheses to guide data collection and analysis, and then check whether the data supports the statement.

**Real-life applications:**

- A retailer tests the hypothesis that discounts increase weekend traffic.
- A telecom company tests whether faster response time improves satisfaction.
- A school tests whether extra tutoring improves exam performance.
- Organizations use hypotheses to move from assumptions to evidence.

**Key concepts:** `hypothesis` `prediction` `variables` `testing`

---

## Q7. What is exploratory research?

**Level:** Basic

Exploratory research is used when the problem is not clearly understood. It helps generate ideas, identify possible causes, and clarify what should be studied further. It often uses interviews, focus groups, or observation to gather deep insights.

**Real-life applications:**

- A new startup explores why people avoid a type of product.
- A hospital explores reasons behind patient dissatisfaction before redesigning services.
- A university explores student expectations before launching a new program.
- Organizations use it as a first step before surveys and large studies.

**Key concepts:** `exploratory research` `insight generation` `qualitative methods` `problem clarity`

## Q8. What is descriptive research?

**Level:** Basic

Descriptive research describes market characteristics such as who buys, what they buy, when they buy, and how they feel. It measures facts like awareness, usage, satisfaction, and demographics. Surveys are commonly used for descriptive research because they can reach many people.

**Real-life applications:**

- A company measures brand awareness across different cities.
- A retailer studies which age group buys most often.
- A school measures student satisfaction with facilities.
- Governments use descriptive surveys to understand public service usage.

**Key concepts:** `descriptive research` `measurement` `surveys` `market profiling`

---

## Q9. What is causal research in simple terms?

**Level:** Basic

Causal research checks whether one factor causes a change in another factor. For example, it tests whether changing an ad message increases purchase intention. This type of research often uses experiments where one variable is changed while others are kept similar.

**Real-life applications:**

- A company tests two ad versions to see which drives more sales.
- A school tests whether a new teaching method improves scores.
- A bank tests whether shorter forms increase account sign-ups.
- Organizations use causal research to justify changes with evidence.

**Key concepts:** `causal research` `experiments` `cause-and-effect` `variables`

---

## Q10. What is primary data?

**Level:** Basic

Primary data is information collected directly for a specific research purpose. It is new data gathered through methods like surveys, interviews, observation, or experiments. Primary data is useful because it matches the exact question the researcher wants to answer.

**Real-life applications:**

- A company surveys customers about satisfaction after a purchase.
- A restaurant interviews guests to improve menu options.
- A university collects student feedback on a new course.
- Organizations use primary data to answer very specific questions.

**Key concepts:** `primary data` `data collection` `surveys` `interviews`

---

## Q11. What is secondary data?

**Level:** Basic

Secondary data is information that already exists and was collected for another purpose. Examples include sales reports, industry studies, government statistics, and online reviews. It is often cheaper and faster to use, but it may not perfectly match the current research need.

**Real-life applications:**

- A firm uses census data to understand neighborhood income levels.
- A brand uses past sales reports to detect seasonal patterns.
- A researcher reviews published reports to learn about market trends.
- Organizations use secondary data to save time before collecting new data.

**Key concepts:** `secondary data` `existing information` `desk research` `data sources`

---

## Q12. What is a research design?

**Level:** Basic

A research design is the overall plan for how the research will be conducted. It explains what data will be collected, from whom, using which method, and how the data will be analyzed. A good design ensures the study answers the research questions properly.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies plan research design before spending on surveys.
- Agencies use it to decide between interviews and questionnaires.
- Schools design studies to evaluate new teaching activities.
- A clear plan prevents wasted time and confusing results.

**Key concepts:** `research design` `planning` `methodology` `study structure`

## Q13. What is qualitative research?

**Level:** Basic

Qualitative research focuses on understanding meanings, opinions, and reasons behind behavior. It uses open-ended methods like interviews and focus groups. Instead of numbers, it produces detailed insights that help explain “why” people think or act a certain way.

**Real-life applications:**

- Brands use it to understand feelings about packaging.
- Hospitals use it to hear patient stories about service problems.
- Schools use it to explore why students struggle in a subject.
- Organizations use qualitative findings to shape better survey questions.

**Key concepts:** `qualitative research` `open-ended data` `insights` `motivations`

---

## Q14. What is quantitative research?

**Level:** Basic

Quantitative research collects information in numbers so it can be measured and compared. It often uses structured surveys with fixed answer options. It helps answer questions like “how many,” “how much,” and “how often,” and allows statistical analysis.

**Real-life applications:**

- A brand measures satisfaction scores across regions.
- A retailer counts how many customers prefer each product size.
- A school tracks attendance rates after a policy change.
- Organizations use quantitative results to set targets and track performance.

**Key concepts:** `quantitative research` `measurement` `surveys` `statistical analysis`

## Q15. What is a focus group?

**Level:** Basic

A focus group is a guided discussion with a small group of participants about a topic, product, or idea. A moderator asks questions and encourages interaction. The goal is to learn opinions, feelings, and reasons that may not appear in structured surveys.

**Real-life applications:**

- A company uses focus groups to test new product concepts.
- A movie studio uses it to understand reactions to a trailer.
- A public office uses it to improve communication messages.
- Schools use group discussions to understand student needs.

**Key concepts:** `focus group` `moderation` `group discussion` `qualitative insights`

---

## Q16. What is an in-depth interview?

**Level:** Basic

An in-depth interview is a one-on-one conversation that explores a person’s opinions, experiences, and motivations. It uses open-ended questions and follows up on interesting points. This method is useful when you need detailed understanding rather than quick answers.

**Real-life applications:**

- A luxury brand interviews customers to learn why they buy premium items.
- A hospital interviews patients to improve service quality.
- A university interviews alumni to understand career outcomes.
- Organizations use interviews to explore sensitive topics privately.

**Key concepts:** `in-depth interview` `probing` `one-on-one` `qualitative method`

## Q17. What is observation as a research method?

**Level:** Basic

Observation means watching real behavior instead of only asking questions. Researchers may observe shoppers in a store, users on an app, or customers using a service. It helps capture what people actually do, which can differ from what they say.

**Real-life applications:**

- Stores observe customer movement to improve product placement.
- Apps observe where users drop off during checkout.
- Schools observe classroom behavior to improve teaching methods.
- Organizations use observation to reduce reliance on self-reported answers.

**Key concepts:** `observation` `behavior` `user experience` `field research`

## Q18. What is a questionnaire?

**Level:** Basic

A questionnaire is a set of written questions used to collect information from people. It can be paper-based, online, or phone-based. Good questionnaires use clear wording and logical order so respondents can answer accurately and comfortably.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies use questionnaires to measure customer satisfaction.
- Schools use them to collect student feedback.
- Governments use them to assess citizen service experiences.
- Nonprofits use them to understand community needs.

**Key concepts:** `questionnaire` `survey instrument` `respondents` `data collection`

## Q19. What is a measurement scale?

**Level:** Basic

A measurement scale is a way to record answers in an organized form. It can be categories (like brand names), ranks (first/second/third), or ratings (1 to 5). Choosing the right scale helps ensure the data is meaningful and usable for analysis.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Companies use rating scales to track satisfaction over time.
Hospitals use scales to rate service quality across departments.
Schools use scales to evaluate course feedback consistently.
Organizations use scales to compare results across groups or locations.
KEY CONCEPTS: measurement scale, categories, ranking, rating

**Key concepts:** `measurement scale` `categories` `ranking` `rating`

---

## Q20. What is a nominal scale with an example?

**Level:** Basic

A nominal scale classifies data into names or categories without any order. For example, “Which brand do you use: A, B, C?” The categories are different, but none is higher or lower than another in the scale.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies classify customers by city or payment method.
- Hospitals classify patients by department type.
- Schools classify students by course stream.
- Organizations use nominal data to count and compare categories.

**Key concepts:** `nominal scale` `categories` `classification` `labels`

---

## Q21. What is an ordinal scale with an example?

**Level:** Basic

An ordinal scale records data in an order or ranking, but the gaps between ranks are not equal or known. For example, “Rank these features from most important to least important.” You know the order, but not how much more important one is than another.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies rank customer priorities to guide product improvements.
- Schools rank student preferences for extracurricular options.
- Hospitals rank service issues by urgency based on patient feedback.
- Organizations use ranking to decide what to fix first.

**Key concepts:** `ordinal scale` `ranking` `preference order` `priorities`

---

## Q22. What is an interval-type rating scale commonly used in surveys?

**Level:** Basic

An interval-type rating scale often asks respondents to rate something on a numeric range like 1 to 10. The idea is that the difference between 6 and 7 is treated as similar to the difference between 7 and 8. It helps compare average ratings across groups.

**Real-life applications:**

- Apps use 1–10 ratings to measure user satisfaction.
- Hotels use rating scales to compare service quality across locations.
- Schools use ratings to improve teaching based on feedback.
- Organizations track changes in ratings after improvements or campaigns.

**Key concepts:** `rating scale` `interval-type scale` `comparison` `satisfaction measurement`

## Q23. What does “validity” mean in marketing research?

**Level:** Basic

Validity means the research measures what it is supposed to measure. If a survey question is intended to measure satisfaction, it should truly capture satisfaction and not something else like price concerns or product familiarity.

**Real-life applications:**

- Businesses check validity to avoid wrong conclusions from surveys.
- Hospitals ensure patient surveys truly reflect service experience.
- Schools validate tests to measure learning, not memorization tricks.
- Organizations improve question wording to better match the intended concept.

**Key concepts:** `validity` `measurement accuracy` `construct` `survey quality`

---

## Q24. What does “reliability” mean in marketing research?

**Level:** Basic

Reliability means the research produces consistent results when repeated under similar conditions. If the same people answer the same questions at two close time points, their results should be similar if nothing important has changed. Reliability helps ensure findings are dependable.

**Real-life applications:**

- Brands use reliable scales to track satisfaction over months.
- Schools use reliable assessments to compare results across classes.
- Organizations rely on consistent data to monitor performance and progress.
- Reliable measures reduce confusion caused by random errors.

**Key concepts:** `reliability` `consistency` `measurement stability` `repeatability`

## Q25. What is sampling in marketing research?

**Level:** Basic

Sampling is selecting a smaller group of people from a larger population to collect information. Since it’s often impossible to study everyone, researchers use samples to estimate what the full population thinks or does, as long as the sample represents the population well.

**Real-life applications:**

- Companies survey a sample of customers instead of all customers.
- Governments sample households to estimate unemployment rates.
- Schools sample students to test new policies before full rollout.
- Sampling saves cost while still providing useful insights.

**Key concepts:** `sampling` `population` `sample` `representation`

## Q26. What is a population in research?

**Level:** Basic

The population is the entire group you want to understand through research. For example, it could be all customers of a brand, all smartphone users in a city, or all students in a university. The sample is taken from this population.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A bank’s population could be all account holders nationwide.
A school’s population could be all students in a grade.
A retailer’s population could be all loyalty program members.
Organizations define population clearly to avoid wrong targeting.
KEY CONCEPTS: population, target group, research scope, sampling base

**Key concepts:** `population` `target group` `research scope` `sampling base`

---

## Q27. What is a sample in research?

**Level:** Basic

A sample is a smaller subset of the population chosen for study. Researchers collect data from the sample and use it to make conclusions about the larger population. A good sample should reflect the population’s important characteristics.

**Real-life applications:**

- A brand surveys 500 customers to estimate overall satisfaction.
- A hospital surveys a sample of patients from different departments.
- A university surveys a sample of alumni across graduation years.
- Sampling helps make research feasible and affordable.

**Key concepts:** `sample` `subset` `representativeness` `inference`

## Q28. What is random sampling?

**Level:** Basic

Random sampling is a method where each member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. It helps reduce selection bias and improves the chance that the sample represents the population fairly, especially when the population list is available.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A company randomly selects customers from a database for a survey.
A school randomly selects students to evaluate a new cafeteria menu.
A city office randomly selects households for a service feedback survey.
Random selection improves trust in results.
KEY CONCEPTS: random sampling, equal chance, representativeness, bias reduction

**Key concepts:** `random sampling` `equal chance` `representativeness` `bias reduction`

---

## Q29. What is convenience sampling?

**Level:** Basic

Convenience sampling selects participants who are easiest to reach, such as nearby shoppers or online followers. It is fast and low-cost but may not represent the full population well. Results should be interpreted carefully because the sample may be biased.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: A store asks only walk-in customers for feedback.
A teacher surveys only students who attend an optional session.
A brand polls only social media followers for opinions.
It’s useful for quick insights but weak for broad conclusions.
KEY CONCEPTS: convenience sampling, non-probability sampling, bias, quick feedback

**Key concepts:** `convenience sampling` `non-probability sampling` `bias` `quick feedback`

**Level:** Basic

Fieldwork is the stage where data is actually collected from respondents. It includes conducting interviews, administering surveys, observing behavior, and recording responses. Good fieldwork requires clear instructions, trained staff, and quality checks to ensure accurate data.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Survey teams collect customer opinions in shopping malls.
Researchers conduct phone interviews for service feedback.
Apps gather user feedback through in-app questionnaires.
Organizations monitor fieldwork to prevent missing or fake data.

## Q31. What is non-response in surveys?

**Level:** Basic

Non-response happens when selected participants do not answer the survey or skip key questions. This can reduce accuracy because the people who respond may be different from those who don’t. Researchers try to reduce non-response with reminders, shorter surveys, and better timing.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Companies send follow-up emails to increase response rates.
Hospitals simplify forms to reduce skipped feedback questions.
Schools provide time in class for surveys to reduce non-response.
Low response can lead to decisions based on incomplete views.
KEY CONCEPTS: non-response, response rate, survey bias, participation

**Key concepts:** `non-response` `response rate` `survey bias` `participation`

## Q32. What is response bias?

**Level:** Basic

Response bias occurs when people give answers that are not fully truthful or accurate. This can happen due to social pressure, fear of judgment, confusing questions, or leading wording. It reduces the quality of data and can mislead decision-making.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Customers may overpraise service when staff are watching.
Employees may hide negative views in workplace surveys.
Students may give “expected” answers rather than honest feedback.
Organizations reduce bias through anonymity and neutral wording.
KEY CONCEPTS: response bias, social desirability, leading questions, measurement error

**Key concepts:** `response bias` `social desirability` `leading questions` `measurement error`

## Q33. What is a leading question in a questionnaire?

**Level:** Basic

A leading question is written in a way that pushes respondents toward a particular answer. For example, “How helpful was our excellent customer service?” It can distort results because it suggests the expected response instead of allowing honest opinions.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Companies rewrite questions to be neutral for accurate feedback.
Public surveys avoid biased wording to maintain trust.
Schools avoid leading questions in student evaluations.
Neutral questions help organizations identify real problems.
KEY CONCEPTS: leading question, questionnaire bias, neutrality, survey design

**Key concepts:** `leading question` `questionnaire bias` `neutrality` `survey design`

---

## Q34. What is a double-barreled question?

**Level:** Basic

A double-barreled question asks two things at once, making it hard to answer clearly. Example: “Are you satisfied with our price and quality?” A person might like the quality but dislike the price, so the response becomes unclear.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Businesses separate questions to get accurate feedback.
Schools avoid combining topics in course evaluation forms.
Healthcare surveys separate waiting time and staff behavior questions.
Clear questions lead to better decisions and fewer misunderstandings.
KEY CONCEPTS: double-barreled question, clarity, questionnaire design, measurement error

**Key concepts:** `double-barreled question` `clarity` `questionnaire design` `measurement error`

REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Companies pilot surveys to prevent costly mistakes in large studies.
Universities test feedback forms before campus-wide distribution.
NGOs pretest questions to ensure they match local language and context.
Pilot testing improves clarity and response quality.
KEY CONCEPTS: pilot test, pretest, questionnaire refinement, survey quality

**Key concepts:** `pilot test` `pretest` `questionnaire refinement` `survey quality`

The mean is the average value of a set of numbers. You get it by adding all values and dividing by the number of values. In marketing research, the mean is commonly used to summarize ratings like satisfaction or likelihood to recommend.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Companies track average satisfaction each month.
Schools calculate average scores to review performance.
Teams compare average ratings between two service locations.
The mean gives a simple overall picture, though it can be affected by extremes.

## Q40. What is the median and when is it useful?

**Level:** Basic

The median is the middle value when numbers are arranged in order. It is useful when data has extreme values that could distort the average. For example, if a few customers spend unusually high amounts, the median spending can represent a more typical customer.
REAL-LIFE_APPLICATION: Retailers use median income to understand typical customers.
Apps use median session time to avoid distortion from a few heavy users.
Schools use median scores when a few outliers change the average a lot.
The median gives a stable “typical” value in skewed data.
KEY CONCEPTS: median, middle value, outliers, central tendency

**Key concepts:** `median` `middle value` `outliers` `central tendency`

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

The selection logic below is [verified from model knowledge, not source]; the source names the concepts, and the frameworks organize how a leader should use them.

### Decision-to-Research Bridge
**Purpose:** Convert a business decision into a research question before anyone spends time collecting data.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Decision problem
    -> Research problem
        -> Research objectives
            -> Method choice
                -> Evidence
                    -> Action
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Decision problem: the choice a manager must make, such as launch, price, or pivot.
Research problem: the information gap that blocks that choice.
Research objectives: the specific facts, themes, or metrics to collect.
Method choice: interview, focus group, survey, experiment, or desk research.
Evidence: the output that is credible enough to change the decision.
Action: the product, pricing, campaign, or consulting recommendation.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A PM wants to know whether to add a premium AI feature. The decision problem is "Should we ship?" The research problem is "What use cases justify pay, and what objections exist?" Objectives become willingness-to-pay, workflow fit, and security concerns. The bridge prevents a vague "let's research the market" request from becoming a six-week waste.

When to pull this out in a meeting: Whenever someone asks for research but cannot name the decision that depends on it.

### Research-Type Selection Matrix
**Purpose:** Choose exploratory, descriptive, or causal research based on how clear the problem is and what decision is pending.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                       DECISION NEED
                 Learn why?   Size it?   Prove impact?
Problem clarity
Unclear           Exploratory  -          -
Known issue       Exploratory   Descriptive Causal
Known hypothesis  -             Descriptive Causal
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Exploratory: when the team does not yet know what to ask.
Descriptive: when the team needs measurement, profiling, or sizing.
Causal: when the team needs proof that a change caused an outcome.
Problem clarity: how well the issue is already defined.
Decision need: whether the goal is explanation, quantification, or causation.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS company sees churn rising. If nobody knows why, start exploratory interviews. If the team knows the likely segments, run descriptive surveys to quantify the pattern. If onboarding has been changed and the team wants to know whether that caused the shift, run a causal test.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When stakeholders jump straight to a survey or experiment without agreeing on the question type.

### Evidence Source Matrix
**Purpose:** Match primary vs secondary data and qualitative vs quantitative methods to the business question.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
                    New data?                Existing data?
Qualitative          Interviews, focus groups Desk research, notes
Quantitative         Surveys, experiments     Reports, dashboards
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Primary data: new information collected for this study.
Secondary data: existing information collected earlier.
Qualitative: open-ended material that explains motivations and language.
Quantitative: numeric material that measures size, frequency, or lift.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product team first reviews app analytics and prior support tickets, then interviews users to understand friction, then surveys the broader base to measure prevalence, and finally runs an experiment to test the fix. That sequence uses the cheapest acceptable evidence at each step.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone wants to pay for primary research before checking whether the answer already exists in company data or public sources.

## 4. Formulas

The formulas below are [verified from model knowledge, not source]; they are standard survey and research-ops math that helps managers operationalize the source concepts.

### Formula 1: Survey Response Rate
Formula: `Response Rate = Completed Responses / Eligible Invitations`

Variables:
Completed Responses = usable survey completes
Eligible Invitations = people who actually received and were qualified for the survey

Why this formula exists: It tells you whether the sample is likely to be biased by non-response and whether you need more follow-up.

How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.20 -> weak participation -> pause and re-contact or shorten the instrument
Value 0.20-0.40 -> usable but caution needed -> check for non-response bias
Value > 0.40 -> strong participation -> proceed with more confidence

Worked example with numbers: 240 completed surveys from 1,000 eligible invitations gives 0.24, or 24%. For a product team, that is enough for directional decisions, but not enough to assume the silent 76% think the same way.

### Formula 2: Margin of Error for a Survey Proportion
Formula: `MOE ≈ z * sqrt(p(1-p) / n)`

Variables:
z = confidence level multiplier, often 1.96 for 95% confidence
p = observed proportion
n = sample size

Why this formula exists: It estimates how much a survey percentage may swing because you only asked a sample, not everyone.

How to interpret the output:
Value < 0.03 -> robust for many decisions -> use for planning and tracking
Value 0.03-0.07 -> acceptable for directional choices -> use with caveats
Value > 0.07 -> too wide for fine decisions -> collect more data or segment less

Worked example with numbers: If 200 of 500 respondents prefer Feature A, p = 0.40 and n = 500. At 95% confidence, MOE is about 0.043, so the result is roughly 40% plus or minus 4.3 points. For a PM, that is good enough to decide which concept to test next, but not enough to lock a final roadmap without other evidence.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Don't ask for research before naming the decision. | Do state the decision problem first, then translate it into a research problem and objective set. |
| Don't start with a survey when the problem is still fuzzy. | Do use exploratory research first when problem clarity is low. |
| Don't buy new data if existing information already answers the question. | Do scan secondary data, dashboards, and reports before commissioning primary data collection. |
| Don't treat a focus group as proof of market-wide behavior. | Do use focus groups to generate hypotheses, language, and theme lists before scaling to surveys. |
| Don't infer cause-and-effect from a simple correlation. | Do use experiments or other causal research when you need to test whether a change drives the outcome. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: SaaS Activation Is Flat
**Situation:** A B2B SaaS product has 1,200 trial sign-ups in a month, but only 180 users reach the activation milestone. The team does not know whether the issue is messaging, onboarding friction, or the feature itself.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Decision-to-Research Bridge + Research-Type Selection Matrix + Response Rate.

**Analysis:** Start with 12 exploratory interviews, then a 500-user survey. The survey returns 210 completes, a 42% response rate, and 39% of respondents cite setup complexity. A follow-up experiment reduces onboarding from 11 steps to 6 and lifts activation from 15% to 27%.

**Decision rule:** If exploratory themes are inconsistent, keep exploring. If one issue appears in more than 30% of the survey and the experiment lifts activation by more than 5 percentage points, ship the change. If the lift is below 5 points, keep testing.

**Action:** Rewrite onboarding, add in-product guidance, and tie the product review to activation lift rather than subjective feedback.

### Scenario 2: AI Feature Pricing for an Enterprise Add-On
**Situation:** A product team is deciding whether an AI summarization module should be bundled or priced separately. Sales thinks the feature is a nice-to-have; customer success says it saves enough time to justify a fee.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Evidence Source Matrix + Margin of Error + survey willingness-to-pay.

**Analysis:** The team starts with secondary data from CRM and support tickets, then interviews 10 customers, then surveys 240 prospects. The survey shows 58% of enterprise buyers would pay an extra $15/user/month, but only 18% would pay $30/user/month. The margin of error is about 6 points, so the $15 price point is directional, while $30 is too aggressive.

**Decision rule:** If willingness-to-pay exceeds the incremental cost by at least 3x and response confidence is tighter than 7 points, price separately. If not, bundle and use the feature to improve retention instead.

**Action:** Launch a split-packaging pilot with a small customer segment and track conversion, expansion revenue, and churn delta.

### Scenario 3: Consulting Team Considering a New Vertical
**Situation:** A consulting practice wants to enter healthcare because inbound leads look attractive. No one has validated demand, decision cycles, or competitor presence.

**Applicable framework/metric:** Research-Type Selection Matrix + secondary data + qualitative research.

**Analysis:** Desk research finds a large market, but six expert interviews show procurement cycles of 9 to 12 months and strong compliance friction. A short focus group with healthcare operators surfaces the exact language buyers use, which later improves the proposal deck.

**Decision rule:** If secondary data suggests market size is large but interviews show decision friction above 6 months and internal expertise is thin, do not commit headcount yet. If both market size and buying velocity are favorable, proceed to a pilot offer.

**Action:** Build a one-page market entry hypothesis, run three more interviews with procurement stakeholders, and only then allocate sales capacity.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Write a decision brief that names the owner, deadline, and business choice that depends on the research.
2. Split the brief into a decision problem and a research problem so the team knows what question it is actually answering.
3. Inventory existing data sources, including CRM exports, analytics dashboards, prior reports, and public market data.
4. Choose the method mix by problem clarity: exploratory for unknowns, descriptive for sizing, causal for impact testing.
5. Draft the instrument or discussion guide, then pilot it with a small sample to catch ambiguity and bias.
6. Collect the minimum new data needed, starting with the cheapest credible source and escalating only when uncertainty remains.
7. Translate findings into a decision rule, owner, and date so the research produces action, not just insight.
