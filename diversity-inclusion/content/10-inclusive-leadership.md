# Inclusive Leadership

## Overview
Inclusive leadership is a style of leadership that actively seeks out, welcomes, and leverages diverse perspectives to drive better outcomes. Inclusive leaders are aware of their own biases, create psychological safety for their teams, and hold themselves accountable for equitable practices. This leadership approach is not limited to executives; it applies to anyone who influences others. Research consistently shows that inclusive leaders build more engaged, innovative, and high-performing teams.

---

## Why It Matters
Leaders set the tone for organizational culture. If leaders do not model inclusive behaviors, policies and programs will have limited impact. Inclusive leadership directly influences whether employees feel they belong, whether diverse candidates are attracted and retained, and whether the organization can adapt to an increasingly diverse marketplace.

## Key Principles
- Self-awareness of personal biases and blind spots is the starting point
- Inclusive leaders actively solicit input from people who think differently
- Accountability means setting measurable goals and reporting on progress
- Courage to challenge exclusionary norms and speak up for equity is essential

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Psychological Safety** | A climate in which people feel comfortable expressing ideas, asking questions, and admitting mistakes without fear of punishment |
| **Servant Leadership** | A leadership philosophy where the primary goal is to serve the needs of team members, empowering them to perform at their best |
| **Cultural Humility** | An ongoing commitment to self-evaluation and self-critique regarding cultural differences, acknowledging that one can never be fully competent in another's culture |
| **Inclusive Decision-Making** | The practice of deliberately involving diverse voices and perspectives in the decision-making process |

## Use Case
A hospital system trains all department heads in inclusive leadership skills, including how to run meetings where every voice is heard, how to give equitable feedback, and how to identify and sponsor high-potential employees from underrepresented backgrounds.

## Scenario
> A vice president at a consumer goods company noticed that her leadership team meetings were dominated by a few senior voices while newer and more diverse team members stayed silent. She restructured meetings to include pre-meeting idea submissions, rotating facilitators, and anonymous feedback channels. Over time, the quality of decisions improved and team members reported feeling more valued and heard.

## Examples
- A manager schedules regular one-on-one check-ins with each team member to understand individual barriers and career aspirations
- A CEO publicly shares the company's diversity data, including areas of underperformance, and outlines a concrete action plan for improvement

## 2. Frameworks & Mental Models

### 2.1 Deloitte 6Cs of Inclusive Leadership

Deloitte's landmark research identified six signature traits that distinguish inclusive leaders from merely diverse-aware managers.

| C | Trait | Core Behavior | Tech/Consulting Signal |
|---|-------|---------------|------------------------|
| 1 | **Commitment** | Authentic personal dedication to inclusion as a value, not a compliance task. | Leader voluntarily ties team inclusion metrics to their own performance goals. |
| 2 | **Courage** | Willingness to challenge the status quo, share personal bias stories, and confront inequity visibly. | Interrupting a hiring panel when a candidate is evaluated differently for an identical answer than a prior candidate. |
| 3 | **Cognizance of Bias** | Active awareness of both personal and systemic biases and their effect on decisions. | Using structured interview scorecards; auditing sprint assignment patterns for workload inequity. |
| 4 | **Curiosity** | A growth mindset toward differences — asking questions, listening deeply, and suspending judgment. | Conducting "cultural listening sessions" before deploying a product feature in a new market. |
| 5 | **Cultural Intelligence (CQ)** | Capability to relate and work effectively across cultures, adapting style without losing authenticity. | Adjusting meeting facilitation norms for a distributed team spanning India, Nigeria, and Germany. |
| 6 | **Collaboration** | Empowering all voices, creating psychological safety, and valuing group intelligence over individual authority. | Rotating technical decision-making roles so no single architect gatekeeps all design choices. |

**Application Rule:** Assess team leads on all 6Cs quarterly. A leader scoring high on Commitment but low on Courage is at risk of performative inclusion.

---

### 2.2 Edmondson Psychological Safety Ladder

Amy Edmondson's research at Harvard defines psychological safety as a prerequisite for team learning and innovation. The ladder model describes four ascending levels:

| Level | Label | Team Behavior | Leadership Action Required |
|-------|-------|---------------|---------------------------|
| 1 | **Silence Zone** | Members withhold concerns; errors are hidden. | Explicitly invite dissent; reward early error reporting. |
| 2 | **Polite Participation** | Members contribute ideas only when asked; avoid contradiction. | Ask "what am I missing?" and follow up on quieter contributors post-meeting. |
| 3 | **Confident Contribution** | Members share perspectives proactively and challenge ideas respectfully. | Codify norms; document disagreements as part of decision records. |
| 4 | **Courageous Learning** | Members experiment, fail publicly, and share learnings broadly. | Create blameless postmortems; celebrate attempts not just outcomes. |

**IT Application:** Teams at Level 1 during incident reviews will systematically underreport near-misses, creating systemic vulnerability. Target Level 3 minimum for production-critical AI systems.

---

### 2.3 Inclusive Meeting Design Framework

Structured to address the core failure mode identified in the scenario: meetings dominated by senior/louder voices.

**The PREP-RUN-FOLLOW Model:**

**PREP (Before Meeting)**
- Distribute agenda and pre-read materials 48 hours in advance (removes real-time processing disadvantage for non-native speakers, introverts, neurodiverse contributors)
- Collect written input via async channel (Slack, Notion, shared doc) before synchronous session
- Assign rotating facilitation role — not always the most senior person

**RUN (During Meeting)**
- Explicit invitation to silent contributors: "We haven't heard from [Name] — what's your read?"
- No HiPPO (Highest Paid Person's Opinion) rule: senior person shares last
- Time-box contributions equally; use anonymous digital voting for polarizing decisions (e.g., Mentimeter, MURAL)

**FOLLOW (After Meeting)**
- Circulate decision record with rationale within 24 hours
- Async comment window: 48 hours to surface dissent post-meeting
- Track voice distribution metrics across recurring meeting series

---

### 2.4 Equitable Feedback Model

Addresses the research-documented disparity in feedback quality across demographic groups.

**The STAR-E Framework (Situation → Task → Action → Result → Equity Check):**

| Step | Question to Answer | Inclusion Test |
|------|-------------------|----------------|
| **S** — Situation | What was the specific context? | Would this context be described identically if the employee were from a different group? |
| **T** — Task | What was the expectation or standard? | Was the standard communicated equitably and clearly in advance? |
| **A** — Action | What specific behavior did the employee exhibit? | Is the critique behavior-based or personality/identity-coded? |
| **R** — Result | What was the measurable impact? | Is the impact assessment calibrated against the same benchmark for all team members? |
| **E** — Equity Check | Does this feedback match the specificity and developmental orientation of feedback given to majority peers? | Run a blind-review calibration: would this feedback hold if the name/demographic were changed? |

## 3. Formulas / Thresholds / Decision Rules

### 3.1 Inclusive Leadership Assessment Score

**Formula:**

```
ILA Score = (Σ Behavioral Indicator Scores across 6Cs) / (Total Possible Score) × 100
```

**Scoring Bands:**

| ILA Score | Classification | Recommended Action |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| 85–100 | Exemplary Inclusive Leader | Deploy as inclusion mentor/coach for peers |
| 70–84 | Developing Inclusive Leader | Targeted coaching on lowest-scoring C |
| 55–69 | Awareness-Level Leader | Structured inclusion development plan |
| Below 55 | Compliance-Risk Leader | Mandatory inclusion training; no people management expansion |

**Assessment Sources:** Self-rating (20%), manager rating (30%), direct report 360 (50%) — weighted to privilege those most impacted by leadership behavior.

---

### 3.2 Voice Distribution Ratio in Meetings

**Formula:**

```
VDR = (Talk Time of Top-3 Voices) / (Total Meeting Talk Time)
```

**Thresholds:**

| VDR | Interpretation | Action |
|-----|---------------|--------|
| > 0.70 | Severely dominated — inclusion failure | Restructure facilitation immediately; introduce pre-meeting submissions |
| 0.50–0.70 | Moderately dominated | Deploy HiPPO rule; assign explicit speaking invitations |
| 0.30–0.50 | Acceptable distribution | Monitor; maintain structured turn-taking |
| < 0.30 | Highly distributed | Recognize and reinforce facilitation practices |

**Measurement Method:** AI-assisted transcription tools (Otter.ai, Fireflies) can auto-generate speaker time-distribution reports for recurring meetings.

---

### 3.3 Sponsorship-to-Mentorship Ratio

**Formula:**

```
S:M Ratio = (Active Sponsorship Actions in Review Period) / (Mentoring Sessions in Review Period)
```

**Decision Rule:** For leaders managing teams with underrepresented members, target S:M Ratio ≥ 1:3 (at least one active sponsorship action for every three mentoring sessions). Pure mentoring without sponsorship fails to convert guidance into career advancement.

**Sponsorship Actions Include:** Project nomination, promotion recommendation, conference/publication advocacy, public credit attribution, client relationship transfer.

---

### 3.4 Equitable Feedback Calibration Threshold

**Formula:**

```
Feedback Specificity Score = (Behavior-Referenced Statements) / (Total Feedback Statements) × 100
```

**Calibration Threshold:** FSS ≥ 80% for all team members. If average FSS for underrepresented employees falls more than 15 percentage points below the team average, a calibration session is mandatory before performance ratings are finalized.

**Decision Rule:** If a manager cannot provide at least three behavior-specific development actions for each direct report, the performance review is incomplete regardless of rating score.

## 4. Do / Don't

### Domain: IT / AI / Consulting Inclusive Leadership

| # | DO | DON'T |
|---|-----|-------|
| 1 | Solicit written async input before live architecture review meetings, especially from distributed team members. | Call only synchronous, real-time-only meetings and treat in-room consensus as "the team's decision." |
| 2 | Assign high-visibility projects (AI model ownership, client-facing delivery leadership) deliberately to underrepresented team members with adequate support. | Reserve stretch assignments informally for those the manager feels "most comfortable" with — typically majority-group members. |
| 3 | Use structured interview rubrics with pre-defined behavioral anchors before seeing any candidates. | Conduct unstructured "culture fit" interviews that encode affinity bias as a selection criterion. |
| 4 | Name the pattern when meetings are dominated by certain voices: "I want to make sure we hear from everyone — [Name], what's your perspective?" | Assume silence equals agreement or that the loudest voice reflects team consensus. |
| 5 | Run blameless postmortems that focus on system failures and learning, not individual blame. | Publicly attribute production incidents to individual engineers, chilling future error reporting. |
| 6 | Calibrate performance ratings across the team using demographic-blind review of evidence before submitting scores. | Submit ratings based on narrative impressions formed throughout the year without structured evidence review. |
| 7 | Actively sponsor underrepresented team members to clients, senior stakeholders, and external conferences — by name and with endorsement. | Only mentor in private 1:1s while leaving public advocacy to organic, network-dependent opportunity. |
| 8 | Track task distribution data (who gets glamour work vs. housekeeping work) and correct imbalances proactively. | Assign administrative tasks, note-taking, and scheduling disproportionately to junior women and underrepresented staff. |
| 9 | Make AI model bias audits a team-wide responsibility with rotating ownership, not a single person's side project. | Treat algorithmic fairness as an ethics team or legal team problem, disconnected from engineering leadership. |
| 10 | Publish team norms, meeting ground rules, and communication expectations in writing for all new team members. | Rely on implicit cultural knowledge that advantages those already socialized into majority group norms. |
| 11 | Seek feedback from direct reports about your own inclusion behaviors quarterly — not just annually. | Wait for formal HR 360 cycles to learn whether your leadership behaviors are landing inclusively. |
| 12 | Interrupt microaggressions in real time using low-escalation language: "I want to pause there — let's make sure we're being precise about what we mean." | Stay silent during microaggressions to preserve meeting flow, implicitly validating the behavior. |

## 5. Metric-Driven Scenarios with Anti-Examples

### Scenario 5.1 — Engineering Manager: Inclusive Sprint Review

**Context:** A senior engineering manager at a mid-size SaaS company leads a 14-person backend team with engineers across India, Poland, and the US. Sprint reviews are consistently dominated by three US-based senior engineers. Remote engineers rarely speak, and their contributions go uncredited.

**Inclusive Practice Applied:**
- Implemented pre-review async update template: each engineer documents their sprint contributions in a shared doc 24 hours before the review
- Manager explicitly reads and credits async contributions during the live review
- Rotated "demo presenter" role to ensure every engineer presents work to stakeholders quarterly
- Voice Distribution Ratio tracked over 3 months: dropped from 0.74 (severely dominated) to 0.41 (acceptable)

**Metrics:**
- VDR improved from 0.74 to 0.41 over 12 weeks
- eNPS for remote engineers increased from 22 to 51
- Bug attribution accuracy improved 34% as engineers felt safe flagging their own errors early

**Anti-Example:**
Manager holds the same live-only sprint reviews. Senior US engineers present most of the work, including work done by remote teammates. Manager interprets low remote engagement as "cultural preference for listening." Remote engineer attrition doubles over two quarters. Exit interviews surface "invisibility" as primary driver.

**Anti-Example Failure Mode:** Misattributing structural exclusion to cultural or personality traits removes manager accountability and embeds inequity.

---

### Scenario 5.2 — AI Team Lead: Managing Neurodiverse Contributors

**Context:** An AI/ML team lead manages a team of eight, including two engineers with ADHD and one with autism spectrum traits. Standard sprint planning sessions involve 90-minute live discussions with rapid topic switching. Neurodiverse team members struggle to contribute in real time and are consistently left out of key design decisions.

**Inclusive Practice Applied:**
- Restructured sprint planning to include a written pre-planning document circulated 48 hours ahead (user stories, constraints, open questions)
- Introduced async comment period: contributors add written design reactions before live session begins
- Live session restructured to 45 minutes maximum, focused only on unresolved questions
- Assigned "design decision recorder" role to ensure all contributions — live and async — are captured and attributed

**Metrics:**
- Neurodiverse engineers' documented contributions to design decisions increased from 2 (per quarter) to 11
- Post-sprint retrospective psychological safety scores increased from 3.1/5.0 to 4.4/5.0
- Model accuracy in a critical NLP project improved 12% after incorporating an architectural suggestion from a previously marginalized team member

**Anti-Example:**
Team lead assumes the "best ideas surface naturally" in open live discussion. Neurodiverse engineers are perceived as "disengaged" or "not strategic." A key model architecture flaw is caught in production that had been flagged in writing by one of these engineers three months prior — a comment that was never surfaced in the live meeting and therefore never acted upon.

**Anti-Example Failure Mode:** "Best idea wins in live debate" encodes neurotypical performance as a proxy for technical merit.

---

### Scenario 5.3 — Consulting Partner: Inclusive Client Engagement

**Context:** A consulting partner leads a 10-person team delivering a digital transformation engagement for a financial services client in South Korea. The team includes four Korean-American consultants and six US-based team members. The partner runs all client-facing meetings in Western facilitation style (direct challenge, open debate, individual idea ownership) — a norm that creates friction with the client's high-context, hierarchical communication culture.

**Inclusive Practice Applied:**
- Partner conducted a cultural intelligence briefing with the team before the first client session, reviewing South Korean professional norms (hierarchy respect, group consensus, indirect communication)
- Adapted client meeting structure: pre-circulated proposals for client review before sessions; avoided direct public contradiction of senior client stakeholders
- Identified Korean-American team member as cultural liaison and elevated their role in client relationship management
- Ran internal team debriefs in a Western facilitation style where direct debate was appropriate, while maintaining adapted norms with the client

**Metrics:**
- Client satisfaction NPS increased from 38 (after Phase 1) to 67 (after Phase 2)
- Proposal acceptance rate improved from 55% to 82% across client senior leadership
- Korean-American consultant promoted to engagement manager, attributed in part to visible client relationship contribution

**Anti-Example:**
Partner continues Western facilitation style throughout. Client stakeholders become progressively less engaged; feedback surfaces through back channels that the consulting team "doesn't listen." Senior client sponsor reduces executive access. Engagement contract renewal negotiations stall. Partner attributes failure to "client resistance to change" rather than engagement design mismatch.

**Anti-Example Failure Mode:** Treating one cultural norm as universal transfers the burden of adaptation entirely onto the client, damaging trust and outcomes simultaneously.

## 6. Practitioner Playbook

### 12-Step Playbook: Building Inclusive Leadership Practices in Daily Technical Work

**Target Audience:** Tech leads, engineering managers, AI product leads, and senior consultants transitioning from individual contributor excellence to inclusive people leadership.

---

**Step 1: Conduct a Personal Bias Audit**
Before changing team practices, map your own dominant patterns. Which team members do you seek input from first? Who do you assign stretch work to? Use your calendar and task-assignment history as data. Bias audit is the entry point — not a one-time exercise.

**Step 2: Measure the Baseline**
Quantify current Voice Distribution Ratio across your last five recurring meetings. Run a demographic analysis of task assignments (glamour work vs. housekeeping work) for the past quarter. Baseline data makes inclusion progress measurable, not aspirational.

**Step 3: Redesign Your Meeting Architecture**
Apply the PREP-RUN-FOLLOW model to all recurring meetings. Mandate async pre-submissions for any decision-impacting session. Introduce rotating facilitation. Remove the default assumption that the most senior person leads every conversation.

**Step 4: Implement Structured Decision Records**
For every significant technical or product decision, document: who was consulted, what alternatives were considered, whose input was received asynchronously vs. synchronously, and what was the final rationale. This creates an auditable trail of inclusive process.

**Step 5: Establish Equitable Task Distribution Protocols**
Create a transparent task assignment board. Rotate high-visibility work (client presentations, demo ownership, patent filing, architecture decisions) across all qualified team members. Make the rotation logic explicit and visible.

**Step 6: Deliver Feedback Using the STAR-E Model**
Convert all performance conversations to behavior-specific, equity-calibrated feedback. Before finalizing any review, apply the equity check: would this feedback match what you gave a demographically different peer for equivalent work?

**Step 7: Build a Sponsorship Practice**
Identify one underrepresented team member per quarter whom you will actively sponsor — nominate by name for a project, recommend for a conference speaking slot, or introduce to a senior stakeholder. Document sponsorship actions separately from mentoring.

**Step 8: Create Psychological Safety Rituals**
Open retrospectives with a "what went wrong and what did we learn?" prompt modeled by the leader sharing their own mistake first. Institutionalize blameless postmortems. Publicly recognize early error reporting as a leadership behavior, not a weakness.

**Step 9: Learn Cultural Intelligence for Your Team Context**
If your team spans cultures, invest in structured CQ learning. Read one substantive resource per quarter on a culture represented in your team. Debrief with a team member from that culture to test your understanding.

**Step 10: Run Quarterly 360 Inclusion Pulse Checks**
Ask three questions to your direct reports quarterly (anonymized): (1) Do you feel your input is heard and credited? (2) Have you received feedback specific enough to act on? (3) Is there any team pattern that makes it harder for you to contribute? Act on at least one finding per cycle.

**Step 11: Set Measurable Inclusion Goals with Public Accountability**
Define two to three quantified inclusion goals per half-year (e.g., "VDR below 0.45 in all sprint reviews," "100% of direct reports receive three behavior-specific feedback points per cycle"). Report progress to your team — not just upward.

**Step 12: Model Leader as Learner Publicly**
Attend inclusion workshops alongside your team, not separately. Share what you are learning and where you are struggling. When you make an exclusionary mistake — and you will — name it, correct it, and describe what you will do differently. Modeling imperfect learning is more powerful than performing expertise.

## 7. Content Critique

### Gap Analysis: Where Mainstream Inclusive Leadership Frameworks Fall Short

---

**Gap 1: Remote and Asynchronous Inclusive Leadership**

Most foundational inclusive leadership frameworks (Deloitte 6Cs, Edmondson's work) were developed in and primarily validated against co-located team contexts. The shift to distributed-first, asynchronous-primary teams in post-2020 tech and consulting work has exposed critical gaps.

Key gaps include:
- **Presence bias in remote settings:** Leaders disproportionately evaluate and advocate for those who show up in synchronous video calls, inadvertently penalizing caregivers, those in disadvantaged time zones, and those who communicate better in writing than in real-time verbal exchange.
- **The "camera-on" equity problem:** Mandating cameras conflates video performance with engagement, disadvantaging employees with bandwidth constraints, disability-related needs, or caregiving responsibilities.
- **Async contribution crediting:** Current frameworks provide no mechanism for attributing and valuing asynchronous intellectual contributions at par with live meeting inputs. This structurally devalues the work of thoughtful async contributors.
- **Missing framework:** A dedicated Async-First Inclusive Leadership Model that addresses contribution equity across synchronous and asynchronous channels has not been standardized in mainstream literature.

**Recommendation:** Extend the Inclusive Meeting Design Framework with explicit async equity provisions and measure contribution quality, not channel, as the leadership input standard.

---

**Gap 2: AI-Augmented Decision-Making and Inclusion**

As organizations increasingly deploy AI tools (LLMs, recommendation engines, automated performance systems) in people management, a new class of inclusion risk emerges that current frameworks do not address.

Key gaps include:
- **Algorithmic bias laundering:** AI-generated performance summaries, attrition risk scores, or promotion probability models trained on historical data encode and amplify past exclusions. Leaders who defer to these outputs without equity scrutiny perpetuate systemic bias under a veneer of objectivity.
- **Differential AI access:** When AI productivity tools (coding assistants, summarization tools) are not equitably accessible across global teams — due to language model performance disparities, infrastructure constraints, or license allocation — they create new performance gaps that leaders mis-attribute to individual capability differences.
- **AI facilitation in meetings:** Emerging AI meeting tools optimize for engagement patterns that may mirror majority-group communication styles, potentially further marginalizing non-dominant contributors.
- **Missing framework:** No mainstream inclusive leadership model yet provides a structured protocol for AI-equity auditing as a leader responsibility.

**Recommendation:** Inclusive leaders must treat AI tool auditing as a direct leadership accountability — not an engineering or ethics team specialty — with explicit equity checkpoints before AI outputs inform people decisions.

---

**Gap 3: Global Leadership Norm Pluralism**

Current inclusive leadership frameworks are disproportionately rooted in Western — particularly North American — professional norms. These norms privilege direct communication, individual idea ownership, public self-advocacy, and challenge-based intellectual exchange.

Key gaps include:
- **Defining "speaking up" as universal good:** Psychological safety frameworks measure inclusion partly by willingness to publicly voice dissent. In many East Asian, Middle Eastern, and Latin American professional cultures, this norm conflicts with legitimate cultural practices of deference, consensus-building, and private challenge.
- **Individual vs. collective credit:** Western frameworks center individual contribution crediting; many global cultures prioritize group credit. Current frameworks do not address how to equitably evaluate and credit individual contributions within collectivist team cultures.
- **Facilitation style hegemony:** Inclusive meeting design as currently codified defaults to Western-style open debate. A genuinely culturally intelligent inclusive meeting design must accommodate multiple legitimate facilitation styles simultaneously.
- **Missing framework:** A global norm pluralism layer for inclusive leadership that allows leaders to adapt inclusion practices to local cultural contexts without abandoning equity commitments.

## 8. Quick-Recall Card

### Inclusive Leadership — High-Density Reference

**Core Premise:** Inclusion is a daily leadership behavior, not a demographic statistic. Representation without inclusive practice produces attrition, not equity.

**The 6Cs:** Commitment — Courage — Cognizance — Curiosity — Cultural Intelligence — Collaboration

**Psychological Safety Ladder:** Silence Zone → Polite Participation → Confident Contribution → Courageous Learning (target: Level 3+)

**Meeting Rule:** VDR > 0.70 = inclusion failure. Apply PREP-RUN-FOLLOW. Senior person speaks last.

**Feedback Rule:** FSS ≥ 80%. If underrepresented employees score 15+ points below team average — mandatory calibration before ratings finalize.

**Sponsorship Rule:** S:M Ratio ≥ 1:3. Mentoring alone does not convert to advancement without active advocacy.

**Critical Scenario Logic:**
- VP restructures dominated meetings → pre-meeting submissions + rotating facilitators + anonymous feedback → improved decision quality + team felt valued
- Anti-pattern: attributing structural exclusion to individual personality or cultural preference removes leader accountability

**Bias Interruption Moments:** Hiring rubrics before candidates are reviewed. Task assignment audits quarterly. Performance calibration before ratings submitted.

**Remote/Async Gap:** Presence bias punishes async contributors. Measure contribution quality, not channel.

**AI Equity Gap:** AI performance outputs require human equity audit before people decisions. Never defer to algorithm alone.

**Global Gap:** "Speaking up" is a culturally specific norm. Inclusive leaders adapt facilitation, not just invite diverse attendance.

**ILA Score Benchmark:** ≥ 85 = exemplary. Below 55 = compliance risk. No people management expansion below 55.

**One-sentence test for every team decision:** Did every relevant voice have a real, structured opportunity to contribute before this conclusion was reached?

As a PM/Consultant/AI Lead, the one question to answer with this framework is: "Are the people most impacted by this decision the ones least likely to have been heard in the process that created it, and if so, what did I do to fix that?"
