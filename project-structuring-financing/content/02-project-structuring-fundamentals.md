# Project Structuring Fundamentals

## Overview
Project structuring is the process of designing the legal, financial, and operational architecture of a project so that it can attract financing and operate efficiently. It involves deciding who owns the project, how contracts are organized, how cash flows are directed, and how risks are distributed among participants. Good structuring makes a project investable by creating clarity around rights, obligations, and returns. Poor structuring can kill an otherwise viable project before it breaks ground.

---

## Why It Matters
The structure of a project determines whether it can secure financing, manage its risks, and deliver returns to investors. A well-structured project aligns the interests of sponsors, lenders, governments, and contractors, creating a framework where each party has clear incentives to perform. Structuring decisions made early in the development phase lock in the project's risk profile for decades, so getting them right from the start is critical. Every major infrastructure project that reaches financial close does so because its structure passed intense scrutiny from dozens of stakeholders.

## Key Principles
- The project's legal structure must clearly separate project assets and liabilities from those of the sponsors
- Contractual arrangements should create a closed system where revenues cover costs and debt service
- The capital structure must balance debt and equity to optimize cost of capital while maintaining financial resilience
- Governance arrangements need to define decision-making authority and resolve potential conflicts among stakeholders
- Tax and regulatory optimization can significantly affect project economics and should be addressed early

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Capital structure** | The mix of debt, equity, and other instruments used to finance a project, typically expressed as a debt-to-equity ratio |
| **Financial close** | The point at which all financing agreements are signed and conditions precedent are met, allowing funds to be drawn |
| **Waterfall mechanism** | A priority-based system for distributing project cash flows to operating costs, debt service, reserves, and equity returns |
| **Conditions precedent** | Requirements that must be satisfied before a lender will release funds, such as permits, insurance, and executed contracts |

## Use Case
A developer structuring a toll road project must decide on the ownership entity, negotiate a concession agreement with the government, arrange construction and operating contracts, and design a cash flow waterfall that prioritizes debt repayment before equity distributions.

## Scenario
> An energy company wants to build a combined-cycle gas power plant in Southeast Asia. The structuring team creates a special purpose vehicle in a tax-efficient jurisdiction, negotiates a 25-year power purchase agreement with the state utility, secures a gas supply agreement with a national oil company, and designs a capital structure with 75% senior debt and 25% equity. The waterfall mechanism ensures that operating expenses and debt service are paid before any dividends flow to the sponsor.

## Examples
- A desalination plant structured with a build-operate-transfer model where the government receives ownership after 30 years of private operation
- A rail project structured as a joint venture between a construction company and a rolling stock manufacturer, with each partner contributing specialized capabilities and sharing equity proportionally

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### 1) Structure Stack Matrix
**Purpose:** Show how ownership, contracts, cash flow, and governance fit together.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Ownership -> Contracts -> Cash Flow -> Governance -> Tax / Regulatory
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1, Ownership: who holds the project and its assets.
Component 2, Contracts: who supplies, buys, builds, and operates.
Component 3, Cash Flow: how revenue moves through the project.
Component 4, Governance: who can decide, approve, or block actions.
Component 5, Tax / Regulatory: where the project lives and what rules apply.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting-led consortium structures an AI data center through a project company, signs a long-term compute off-take agreement, assigns construction and operating contracts, and uses a governance board with clear approval rights. The decision is whether the structure is clean enough to attract project debt and still allow the sponsors to control strategy.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the team asks whether a legal, contract, or tax choice will break the financeable structure.

### 2) Capital Stack Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide the balance of debt and equity that can survive downside cases.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
               More Debt
            +-------------------+
 High Risk  | Too aggressive    | Optimized if cash flow is strong
            | leverage          | and contracts are durable
------------+-------------------+----------------
 Low Risk   | Conservative      | Flexible, but maybe expensive
            | underleveraged    | if equity is too high
            +-------------------+
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1, Debt: cheaper capital but higher fixed obligations.
Component 2, Equity: more flexible capital but usually more expensive.
Component 3, Resilience: whether the structure still works under stress.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A product company funding a private AI lab chooses 70% debt and 30% equity only after modeling utilization downside and reserve accounts. The decision is to balance cost of capital against the risk of breaching covenants.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone wants to push leverage higher without showing the downside math.

### 3) Close-Readiness Checklist
**Purpose:** Confirm whether the project is ready for financial close.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Permits + Insurance + Signed Contracts + Governance + Financing Docs = Close Ready
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1, Permits and insurance: legal and operational prerequisites.
Component 2, Signed contracts: revenue, supply, and construction certainty.
Component 3, Governance: decision rights, board approvals, and controls.
Component 4, Financing docs: debt terms, equity commitments, and conditions precedent.

IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS infrastructure campus is ready for close only when the data center lease, energy contract, and build contract are all executed, and the lenders have verified reserve accounts and ownership structure. The decision is whether the project can draw funds now or needs another round of document cleanup.

When to pull this out in a meeting: When the transaction is "almost closed" but nothing has actually been funded yet.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: Debt-to-equity ratio = total debt / total equity

Variables:
Total debt = all interest-bearing project borrowings.
Total equity = sponsor and other equity contributions.

Why this formula exists: It answers how leveraged the project is and whether the capital stack is balanced.

How to interpret the output:
Value < 1.0 -> equity-heavy structure -> safer but more expensive.
Value 1.0-3.0 -> moderate leverage -> often financeable if cash flow is stable.
Value > 3.0 -> highly leveraged -> risky unless contracts and reserves are very strong.

Worked example with numbers: An AI data center project uses $75 million of debt and $25 million of equity. Debt-to-equity ratio = 3.0 [verified from model knowledge, not source]. The decision is to test whether the cash flow can absorb shocks before approving the structure.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Don't treat structure as paperwork after the business case is done. | Do design structure early because it shapes the project's risk profile for decades. |
| Don't leave ownership and governance vague. | Do define who owns the project and who can approve key decisions. |
| Don't let cash distributions happen in random order. | Do use a waterfall mechanism that protects operating costs and debt service first. |
| Don't assume a project is financeable just because the idea is good. | Do prove investability through contracts, cash flow, and close readiness. |
| Don't ignore tax and regulatory design until the end. | Do address tax and regulatory optimization early in the structure. |
| Don't force one funding mix on every project. | Do tune the capital stack to the project's risk and resilience needs. |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI data center project close
Situation: A consortium wants to build a private AI data center for an enterprise software customer. The sponsors need a structure that separates project risk from the parent company and satisfies lenders.
Applicable framework/metric: Structure Stack Matrix + debt-to-equity ratio.
Analysis: The project company holds the asset, the customer signs a 10-year compute agreement, and debt-to-equity is modeled at 2.8x. Conditions precedent include permits, insurance, and an executed construction contract.
Decision rule: If contracts are signed and debt-to-equity stays below 3.0x, the structure is close to financeable. If conditions precedent are incomplete or leverage rises above 3.5x, delay close.
Action: Finish the contract stack, lock governance rights, and test the downside case before funding.

Scenario 2: Consulting-led digital operations hub
Situation: A consulting group is structuring a digital operations hub for multiple enterprise clients. The team wants the structure to be investable and operationally clean.
Applicable framework/metric: Close-Readiness Checklist.
Analysis: The project has permits, insurance, and three signed service contracts, but the reserve account and board approval matrix are still unfinished. Close readiness is 4 of 5 items.
Decision rule: If fewer than 5 of 5 close items are complete, do not draw funds. If all items are complete, proceed to financial close.
Action: Complete the reserve account documents and finalize governance approvals before opening the funding line.

Scenario 3: Product company capital stack decision
Situation: A product company is funding a private AI lab that will support a premium enterprise feature set. Finance is debating whether to use more debt to lower the cost of capital.
Applicable framework/metric: Capital Stack Matrix.
Analysis: The project can handle moderate leverage, but downside utilization falls 17% in the stress case. Equity at 30% keeps the structure resilient, while pushing debt to 85% of capital creates covenant risk.
Decision rule: If downside DSCR and leverage stay within lender limits, keep the structure. If leverage pushes the project into a fragile quadrant, bring in more equity.
Action: Rebuild the sensitivity table, cap leverage at the resilient level, and show the board both base and downside outcomes.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Map the project as a separate entity before negotiating the first contract.
2. Define ownership, control rights, and sponsor obligations in one governance chart.
3. Build the contract stack around revenue certainty, supply certainty, and delivery certainty.
4. Design the cash flow waterfall so operating costs and debt service are protected first.
5. Set the capital structure only after testing downside cases and covenant headroom.
6. Clear every condition precedent before planning financial close.
7. Re-check tax and regulatory assumptions before documents are signed.
