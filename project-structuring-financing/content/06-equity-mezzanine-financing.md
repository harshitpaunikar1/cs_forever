# Equity and Mezzanine Financing

## Overview
Equity is the risk capital that project sponsors contribute, sitting at the bottom of the capital structure and absorbing losses first. Mezzanine financing occupies the layer between senior debt and equity, offering lenders higher returns in exchange for being subordinated to senior creditors. Together, equity and mezzanine fill the gap that senior debt cannot cover, making the overall financing package complete.

---

## Why It Matters
Lenders will not fund a project unless sponsors have meaningful equity at risk, because it aligns incentives and provides a cushion against losses. Mezzanine instruments can reduce the amount of pure equity required, improving returns for sponsors while still giving senior lenders a comfortable buffer. The balance between equity, mezzanine, and senior debt defines who bears losses and who captures upside.

## Key Principles
- Equity investors expect higher returns than debt holders because they bear more risk and are paid last
- Mezzanine debt is typically unsecured or second-lien and carries a higher interest rate than senior debt
- Sponsors often use holding-company structures to inject equity and manage tax efficiency
- Institutional investors such as pension funds and infrastructure funds increasingly provide equity for large projects

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Sponsor Equity** | Cash or assets contributed by the project developers who initiate and manage the project |
| **Mezzanine Debt** | Subordinated financing that ranks below senior debt but above equity in the repayment waterfall |
| **Subordination** | The order in which claims are paid, with subordinated claims settled only after senior claims |
| **Equity IRR** | The internal rate of return earned by equity investors after all debt obligations are met |

## Use Case
A highway concession requires 30 percent equity. The sponsor contributes 20 percent from its own funds and raises 10 percent as mezzanine debt from an infrastructure fund, keeping the senior debt ratio at 70 percent to satisfy lender requirements.

## Scenario
> A consortium bidding on an airport expansion needed to demonstrate 25 percent equity to win the concession. Two construction firms contributed 15 percent as sponsor equity, and a pension fund provided 10 percent as convertible mezzanine notes. The mezzanine carried a 12 percent coupon but could convert to equity if the airport exceeded traffic targets, giving the pension fund a share of the upside.

## Examples
- A private equity firm investing in a water treatment plant through a special purpose vehicle and earning returns via dividends after debt service
- A development finance institution providing subordinated loans to a renewable energy project to crowd in commercial senior debt

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### 1) Capital Stack Waterfall
**Purpose:** Show who gets paid first and who takes losses first.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Project value first goes to senior debt
Then mezzanine claims
Then equity
Losses hit equity first, then mezzanine, then senior debt
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Senior debt: cheapest capital, protected by priority.
Mezzanine: higher-risk bridge layer.
Equity: residual owner of upside and downside.
Waterfall: payment and loss order.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: An AI infrastructure SPV funds a model-training cluster with senior debt, mezzanine notes, and sponsor equity. If operating cash weakens, the sponsor loses first, mezzanine next, and senior debt last.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When someone asks who should own the next dollar of risk or upside.

### 2) Return Stack Matrix
**Purpose:** Match return expectation to position in the capital structure.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Lower risk -> lower return
Higher risk -> higher return
Senior debt  -> lowest return
Mezzanine    -> middle return
Equity       -> highest return
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Risk level: how exposed the investor is to project failure.
Expected return: how much compensation the layer needs.
Priority: where the claim sits in the waterfall.
Control: how much influence the provider may seek.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A SaaS platform expansion uses senior debt for the stable recurring base, mezzanine for the growth gap, and sponsor equity for the highest-risk launch phase. The return stack keeps each investor in the risk zone they expect.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When negotiating pricing, conversion rights, or whether to use equity versus mezzanine.

### 3) Equity vs Mezzanine Decision Matrix
**Purpose:** Decide whether the gap should be filled with more equity or a subordinated instrument.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Need more certainty -> equity
Need less dilution  -> mezzanine
Need lower leverage risk -> equity
Need capital efficiency  -> mezzanine
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Dilution: how much ownership the sponsor gives up.
Cost: how expensive the capital is.
Flexibility: how much structure can be customized.
Priority: how exposed the capital is to loss.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: A consulting-led enterprise rollout wants to avoid over-diluting the sponsor, so it adds mezzanine notes instead of forcing a larger equity cheque. That keeps control with the sponsor while still satisfying senior lenders.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When the sponsor asks how to fund the gap without giving away too much ownership.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: `Equity IRR = discount rate where equity NPV = 0`
Variables:
Equity cash flows = after-debt cash paid to equity holders
Discount rate = the return rate that sets NPV to zero
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the sponsor's risk-adjusted return is attractive.
How to interpret the output:
Value < target return -> unattractive or too diluted
Value near target return -> structure needs tightening
Value > target return -> acceptable, assuming risk is controlled
Worked example with numbers: A product platform SPV returns 14 percent equity IRR after debt and mezzanine service. If the sponsor target is 12 percent, the structure is acceptable; if mezzanine pricing pushes IRR below target, the capital stack needs rework.

Formula: `Subordinated capital = total project need - senior debt`
Variables:
Total project need = the full amount the project must raise
Senior debt = the portion lenders are willing to fund first
Why this formula exists: It answers how much equity or mezzanine is needed to close the gap.
How to interpret the output:
Value = 0 -> senior debt alone is enough
Value > 0 -> gap must be filled by equity or mezzanine
Value too large -> project may be too risky for current leverage
Worked example with numbers: An AI deployment project needs 100 of capital and senior lenders will provide 70. The remaining 30 must come from sponsor equity, mezzanine debt, or both.

Formula: `Mezzanine return = coupon + conversion upside`
Variables:
Coupon = fixed or floating periodic cash return
Conversion upside = value gained if the note converts to equity
Why this formula exists: It explains why mezzanine can be cheaper than pure equity yet riskier than senior debt.
How to interpret the output:
Value mostly coupon -> behaves more like debt
Value balanced with conversion -> hybrid capital
Value heavily conversion-driven -> behaves more like equity
Worked example with numbers: A mezzanine note pays a 12 percent coupon and can convert if the project outperforms the base case. The lender gets steady income plus upside, while the sponsor avoids a larger equity cheque.

## 5. Do vs Don't

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat every funding gap as more senior debt | Use mezzanine or equity when senior debt should not be stretched |
| Ignore subordination | Make the payment and loss order explicit in the term sheet |
| Overuse equity and dilute the sponsor unnecessarily | Use mezzanine when you need capital efficiency and can price the extra risk |
| Price mezzanine like senior debt | Charge a higher return because mezzanine sits below senior claims |
| Skip the SPV structure | Ring-fence the project so cash flow and risk stay isolated |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)

### Scenario 1: AI Data Center Capital Stack
Situation: An AI data center sponsor needs to fund build-out without over-diluting the parent company. Senior lenders will only cover 70 percent of project need.
Applicable framework/metric: Equity IRR and capital stack waterfall.
Analysis: Senior debt covers 70, mezzanine covers 10, and sponsor equity covers 20. The sponsor checks whether the resulting equity IRR still clears the target after mezzanine coupon costs.
Decision rule: If equity IRR > target, proceed; if near target, resize mezzanine; if below target, add equity or improve project economics.
Action: Keep the SPV structure, document subordination clearly, and ask the mezzanine provider whether convertibility should be included.

### Scenario 2: SaaS Expansion With Controlled Dilution
Situation: A SaaS company wants to fund international expansion but does not want to give away too much ownership. The board prefers a financing layer that behaves like debt now and may convert later.
Applicable framework/metric: Equity vs mezzanine decision matrix.
Analysis: The project needs 30 of subordinate capital. Pure equity would solve the gap but dilute the founders; mezzanine notes could bridge the gap with a coupon and possible conversion.
Decision rule: If dilution pressure is high, use mezzanine; if risk is too high for a subordinated layer, add equity instead.
Action: Compare coupon, conversion terms, and expected equity IRR before signing the term sheet.

### Scenario 3: Consulting-Led Implementation Vehicle
Situation: A consulting firm creates a special purpose vehicle for a multi-year enterprise transformation program. Senior debt will fund the stable delivery stream, while mezzanine fills the remaining funding gap.
Applicable framework/metric: Loss waterfall and return stack.
Analysis: If project value weakens, senior debt still gets paid first, mezzanine absorbs the next layer of pain, and equity is first in line for losses. That hierarchy explains the pricing spread between the layers.
Decision rule: If the downside still leaves mezzanine whole, proceed; if mezzanine looks exposed, shift more risk to equity; if even senior debt is threatened, the structure is too aggressive.
Action: Re-price the capital stack, document intercreditor rights, and keep the SPV ring-fenced from other business lines.

## 7. Implementation Playbook

1. Map the capital stack with senior debt, mezzanine, and equity in separate layers.
2. Put the project into an SPV so claims and cash flows stay ring-fenced.
3. Write the waterfall so payment order and loss order are visible to all parties.
4. Model sponsor equity, mezzanine coupon, and any conversion features separately.
5. Test equity IRR after debt service and mezzanine costs, not before them.
6. Compare dilution against financing flexibility before choosing equity or mezzanine.
7. Check tax efficiency and holding-company structure early so legal form does not block closing.
8. Confirm institutional investor appetite before relying on non-standard capital.
