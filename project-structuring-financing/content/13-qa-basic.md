# Practice Q&A — Basic

Foundational definitions and concepts covering all 12 topics in the Project Structuring and Financing course.

---

## Q1. What is project finance?

**Level:** Basic

Project finance is a method of funding large-scale infrastructure and industrial projects where lenders rely primarily on the project's own cash flows for repayment rather than on the balance sheet of the sponsors. The project's assets, rights, and revenues serve as collateral. This structure isolates the project's financial risk from the sponsors' other business activities.

**Real-life applications:**
- Financing a new toll highway without putting the construction company's other assets at risk
- Funding a power plant where electricity sales revenue is the primary source of debt repayment
- Building a desalination plant through a standalone entity with ring-fenced cash flows
- Developing an offshore wind farm using non-recourse lending structures

**Key concepts:** `non-recourse financing`, `project cash flows`, `special purpose vehicle`, `limited recourse`, `ring-fencing`

---

## Q2. What is a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) and why is it used?

**Level:** Basic

An SPV is a legally separate entity created solely to own and operate a specific project. It holds all project assets, contracts, and liabilities. The SPV structure ensures that the project's risks and debts do not contaminate the sponsor's balance sheet and vice versa, providing bankruptcy remoteness for lenders.

**Real-life applications:**
- A consortium creating an SPV to hold the concession rights for a metro rail project
- A developer establishing an SPV for each wind farm to isolate financial risks across a portfolio
- Banks requiring an SPV structure before approving project finance loans
- Governments mandating SPV formation as part of PPP procurement requirements

**Key concepts:** `SPV`, `bankruptcy remoteness`, `ring-fencing`, `legal separation`, `project company`

---

## Q3. What is the difference between recourse and non-recourse financing?

**Level:** Basic

In recourse financing, lenders can claim the borrower's other assets if the project defaults. In non-recourse financing, lenders can only look to the project's own assets and cash flows for repayment. Most project finance deals are structured as limited recourse, meaning sponsors provide some support during construction but recourse falls away once the project is operational.

**Real-life applications:**
- A sponsor providing a completion guarantee that converts to non-recourse after commercial operation
- Lenders accepting non-recourse terms for a mature toll road with a proven traffic record
- A development bank offering limited-recourse loans to encourage private infrastructure investment
- An investor choosing project finance over corporate finance to protect its balance sheet

**Key concepts:** `recourse`, `non-recourse`, `limited recourse`, `completion guarantee`, `balance sheet protection`

---

## Q4. What are the main parties in a project finance transaction?

**Level:** Basic

The main parties include sponsors (equity investors who develop the project), lenders (banks or bondholders who provide debt), the project company or SPV, the construction contractor, the operator, the off-taker (who buys the project's output), and often a government grantor in the case of concessions. Each party has distinct roles, risks, and returns.

**Real-life applications:**
- A construction firm acting as both sponsor and EPC contractor for a highway project
- A utility company serving as the off-taker for a gas-fired power plant
- A multilateral bank acting as lender and political risk guarantor simultaneously
- A government granting a concession and also purchasing the project's output

**Key concepts:** `sponsors`, `lenders`, `off-taker`, `EPC contractor`, `project company`

---

## Q5. What does "bankability" mean in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

Bankability refers to whether a project's structure, contracts, and risk profile are acceptable to commercial lenders. A bankable project has predictable cash flows, clearly allocated risks, enforceable contracts, and sufficient coverage ratios. If a project is not bankable, it cannot attract debt financing on reasonable terms.

**Real-life applications:**
- A lender declining a project because the off-take agreement has too short a tenor
- An advisor restructuring contract terms to make a renewable energy project bankable
- A government improving its regulatory framework to make PPP projects bankable for international lenders
- A sponsor adding a reserve account to improve bankability by enhancing debt service coverage

**Key concepts:** `bankability`, `credit risk`, `coverage ratios`, `contract enforceability`, `lender requirements`

---

## Q6. What is a project structure in the context of project finance?

**Level:** Basic

A project structure is the overall arrangement of contracts, entities, and financial instruments that define how a project is organized, financed, built, and operated. It includes the corporate structure (who owns the SPV), the contractual structure (the web of agreements), and the financial structure (the mix of debt and equity). Good structuring aligns incentives and allocates risks efficiently.

**Real-life applications:**
- Choosing between a single SPV and a holding-company structure for tax optimization
- Structuring a project with separate EPC and O&M contracts versus a single turnkey agreement
- Designing a financial structure with 70 percent debt and 30 percent equity based on lender requirements
- Organizing a multi-country project under a master holding company with local SPVs in each jurisdiction

**Key concepts:** `project structure`, `contractual framework`, `financial structure`, `corporate structure`, `incentive alignment`

## Q7. What is a feasibility study and when is it conducted?

**Level:** Basic

A feasibility study is an assessment conducted during the early stages of project development to determine whether a project is technically viable, economically attractive, and financially sound. It typically covers market demand, technical design options, cost estimates, revenue projections, and preliminary risk analysis. The study informs the sponsor's decision to proceed to detailed development.

**Real-life applications:**
- A government commissioning a feasibility study before launching a PPP tender for a new airport
- A developer using feasibility study results to approach lenders for preliminary financing indications
- An investor reviewing a feasibility study to decide whether to join a consortium
- A multilateral bank requiring an independent feasibility study before considering project funding

**Key concepts:** `feasibility study`, `technical viability`, `economic analysis`, `market demand`, `preliminary risk assessment`

## Q8. What is a risk register?

**Level:** Basic

A risk register is a structured document that lists all identified project risks along with their likelihood, potential impact, assigned owner, and planned mitigation measures. It is created during the project development phase and updated throughout the project's life. The risk register serves as the foundation for risk allocation discussions among sponsors, lenders, and contractors.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project team updating the risk register monthly during the construction phase
- Lenders reviewing the risk register as part of their due diligence process
- An independent engineer using the risk register to prioritize monitoring activities
- A sponsor presenting the risk register during investor roadshows to demonstrate risk awareness

**Key concepts:** `risk register`, `risk identification`, `risk mitigation`, `likelihood`, `impact assessment`

## Q9. What is a risk matrix and how does it work?

**Level:** Basic

A risk matrix is a visual tool that plots risks on a grid based on their probability of occurrence and the severity of their impact. Risks in the high-probability, high-impact quadrant receive the most attention and resources. The matrix helps project teams prioritize which risks to mitigate first and communicate risk levels to stakeholders in an accessible format.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project manager color-coding risks as red, amber, or green based on the matrix output
- A board presentation using a risk matrix to summarize the top ten project risks
- An insurance advisor using the matrix to recommend which risks to insure versus retain
- A lender requiring the project company to maintain and update a risk matrix quarterly

**Key concepts:** `risk matrix`, `probability`, `impact`, `risk prioritization`, `risk communication`

## Q10. What is force majeure in a project finance context?

**Level:** Basic

Force majeure refers to extraordinary events beyond the control of the contracting parties, such as natural disasters, wars, or pandemics, that prevent one or both parties from fulfilling their contractual obligations. Project finance contracts typically include force majeure clauses that excuse performance during such events and define the consequences, including potential termination and compensation.

**Real-life applications:**
- A construction contractor invoking force majeure after an earthquake damages the project site
- A power plant operator claiming force majeure when a government-imposed lockdown halts fuel deliveries
- Lenders negotiating force majeure definitions to ensure they cover political events in unstable regions
- An off-taker disputing a force majeure claim because the event was foreseeable and insurable

**Key concepts:** `force majeure`, `excused performance`, `extraordinary events`, `contract relief`, `termination rights`

## Q11. What is the Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)?

**Level:** Basic

The DSCR measures the project's ability to pay its debt obligations in a given period. It is calculated by dividing the cash flow available for debt service by the total debt service (principal plus interest) due in that period. A DSCR above 1.0x means the project generates enough cash to cover its debt payments, while lenders typically require minimum DSCRs of 1.20x to 1.50x depending on the sector.

**Real-life applications:**
- A lender setting a minimum DSCR covenant of 1.30x in the loan agreement
- A financial model showing that the project's DSCR drops below 1.15x in year seven under the downside case
- A project company restricted from paying dividends because the DSCR fell below the lock-up threshold
- An investor using the average DSCR to compare the creditworthiness of two competing projects

**Key concepts:** `DSCR`, `debt service`, `cash flow available for debt service`, `coverage ratio`, `covenant`

## Q12. What is the Internal Rate of Return (IRR)?

**Level:** Basic

The IRR is the discount rate at which the net present value of all future cash flows from an investment equals zero. In project finance, the equity IRR measures the return earned by equity investors after all debt payments are made. A project is considered attractive if its IRR exceeds the investor's hurdle rate or cost of capital.

**Real-life applications:**
- A sponsor targeting a minimum equity IRR of 15 percent for infrastructure investments
- A financial model calculating both project IRR and equity IRR to show returns at different levels
- An investment committee rejecting a project because its IRR falls below the fund's threshold
- A lender verifying that the project IRR is high enough to provide a buffer above the cost of debt

**Key concepts:** `IRR`, `net present value`, `hurdle rate`, `equity IRR`, `project IRR`

## Q13. What is a base case in financial modeling?

**Level:** Basic

The base case is the financial projection built on the most likely set of assumptions about revenues, costs, construction timelines, and macroeconomic conditions. It represents the expected outcome and serves as the reference point against which upside and downside scenarios are compared. Lenders use the base case to size the debt and set repayment schedules.

**Real-life applications:**
- A financial advisor presenting the base case to lenders during the credit approval process
- A sponsor stress-testing the base case by varying traffic growth assumptions by plus or minus two percent
- An independent model auditor verifying that base case assumptions are reasonable and well-documented
- A rating agency using the base case DSCR profile to assign a preliminary credit rating

**Key concepts:** `base case`, `financial projection`, `assumptions`, `debt sizing`, `reference scenario`

## Q14. What is sensitivity analysis in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

Sensitivity analysis tests how changes in a single input variable affect the project's key outputs such as DSCR, IRR, or net present value. By varying one assumption at a time while holding others constant, analysts identify which variables have the greatest impact on project performance. This helps sponsors and lenders understand where the project is most vulnerable.

**Real-life applications:**
- Testing how a 10 percent increase in construction costs affects the equity IRR
- A lender requiring sensitivity tables showing the impact of interest rate changes on the DSCR
- A sponsor discovering that the project is more sensitive to fuel prices than to demand volume
- An investor using tornado charts to visualize which variables drive the most variation in returns

**Key concepts:** `sensitivity analysis`, `input variable`, `output variable`, `tornado chart`, `vulnerability assessment`

## Q15. What is debt financing in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

Debt financing involves borrowing money that must be repaid with interest over a specified period. In project finance, debt typically constitutes 60 to 80 percent of total project costs and is secured by the project's assets and cash flows rather than the sponsors' balance sheets. Debt is cheaper than equity because lenders have priority in the repayment waterfall and bear less risk.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project company drawing down a term loan in tranches as construction progresses
- A bank charging a lower interest rate on senior secured debt compared to unsecured corporate lending
- A project refinancing its construction loan with a long-term bond after reaching commercial operation
- Lenders requiring the project to maintain insurance policies as a condition of the debt facility

**Key concepts:** `debt financing`, `term loan`, `interest rate`, `senior secured debt`, `leverage`

## Q16. What is loan syndication?

**Level:** Basic

Loan syndication is the process of distributing a large loan across multiple lenders so that no single institution bears the full credit exposure. A lead arranger structures the deal, sets terms, and invites other banks to participate. Syndication enables projects to access larger amounts of capital than any individual lender could provide and diversifies risk across the lending group.

**Real-life applications:**
- A mandated lead arranger inviting ten banks to participate in a billion-dollar infrastructure loan
- A project company benefiting from competitive pricing because multiple lenders bid for allocations
- A regional bank participating in a syndicated loan to gain exposure to infrastructure assets
- A development finance institution joining a syndicate to demonstrate confidence and crowd in commercial lenders

**Key concepts:** `syndication`, `lead arranger`, `credit exposure`, `participation`, `risk diversification`

---

## Q17. What is a loan tenor?

**Level:** Basic

The tenor is the total duration of a loan from the date of first drawdown to the date of final repayment. In project finance, tenors typically range from 10 to 25 years, depending on the project's asset life and revenue profile. Longer tenors reduce annual debt service payments but increase total interest costs and expose lenders to more risk over time.

**Real-life applications:**
- A power plant receiving an 18-year loan tenor matched to the 20-year power purchase agreement
- A toll road project negotiating a 25-year tenor to match the concession period
- A lender shortening the tenor to 12 years to reduce long-term credit exposure in a risky jurisdiction
- A mini-perm structure with a 7-year tenor and a mandatory refinancing requirement

**Key concepts:** `tenor`, `loan maturity`, `asset life`, `debt service`, `refinancing`

---

## Q18. What is equity financing in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

Equity financing is the capital contributed by sponsors and investors who become owners of the project company. Equity holders bear the highest risk because they are paid last in the cash flow waterfall and absorb losses first. In return, they receive the residual profits after all operating costs and debt obligations are met, giving them unlimited upside potential.

**Real-life applications:**
- A construction company contributing equity to an SPV to build a water treatment plant
- A pension fund purchasing a minority equity stake in an operational toll road
- A sponsor injecting additional equity during construction to cover cost overruns
- An infrastructure fund earning returns through dividends distributed after debt service

**Key concepts:** `equity financing`, `sponsor contribution`, `residual profits`, `risk absorption`, `ownership stake`

---

## Q19. What is mezzanine financing?

**Level:** Basic

Mezzanine financing is a hybrid form of capital that sits between senior debt and equity in the repayment hierarchy. It typically carries a higher interest rate than senior debt because it is subordinated, meaning it gets repaid only after senior lenders are satisfied. Mezzanine can take the form of subordinated loans, preferred equity, or convertible instruments.

**Real-life applications:**
- A development finance institution providing a subordinated loan to fill the gap between senior debt and sponsor equity
- A mezzanine fund earning a 12 percent coupon plus an equity kicker on a hospital PPP project
- A project using mezzanine to reduce the amount of sponsor equity required and improve equity returns
- A convertible mezzanine note that converts to equity shares if the project exceeds performance targets

**Key concepts:** `mezzanine financing`, `subordinated debt`, `preferred equity`, `convertible instruments`, `capital stack`

## Q20. What is a Public-Private Partnership (PPP)?

**Level:** Basic

A PPP is a long-term contractual arrangement between a government entity and a private company to deliver public infrastructure or services. The private partner typically designs, builds, finances, and operates the asset, while the government defines the service requirements and may contribute funding or guarantees. PPPs aim to combine public-sector oversight with private-sector efficiency.

**Real-life applications:**
- A city government partnering with a private firm to build and operate a wastewater treatment plant for 25 years
- A national highway authority using a PPP to expand a motorway without increasing public debt
- A school district using a PPP to build new facilities where the private partner maintains the buildings
- A government using PPPs to deliver universal broadband coverage in underserved regions

**Key concepts:** `PPP`, `public infrastructure`, `private capital`, `service delivery`, `long-term contract`

---

## Q21. What is an availability payment?

**Level:** Basic

An availability payment is a regular payment made by the government to the private partner in a PPP, conditional on the asset being available and meeting specified performance standards. Unlike user-fee models, the government bears the demand risk and the private partner earns revenue as long as the facility is operational and compliant. Deductions are applied if performance standards are not met.

**Real-life applications:**
- A government paying monthly availability fees for a prison facility that meets occupancy and maintenance standards
- A hospital PPP where payments are reduced if operating rooms are unavailable due to maintenance failures
- A road project where the government pays availability fees rather than relying on uncertain toll revenues
- A defense housing PPP where availability payments cover both construction financing and ongoing facility management

**Key concepts:** `availability payment`, `performance standards`, `demand risk`, `deduction mechanism`, `PPP revenue model`

---

## Q22. What is Value for Money (VfM) in PPPs?

**Level:** Basic

Value for Money is an assessment comparing whether delivering a project through a PPP offers better outcomes per dollar spent than traditional public procurement. It considers lifecycle costs, risk transfer, innovation potential, and delivery speed. A positive VfM finding means the PPP approach is expected to deliver the same or better service at lower total cost to the public.

**Real-life applications:**
- A government treasury conducting a VfM analysis before approving a PPP for a new bridge
- A public comparator model estimating the cost of the government building and operating the facility itself
- An opposition party challenging a PPP decision by arguing that the VfM analysis used unrealistic assumptions
- A multilateral bank requiring VfM documentation before providing guarantees for a PPP project

**Key concepts:** `Value for Money`, `public comparator`, `lifecycle cost`, `risk transfer`, `procurement method`

---

## Q23. What is a concession agreement?

**Level:** Basic

A concession agreement is a contract between a government and a private party granting the right to build, finance, operate, and earn revenue from a public asset for a defined period. At the end of the concession, the asset typically reverts to the government. The agreement specifies construction obligations, operating standards, tariff structures, and termination conditions.

**Real-life applications:**
- A government awarding a 35-year concession to build and operate a container port
- A concession agreement specifying that the toll rate will increase by inflation plus one percent annually
- A private company negotiating early termination compensation clauses in the concession agreement
- A government monitoring concession compliance through annual performance audits

**Key concepts:** `concession agreement`, `concession period`, `tariff`, `reversion`, `performance obligations`

## Q24. What is the Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) model?

**Level:** Basic

BOT is a project delivery model where a private party builds an infrastructure asset, operates it for a specified period to recover costs and earn a return, and then transfers ownership to the government at the end of the concession. The private party bears construction and operating risks during the concession but benefits from revenue collection. BOT is one of the most widely used PPP models globally.

**Real-life applications:**
- A private consortium building a bridge under a BOT contract and collecting tolls for 30 years before handing it to the government
- A water utility operating under a BOT model where treated water is sold to municipalities
- A government choosing BOT over BOO to ensure eventual public ownership of a strategic asset
- A BOT highway where the government retains land ownership while the private party holds the operating license

**Key concepts:** `BOT`, `build-operate-transfer`, `concession`, `ownership transfer`, `revenue collection`

## Q25. What is a cash flow waterfall?

**Level:** Basic

A cash flow waterfall is the contractual order in which a project's available cash is distributed among various claimants. Typically, operating expenses are paid first, followed by senior debt service, then reserve account funding, then subordinated debt, and finally equity distributions. The waterfall ensures that higher-priority obligations are met before lower-priority ones receive any payment.

**Real-life applications:**
- A loan agreement specifying that no dividends may be paid until the debt service reserve is fully funded
- A project company following the waterfall to allocate quarterly revenues among lenders and equity holders
- A mezzanine lender receiving payments only after the senior debt tranche is fully serviced
- An equity investor analyzing the waterfall to understand when distributions will begin flowing

**Key concepts:** `cash flow waterfall`, `payment priority`, `senior debt service`, `reserve accounts`, `equity distributions`

---

## Q26. What is Cash Flow Available for Debt Service (CFADS)?

**Level:** Basic

CFADS is the amount of cash remaining after a project pays its operating expenses and taxes but before it makes any debt payments. It represents the pool of money available to service senior debt obligations. Lenders use CFADS to calculate the DSCR and to size the maximum amount of debt a project can support.

**Real-life applications:**
- A financial model calculating CFADS for each semi-annual period over the project's 20-year life
- A lender sizing the loan so that the minimum DSCR based on CFADS never falls below 1.25x
- A project company reporting CFADS to lenders as part of its quarterly compliance certificate
- An independent model auditor verifying that CFADS calculations correctly exclude non-cash items

**Key concepts:** `CFADS`, `operating cash flow`, `debt sizing`, `cash available`, `debt service capacity`

## Q27. What is a debt service reserve account?

**Level:** Basic

A debt service reserve account is a funded account set aside to cover debt payments during periods when the project's operating cash flows are insufficient. It is typically sized at three to six months of debt service and must be replenished if drawn upon. The reserve provides a buffer that protects lenders from short-term cash flow disruptions.

**Real-life applications:**
- A wind farm funding a six-month debt service reserve at financial close to cover seasonal revenue variability
- A project company drawing on its reserve during a three-month equipment shutdown for maintenance
- A lender requiring immediate replenishment of the reserve account after any drawdown
- A toll road using the reserve to cover debt payments during an unexpected traffic decline

**Key concepts:** `debt service reserve`, `cash buffer`, `liquidity protection`, `reserve funding`, `replenishment`

## Q28. What is a partial credit guarantee?

**Level:** Basic

A partial credit guarantee covers a portion of a project's debt repayment obligations, typically the later maturities that commercial lenders are reluctant to finance. By guaranteeing the tail end of the debt, the guarantor allows the project to access longer tenors and lower interest rates. Development finance institutions and multilateral banks commonly provide these guarantees.

**Real-life applications:**
- The World Bank guaranteeing the final five years of a 20-year bond to help a project access capital markets
- A development bank providing a partial credit guarantee to extend a loan tenor from 12 to 18 years
- A project saving 100 basis points on its borrowing cost because the guarantee improved its credit rating
- A guarantee provider charging a 1.5 percent annual fee for partial credit coverage

**Key concepts:** `partial credit guarantee`, `tenor extension`, `credit enhancement`, `guarantee fee`, `development finance`

## Q29. What is political risk insurance?

**Level:** Basic

Political risk insurance protects project investors and lenders against losses caused by government actions or political events such as expropriation, currency inconvertibility, breach of contract by a government entity, or political violence. Agencies like MIGA (part of the World Bank Group) and national export credit agencies are major providers of this coverage.

**Real-life applications:**
- A foreign investor purchasing political risk insurance before building a factory in an emerging market
- MIGA providing coverage against expropriation for a mining project in Sub-Saharan Africa
- A lender requiring political risk insurance as a condition for participating in a project in a politically unstable country
- An insurer paying a claim after a government nationalized a private power plant without compensation

**Key concepts:** `political risk insurance`, `expropriation`, `currency inconvertibility`, `MIGA`, `sovereign risk`

## Q30. What is governing law in a project finance contract?

**Level:** Basic

Governing law refers to the legal jurisdiction whose laws will be used to interpret and enforce a contract. In international project finance, parties often choose English law or New York law because these jurisdictions have well-developed commercial legal frameworks, extensive case law, and predictable courts. The choice of governing law affects how disputes are resolved and contracts are enforced.

**Real-life applications:**
- A cross-border project choosing English law for its loan agreement despite being located in Southeast Asia
- A sponsor insisting on international arbitration because local courts are perceived as unreliable
- A lender refusing to participate unless the governing law provides clear remedies for security enforcement
- A government requiring that the concession agreement be governed by local law for sovereignty reasons

**Key concepts:** `governing law`, `jurisdiction`, `contract enforcement`, `legal predictability`, `dispute resolution`

## Q31. What is an Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)?

**Level:** Basic

An EIA is a systematic study that evaluates the potential environmental consequences of a proposed project before construction begins. It identifies impacts on air, water, land, biodiversity, and local communities, and recommends mitigation measures. Most countries require an EIA as a condition for granting construction permits, and international lenders apply their own environmental standards.

**Real-life applications:**
- A dam project conducting an EIA that identifies downstream water flow impacts and proposes minimum release requirements
- A lender applying the Equator Principles and requiring an EIA that meets IFC Performance Standards
- A community group using EIA findings to negotiate additional environmental protections from a mining company
- A project delayed by six months because the EIA review process took longer than expected

**Key concepts:** `EIA`, `environmental impact`, `mitigation measures`, `permitting`, `Equator Principles`

## Q32. What is regulatory risk?

**Level:** Basic

Regulatory risk is the possibility that changes in laws, regulations, or government policies will negatively affect a project's revenues, costs, or ability to operate. This can include changes in tariff structures, tax rules, environmental standards, or licensing requirements. Regulatory risk is particularly significant for long-lived projects because the legal environment may change multiple times over a 20 to 30-year concession.

**Real-life applications:**
- A solar project losing revenue after the government retroactively reduced feed-in tariff rates
- A toll road facing increased costs after new environmental regulations required expensive retrofitting
- A water utility project affected by a government decision to cap consumer tariffs below inflation
- A foreign investor unable to repatriate profits due to new capital controls imposed after financial close

**Key concepts:** `regulatory risk`, `tariff changes`, `policy uncertainty`, `legal environment`, `compliance costs`

## Q33. What is an independent engineer in project finance?

An independent engineer is a third-party technical expert appointed to represent lenders' interests by monitoring the project's construction progress, technical performance, and asset condition. The independent engineer reviews construction milestones, certifies completion, and provides periodic reports to lenders. ## Q34. What is a covenant breach?

**Level:** Basic

A covenant breach occurs when a project company fails to meet a condition specified in its loan agreement. Common covenants include maintaining a minimum DSCR, providing regular financial reports, or keeping insurance policies in force. A breach can trigger remedies ranging from restrictions on distributions to acceleration of the loan, depending on the severity and the terms of the agreement.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project company breaching its DSCR covenant when revenues fall below projections for two consecutive quarters
- Lenders issuing a cure notice giving the project company 30 days to remedy a covenant breach
- A breach of the insurance covenant because the project company failed to renew a required policy
- A technical breach triggered by late submission of quarterly financial reports

**Key concepts:** `covenant breach`, `loan agreement`, `cure period`, `remedies`, `event of default`

## Q35. What is debt restructuring?

**Level:** Basic

Debt restructuring is the process of renegotiating the terms of a project's outstanding debt when the project faces financial distress. Changes may include extending the loan tenor, reducing the interest rate, deferring principal payments, or converting debt to equity. The goal is to align the debt burden with the project's realistic ability to generate cash flows, avoiding outright default.

**Real-life applications:**
- A toll road renegotiating its loan tenor from 15 to 20 years after traffic volumes fell short of projections
- Lenders agreeing to defer principal payments for two years while a troubled power plant repairs equipment
- A distressed project converting a portion of senior debt to equity to reduce the annual debt service burden
- A syndicate of banks appointing a restructuring advisor to coordinate negotiations with the project company

**Key concepts:** `debt restructuring`, `loan renegotiation`, `financial distress`, `tenor extension`, `principal deferral`

## Q36. What is the difference between project finance and corporate finance?

**Level:** Basic

In corporate finance, a company borrows against its overall balance sheet and creditworthiness, and the loan is repaid from the company's total revenues across all its businesses. In project finance, a standalone SPV borrows against a specific project's assets and cash flows, with limited or no recourse to the sponsors. Project finance isolates risk to a single asset, while corporate finance exposes the lender to the borrower's entire business.

**Real-life applications:**
- A utility company choosing project finance for a new power plant to avoid increasing its corporate leverage
- A bank charging a higher interest rate for project finance than corporate finance because of the non-recourse structure
- A conglomerate using corporate finance for routine capital needs but project finance for large, risky infrastructure investments
- A rating agency assigning separate credit ratings to a project SPV and its sponsor parent company

**Key concepts:** `project finance`, `corporate finance`, `balance sheet`, `non-recourse`, `risk isolation`

---

## Q37. What is the capital structure of a project?

**Level:** Basic

The capital structure is the mix of debt and equity used to finance a project. In project finance, the typical capital structure ranges from 60 to 80 percent debt and 20 to 40 percent equity, though the exact ratio depends on the project's risk profile, sector norms, and lender requirements. The capital structure determines how risk and return are shared between lenders and equity investors.

**Real-life applications:**
- A low-risk regulated utility project achieving an 80-20 debt-to-equity ratio
- A greenfield project in a risky market limited to a 60-40 debt-to-equity ratio by cautious lenders
- A sponsor optimizing the capital structure to maximize equity IRR while staying within lender covenants
- A financial advisor recommending a layer of mezzanine debt to bridge the gap between senior debt and equity

**Key concepts:** `capital structure`, `debt-to-equity ratio`, `leverage`, `gearing`, `financial structure`

## Q38. What is an off-take agreement?

**Level:** Basic

An off-take agreement is a long-term contract in which a buyer commits to purchasing all or a defined portion of the project's output at an agreed price. In power projects, this is called a power purchase agreement. Off-take agreements provide revenue certainty, which is critical for securing project finance because they give lenders confidence that the project will generate predictable cash flows.

**Real-life applications:**
- A government utility signing a 20-year power purchase agreement with a solar farm at a fixed tariff
- A mining company signing an off-take agreement to sell all copper output to a commodity trader
- A lender requiring a minimum off-take contract length that matches or exceeds the loan tenor
- An LNG project securing off-take agreements with multiple buyers across different countries to diversify revenue risk

**Key concepts:** `off-take agreement`, `power purchase agreement`, `revenue certainty`, `contracted revenue`, `buyer commitment`

---

## Q39. What is an EPC contract?

**Level:** Basic

An EPC (Engineering, Procurement, and Construction) contract is an agreement in which a single contractor is responsible for designing, procuring materials, and building the project. The contractor typically provides a fixed price, a fixed completion date, and performance guarantees. EPC contracts transfer construction risk from the project company to the contractor, which is critical for lender comfort.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project company awarding a lump-sum turnkey EPC contract for a petrochemical plant
- The EPC contractor paying liquidated damages for each day of delay beyond the guaranteed completion date
- A lender requiring an EPC contract from a creditworthy contractor before approving the construction loan
- An EPC contractor subcontracting specialized work while remaining responsible for the entire project delivery

**Key concepts:** `EPC contract`, `turnkey`, `fixed price`, `liquidated damages`, `performance guarantee`

---

## Q40. What is financial close?

**Level:** Basic

Financial close is the point at which all project agreements and financing documents are signed and all conditions precedent are satisfied, allowing funds to be drawn. It marks the transition from the development phase to the construction phase. Reaching financial close can take months or years of negotiation and requires alignment among sponsors, lenders, contractors, and government authorities.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project team celebrating financial close after 18 months of negotiations with 12 banks
- A lender requiring all permits, insurance policies, and key contracts to be in place before financial close
- A sponsor paying development costs out of pocket until financial close when the loan becomes available
- A government granting a concession conditional on the concessionaire achieving financial close within 12 months

**Key concepts:** `financial close`, `conditions precedent`, `loan drawdown`, `development phase`, `documentation`

---

## Q41. What is a grace period in project finance lending?

**Level:** Basic

A grace period is the initial portion of the loan tenor during which the borrower is not required to make principal repayments, though interest payments may still be due. In project finance, grace periods typically cover the construction phase because the project does not generate revenue until it begins operating. The grace period aligns debt repayment obligations with the start of cash flow generation.

**Real-life applications:**
- A three-year grace period on a power plant loan where construction takes two and a half years
- A lender capitalizing interest during the grace period by adding it to the loan balance rather than requiring cash payments
- A road project receiving a four-year grace period to account for construction time plus a traffic ramp-up period
- A borrower negotiating a longer grace period to reduce financial stress during the early years of operation

**Key concepts:** `grace period`, `construction phase`, `principal deferral`, `interest capitalization`, `debt service commencement`

---

## Q42. What is a completion guarantee?

**Level:** Basic

A completion guarantee is a commitment by the project sponsors to ensure that the project is built on time, on budget, and to the required specifications. If the project fails to reach completion, sponsors are obligated to cover cost overruns, provide additional equity, or repay the lenders. The guarantee protects lenders during the high-risk construction phase and typically falls away once the independent engineer certifies completion.

**Real-life applications:**
- Sponsors providing a joint and several completion guarantee to lenders for a new refinery
- A completion guarantee requiring the sponsor to inject additional equity if construction costs exceed the budget by more than 10 percent
- A lender converting the loan from recourse to non-recourse once the completion guarantee is released
- A sponsor negotiating the completion test criteria to include only technical performance, not financial targets

**Key concepts:** `completion guarantee`, `construction risk`, `sponsor support`, `cost overrun`, `recourse conversion`

---

## Q43. What is a concession period?

**Level:** Basic

The concession period is the total duration during which a private party has the right to operate a public asset and earn revenue from it under a concession agreement. It typically includes the construction phase and the operating phase. The period is set long enough for the private party to recover its investment and earn a reasonable return, after which the asset is transferred back to the government.

**Real-life applications:**
- A 30-year concession for a toll road that includes 3 years of construction and 27 years of operation
- A government shortening the concession period in response to lower-than-expected construction costs
- An investor calculating that the concession period is long enough to repay debt and achieve a 14 percent equity IRR
- A concession agreement that allows extension of the period if force majeure events cause significant delays

**Key concepts:** `concession period`, `operating period`, `investment recovery`, `asset transfer`, `concession duration`

---

## Q44. What are step-in rights?

**Level:** Basic

Step-in rights are contractual provisions that allow lenders to take control of the project or replace the operator if the project company defaults on its obligations. These rights give lenders a mechanism to protect their investment by appointing a new operator or restructuring the project rather than accepting outright loss. Step-in rights are a critical part of the lender security package.

**Real-life applications:**
- A lender exercising step-in rights to replace an underperforming operator in a water treatment PPP
- A concession agreement granting lenders the right to step in and cure defaults before the government can terminate
- A step-in clause giving lenders 90 days to find a replacement concessionaire
- A government agreeing to recognize lender step-in rights to make the project more bankable

**Key concepts:** `step-in rights`, `lender control`, `operator replacement`, `default remedy`, `security package`

---

## Q45. What is a tariff in the context of infrastructure projects?

**Level:** Basic

A tariff is the price charged to end users for using the project asset or consuming its output. In toll roads, it is the toll rate. In power projects, it is the electricity tariff. Tariffs are often regulated or capped by the concession agreement or a sector regulator and may include adjustment mechanisms tied to inflation, exchange rates, or cost indices.

**Real-life applications:**
- A water utility charging a tariff per cubic meter that increases annually by the consumer price index
- A toll road operator adjusting tolls every three years based on a formula in the concession agreement
- A government capping electricity tariffs to protect consumers, creating a gap that must be covered by subsidies
- An investor modeling different tariff escalation scenarios to understand their impact on project returns

**Key concepts:** `tariff`, `user charges`, `tariff escalation`, `price regulation`, `revenue stream`

---

## Q46. What is a handback in a concession?

**Level:** Basic

Handback is the process of transferring the project asset back to the government at the end of the concession period. The concession agreement typically specifies the condition in which the asset must be returned, requiring the operator to perform any necessary maintenance or repairs before handback. An independent assessment is usually conducted to verify compliance with handback requirements.

**Real-life applications:**
- A concession agreement requiring the toll road to have a remaining pavement life of at least 10 years at handback
- An independent engineer inspecting a port terminal two years before handback to identify required repairs
- A concessionaire funding a maintenance reserve in the final five years of the concession to cover handback costs
- A government rejecting the initial handback because the asset failed to meet specified condition standards

**Key concepts:** `handback`, `asset condition`, `maintenance requirements`, `concession end`, `independent assessment`

---

## Q47. What is a lock-up mechanism?

**Level:** Basic

A lock-up mechanism is a covenant in the loan agreement that prevents the project company from making equity distributions (dividends) when the DSCR falls below a specified threshold, typically 1.10x to 1.20x. The purpose is to trap cash within the project to ensure there is enough to service debt. Once the DSCR recovers above the threshold, distributions can resume.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project company unable to pay dividends for three quarters because the DSCR dropped to 1.08x
- Lenders setting a distribution lock-up at 1.15x and a default trigger at 1.05x in the loan agreement
- An equity investor modeling the frequency of expected lock-up events when evaluating the investment
- A project company building cash reserves during a lock-up period, which are released when the DSCR recovers

**Key concepts:** `lock-up`, `distribution restriction`, `DSCR threshold`, `cash trapping`, `lender protection`

---

## Q48. What is a reserve account?

**Level:** Basic

A reserve account is a funded account established to provide a financial cushion for the project. Common types include debt service reserves, maintenance reserves, and contingency reserves. These accounts are funded from project cash flows or at financial close and can be drawn upon when the project faces unexpected shortfalls. Lenders require reserves as part of the security package.

**Real-life applications:**
- A maintenance reserve account funded annually to cover major equipment overhauls every five years
- A debt service reserve account sized at six months of payments to protect against seasonal revenue dips
- A project company drawing on its contingency reserve to cover unexpected environmental remediation costs
- A lender requiring all reserve accounts to be held in escrow with the facility agent

**Key concepts:** `reserve account`, `debt service reserve`, `maintenance reserve`, `contingency`, `escrow`

---

## Q49. What is a power purchase agreement (PPA)?

**Level:** Basic

A PPA is a long-term contract between a power producer and a buyer (usually a utility or government) that specifies the price, quantity, and terms for electricity purchases over a defined period. The PPA is the primary revenue contract for most power project financings. It provides the cash flow predictability that lenders require and allocates demand, price, and payment risks between the parties.

**Real-life applications:**
- A solar farm signing a 25-year PPA with a state utility at a fixed tariff indexed to inflation
- A wind project negotiating a PPA that includes both a capacity payment and an energy payment
- A lender requiring the PPA tenor to exceed the loan tenor by at least two years
- A government aggregator signing PPAs with multiple small renewable generators under a feed-in tariff program

**Key concepts:** `PPA`, `power purchase agreement`, `tariff`, `capacity payment`, `energy payment`

---

## Q50. What is the difference between a BOT and a BOOT model?

**Level:** Basic

In a BOT model, the private party builds and operates the asset but does not hold legal ownership during the concession period; the government retains title. In a BOOT model, the private party owns the asset during the concession period and transfers ownership to the government only at the end. The practical differences relate to tax treatment, security rights, and the ability to mortgage the asset.

**Real-life applications:**
- A lender preferring a BOOT structure because the concessionaire can pledge the asset as collateral
- A government choosing BOT to maintain ownership of a strategic port throughout the concession
- A BOOT concessionaire depreciating the asset on its books for tax purposes during the concession period
- A legal advisor recommending BOOT in a jurisdiction where government-owned assets cannot be mortgaged

**Key concepts:** `BOT`, `BOOT`, `ownership`, `asset title`, `collateral`

---

## Q51. What is a financial model audit?

**Level:** Basic

A financial model audit is an independent review of the project's financial model to verify that formulas are correct, assumptions are reasonable, and the model accurately represents the project's cash flow mechanics. Lenders almost always require a model audit by a reputable firm before they will approve financing. The audit ensures that all parties are relying on a trustworthy analytical tool.

**Real-life applications:**
- A Big Four accounting firm auditing the financial model of a highway PPP before financial close
- A model auditor discovering a circular reference error that overstated the DSCR by 0.15x
- Lenders refusing to proceed until the model audit findings are resolved and the model is corrected
- A sponsor paying for an early-stage model audit to identify and fix issues before formal lender review

**Key concepts:** `model audit`, `formula verification`, `assumption review`, `independent review`, `lender requirement`

---

## Q52. What is refinancing in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

Refinancing is the process of replacing existing project debt with new debt, usually on more favorable terms. Projects often refinance after construction is complete because the risk profile has improved, allowing them to secure lower interest rates, longer tenors, or higher leverage. Refinancing can generate significant gains for equity investors by freeing up cash or reducing costs.

**Real-life applications:**
- A toll road refinancing its construction loan with a long-term bond at a lower interest rate after opening to traffic
- A sponsor sharing refinancing gains with the government as required by the concession agreement
- A project refinancing to extend the tenor and reduce annual debt service after proving stable operations
- A lender requiring refinancing within five years under a mini-perm structure

**Key concepts:** `refinancing`, `interest rate reduction`, `tenor extension`, `construction-to-permanent`, `refinancing gain`

---

## Q53. What is a credit rating in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

A credit rating is an independent assessment of the project's ability to meet its debt obligations, assigned by rating agencies such as Moody's, S&P, or Fitch. Ratings range from investment grade to speculative grade. A higher rating allows the project to borrow at lower interest rates and access a broader pool of investors, including institutional buyers who are restricted to investment-grade securities.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project bond receiving a BBB rating from S&P, making it eligible for purchase by pension funds
- A project paying for a shadow rating to gauge investor appetite before deciding to issue bonds
- A downgrade from investment grade to speculative grade triggering higher borrowing costs at refinancing
- A credit guarantee lifting a project's rating from BB to BBB, saving 80 basis points on the coupon

**Key concepts:** `credit rating`, `investment grade`, `rating agency`, `creditworthiness`, `bond pricing`

---

## Q54. What is a letter of credit?

**Level:** Basic

A letter of credit is a bank-issued instrument that guarantees a payment will be made on time and in full. In project finance, letters of credit are used to backstop various obligations such as construction performance bonds, advance payment guarantees, or debt service reserve requirements. If the obligor fails to pay, the bank honors the letter of credit, providing immediate liquidity.

**Real-life applications:**
- An EPC contractor providing a letter of credit to guarantee advance payment refund if it fails to perform
- A project company using a standby letter of credit instead of cash to fund its debt service reserve
- A government requiring a performance letter of credit as part of the concession bid submission
- A supplier receiving a letter of credit from the project company to guarantee payment for equipment delivery

**Key concepts:** `letter of credit`, `bank guarantee`, `payment assurance`, `standby LC`, `performance bond`

---

## Q55. What is a shareholders' agreement in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

A shareholders' agreement governs the relationship among the equity investors in the project company. It covers matters such as capital contributions, voting rights, dividend policies, transfer restrictions, deadlock resolution, and exit mechanisms. The agreement aligns the interests of multiple sponsors and provides a framework for decision-making throughout the project's life.

**Real-life applications:**
- A shareholders' agreement specifying that each sponsor must fund equity in proportion to its ownership share
- A drag-along clause requiring minority shareholders to sell their shares if the majority decides to exit
- A deadlock resolution mechanism that provides for independent mediation if sponsors cannot agree on a major decision
- A pre-emptive rights clause giving existing shareholders the first opportunity to purchase any shares being sold

**Key concepts:** `shareholders' agreement`, `voting rights`, `dividend policy`, `exit mechanism`, `sponsor alignment`

---

## Q56. What is insurance in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

Insurance transfers specific project risks to an insurer in exchange for premium payments. Common project finance insurance covers construction all-risks, business interruption, third-party liability, and environmental damage. Lenders require comprehensive insurance programs as a condition of financing because insurance protects both the project assets and the cash flows that service debt.

**Real-life applications:**
- A construction all-risks policy covering damage to the project during the building phase
- Business interruption insurance compensating the project for lost revenue during a forced shutdown
- A lender named as loss payee on the insurance policy to ensure proceeds are used for repair or debt repayment
- An insurance advisor designing a program that covers the specific risks identified in the project's risk register

**Key concepts:** `project insurance`, `construction all-risks`, `business interruption`, `loss payee`, `risk transfer`

---

## Q57. What is a direct agreement in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

A direct agreement is a contract between the project's lenders and a key project counterparty, such as the government grantor or the off-taker. It gives lenders the right to receive notice of defaults, the opportunity to cure defaults, and step-in rights before the counterparty can terminate the underlying project agreement. Direct agreements protect lenders by preserving the project's critical contracts.

**Real-life applications:**
- A lender requiring a direct agreement with the government to prevent concession termination without lender consent
- A direct agreement giving lenders 180 days to cure a default or find a replacement concessionaire
- An off-taker signing a direct agreement acknowledging the lenders' security interests in the project
- A government resisting a direct agreement clause that limits its ability to terminate for national security reasons

**Key concepts:** `direct agreement`, `lender protection`, `cure rights`, `step-in`, `contract preservation`

---

## Q58. What is a construction phase in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

The construction phase is the period from financial close to commercial operation during which the project asset is physically built. It carries the highest risk in the project lifecycle because no revenue is generated, costs can overrun, and unforeseen technical challenges may arise. Lenders closely monitor the construction phase through independent engineers and milestone-based drawdown schedules.

**Real-life applications:**
- A power plant's construction phase lasting 30 months, with loan drawdowns tied to certified construction milestones
- An independent engineer certifying that 60 percent of construction is complete before the next tranche is released
- Cost overruns during construction requiring the sponsor to inject additional equity under the completion guarantee
- Weather delays pushing the construction phase beyond the scheduled completion date and triggering liquidated damages

**Key concepts:** `construction phase`, `milestone drawdown`, `cost overrun`, `independent engineer`, `completion risk`

---

## Q59. What is commercial operation date (COD)?

**Level:** Basic

The commercial operation date is the point at which the project asset begins generating revenue on a sustained basis. It is formally certified by the independent engineer after the project passes a series of performance tests. COD is a critical milestone because it triggers the start of revenue collection, the commencement of debt repayment, and often the release of the completion guarantee.

**Real-life applications:**
- A power plant achieving COD after running at 95 percent capacity for 30 consecutive days
- The independent engineer issuing a COD certificate that triggers the first debt service payment
- A delay in COD causing the sponsor to pay liquidated damages to the off-taker for late delivery
- Revenue from the off-take agreement beginning to flow on the date the COD certificate is issued

**Key concepts:** `commercial operation date`, `COD`, `performance testing`, `revenue commencement`, `completion certification`

---

## Q60. What is a termination payment?

**Level:** Basic

A termination payment is the compensation paid when a concession or project agreement is ended before the expiry of its full term. The amount and triggers vary by the cause of termination. If the government terminates for convenience, the payment typically covers outstanding debt plus a portion of lost equity returns. If the private party defaults, the payment may only cover outstanding senior debt.

**Real-life applications:**
- A government paying a termination payment equal to outstanding debt plus accrued interest after canceling a highway concession
- A concessionaire receiving reduced compensation because termination was triggered by its own default
- Lenders insisting that the termination payment formula covers at least 100 percent of outstanding debt
- A force majeure termination resulting in a payment that splits losses between the government and the private party

**Key concepts:** `termination payment`, `early termination`, `compensation`, `concession termination`, `default termination`

---

## Q61. What is due diligence in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

Due diligence is the comprehensive investigation conducted by lenders, investors, and their advisors before committing capital to a project. It covers technical, financial, legal, environmental, and market aspects. The purpose is to identify risks, verify assumptions, and confirm that the project is structured in a way that protects all stakeholders. Due diligence findings often lead to changes in project terms.

**Real-life applications:**
- A legal advisor reviewing all project contracts during due diligence to identify gaps and inconsistencies
- A technical consultant assessing the EPC contractor's track record and construction methodology
- Lenders discovering during due diligence that the off-taker has a weak credit profile and requiring additional security
- An environmental consultant conducting site assessments to verify the EIA findings

**Key concepts:** `due diligence`, `risk identification`, `lender investigation`, `advisor review`, `investment decision`

---

## Q62. What is a currency risk in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

Currency risk arises when a project's revenues and costs are denominated in different currencies, or when debt is denominated in a currency different from the project's revenue currency. Exchange rate movements can increase debt service costs or reduce the value of revenues when converted. Managing currency risk is critical for projects in emerging markets where exchange rate volatility is common.

**Real-life applications:**
- A power project earning tariffs in local currency but repaying dollar-denominated debt
- A project using a currency swap to convert floating-rate local-currency revenue into fixed-rate dollar payments
- A government providing a dollar-indexed tariff to protect the project from currency depreciation
- A lender requiring at least partial natural hedging by matching the currency of revenues and debt

**Key concepts:** `currency risk`, `exchange rate`, `currency mismatch`, `hedging`, `natural hedge`

---

## Q63. What is a security package in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

A security package is the collection of assets, rights, and contractual protections that lenders hold as collateral for their loans. It typically includes a pledge over SPV shares, assignment of key contracts and insurance proceeds, a mortgage over project assets, and charges over project bank accounts. The security package gives lenders recourse to the project's value if the borrower defaults.

**Real-life applications:**
- A lender taking a first-priority mortgage over a power plant's land and equipment
- An assignment of the off-take agreement allowing the lender to collect payments directly if the borrower defaults
- A pledge over all SPV shares giving lenders the ability to sell the project company to a new owner
- A charge over project bank accounts ensuring that cash cannot be withdrawn without lender consent

**Key concepts:** `security package`, `collateral`, `pledge`, `assignment`, `mortgage`

---

## Q64. What is a conditions precedent (CP) in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

Conditions precedent are specific requirements that must be satisfied before a loan can be drawn or a financial close can occur. They include items such as signed project contracts, obtained permits, established insurance coverage, funded reserve accounts, and delivered legal opinions. CPs ensure that all essential elements are in place before money starts flowing.

**Real-life applications:**
- A loan agreement listing 50 conditions precedent that must be met before the first drawdown
- A financial close delayed by three months because an environmental permit CP was not yet satisfied
- A legal opinion CP requiring the borrower's counsel to confirm that all contracts are enforceable
- A CP requiring the debt service reserve account to be funded before construction loan drawdowns begin

**Key concepts:** `conditions precedent`, `drawdown requirements`, `financial close`, `documentation`, `lender safeguards`

---

## Q65. What is an interest rate swap?

**Level:** Basic

An interest rate swap is a financial derivative in which the project company exchanges its floating-rate interest payments for fixed-rate payments with a swap counterparty. This protects the project from rising interest rates that could increase debt service costs beyond what the cash flows can support. Lenders often require projects to hedge a significant portion of their floating-rate exposure.

**Real-life applications:**
- A project swapping its floating SOFR-based loan interest for a fixed rate of 4.5 percent for the full loan tenor
- A lender requiring that at least 75 percent of the floating-rate debt be hedged through interest rate swaps
- A swap breakage cost arising when a project refinances before the swap maturity date
- A project benefiting from lower total interest costs because the swap was entered when rates were low

**Key concepts:** `interest rate swap`, `floating rate`, `fixed rate`, `hedging`, `swap counterparty`

---

## Q66. What is a performance bond?

**Level:** Basic

A performance bond is a guarantee, usually issued by a bank or surety company, that ensures a contractor will complete its work according to the contract terms. If the contractor fails to perform, the bond issuer compensates the project company up to the bond amount. Performance bonds are commonly required under EPC contracts to protect the project company and its lenders from contractor default.

**Real-life applications:**
- An EPC contractor providing a performance bond equal to 10 percent of the contract value
- A project company calling on the performance bond after the contractor abandoned the site
- A lender requiring that the performance bond remain in force until the independent engineer certifies completion
- A contractor negotiating a reduction in the bond amount as construction milestones are achieved

**Key concepts:** `performance bond`, `contractor guarantee`, `surety`, `EPC protection`, `bond amount`

---

## Q67. What is a liquidated damages clause?

**Level:** Basic

A liquidated damages clause specifies a predetermined amount of compensation payable by one party for failing to meet a contractual obligation, such as completing construction by the agreed date or achieving a specified level of performance. The amount is agreed upon in advance and avoids the need to prove actual losses in court. It provides certainty and incentivizes timely and compliant delivery.

**Real-life applications:**
- An EPC contractor paying delay liquidated damages of 50,000 dollars per day for each day past the guaranteed completion date
- Performance liquidated damages paid when a power plant fails to achieve its guaranteed heat rate
- A cap on total liquidated damages set at 15 percent of the EPC contract value
- A project company using liquidated damages received from the contractor to cover additional interest costs during a delay

**Key concepts:** `liquidated damages`, `delay damages`, `performance damages`, `predetermined compensation`, `contractual remedy`

---

## Q68. What is a project bond?

**Level:** Basic

A project bond is a debt security issued by a project company in the capital markets to finance or refinance a project. Unlike bank loans, bonds are sold to a wide range of institutional investors such as pension funds, insurance companies, and asset managers. Project bonds typically carry a fixed interest rate and a long tenor, making them attractive for stable, operational infrastructure assets.

**Real-life applications:**
- A toll road issuing a 20-year project bond rated BBB to institutional investors after two years of successful operation
- A project company refinancing its bank construction loan with a lower-cost bond in the capital markets
- A pension fund purchasing a project bond for its portfolio because the long tenor matches its liability profile
- A bond trustee monitoring covenant compliance on behalf of hundreds of dispersed bondholders

**Key concepts:** `project bond`, `capital markets`, `institutional investors`, `fixed coupon`, `bond refinancing`

---

## Q69. What is a sponsor in project finance?

**Level:** Basic

A sponsor is the company or group of companies that initiates, develops, and provides equity capital for a project. Sponsors are the driving force behind the project, responsible for assembling the team, securing permits, negotiating contracts, and arranging financing. They bear the highest risk because equity is the last claim in the cash flow waterfall, but they also capture the greatest upside if the project succeeds.

**Real-life applications:**
- A construction company and an infrastructure fund jointly sponsoring a bridge project with a 50-50 equity split
- A sponsor providing a completion guarantee to lenders during the construction phase
- An industrial company sponsoring a captive power plant to secure its own energy supply
- A sponsor selling its equity stake to a pension fund after the project reaches stable operations

**Key concepts:** `sponsor`, `equity investor`, `project developer`, `risk bearer`, `residual return`

---

## Q70. What is an escrow account?

**Level:** Basic

An escrow account is a bank account held by a neutral third party that controls the release of funds based on specified conditions. In project finance, escrow accounts are used to hold construction proceeds, reserve funds, or revenue collections. They provide security to lenders by ensuring that project cash is managed according to the agreed waterfall and cannot be diverted by the project company.

**Real-life applications:**
- Construction loan proceeds held in escrow and released only upon certified milestone completion
- Revenue from the off-taker deposited into an escrow account controlled by the facility agent
- An escrow account holding the debt service reserve fund with withdrawal subject to lender approval
- Insurance proceeds deposited into escrow pending a decision on whether to repair the asset or repay debt

**Key concepts:** `escrow account`, `third-party control`, `cash management`, `lender security`, `fund release conditions`

## 1. Topic Snapshot
This topic is the vocabulary layer for project finance: how a project is isolated, funded, and defended through structure, contracts, and cash flow rules.

## 3. Frameworks & Matrices

### 1) SPV Ring-Fence Framework
**Purpose:** Keep project risk, cash, and contracts inside one legally separate box.

**Text Diagram:**
```text
Sponsor Equity  -->  SPV / Project Company  <--  Debt Lenders
                         |
           Project assets, contracts, cash accounts
                         |
                  Ring-fenced cash flow
```

Axes / Quadrants / Components explained:
Component 1: SPV legal separateness - what entity owns the project.
Component 2: Ring-fenced cash accounts - what money can be used for repayment.
Component 3: Contract web - who sells, builds, and operates for the SPV.
IT/AI/Product/Consulting worked example: [verified from model knowledge, not source] An AI data-center build is housed in a separate SPV so the cloud-services sponsor can fail elsewhere without contaminating the project lenders.
When to pull this out in a meeting: When a sponsor asks whether the project debt can sit off the core balance sheet.

### 2) Risk Register to Risk Matrix

**Purpose:** Decide whether the project can raise debt on acceptable terms.

## 4. Formulas

Formula: `DSCR = CFADS / Debt Service`
Variables:
CFADS = cash flow available for debt service.
Debt Service = principal + interest due in the period.
Why this formula exists: It answers whether the project produces enough cash to pay lenders on time.
How to interpret the output:
Value < 1.0x -> cash shortfall -> restructure, add equity, or reduce debt.
Value 1.0x-1.2x -> thin cover -> watch closely and stress test harder.
Value > 1.2x -> healthier cover -> debt is more defensible.
Worked example with numbers: If CFADS is 120 and debt service is 100, DSCR is 1.20x. That usually supports debt, but only with tight covenant monitoring.

Formula: `IRR = discount rate where NPV = 0`
Variables:
NPV = net present value of all project cash flows.
Discount rate = the rate being solved for.
Why this formula exists: It answers what annualized return the project or equity stack is producing.
How to interpret the output:
Value < hurdle rate -> reject or redesign.
Value around hurdle rate -> borderline, compare against alternatives.
Value > hurdle rate -> proceed if risk is acceptable.
Worked example with numbers: [verified from model knowledge, not source] If an AI infrastructure project requires a 14 percent hurdle rate and the solved equity IRR is 17 percent, the sponsor has return room for added risk.

## 5. Do vs Don't
| Don't | Do |
|---------|-------|
| Fund the project as if the sponsor's balance sheet is the backstop | Use an SPV and ring-fence the assets, contracts, and cash |
| Treat all project risks as equal | Use a risk register and risk matrix to rank likelihood and impact |
| Size debt from the optimistic case | Size debt from the base case and test downside sensitivity |
| Accept a vague off-take or weak remedy language | Insist on enforceable contracts and clear force majeure treatment |
| Let one bank carry the whole exposure | Syndicate the loan and spread credit exposure across lenders |

## 6. Real-Life Scenarios (Metric-Driven)
Scenario 1: AI data-center financing
Situation: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A product company wants to finance a dedicated AI training cluster through a separate project company so the hardware build does not burden the parent balance sheet. The sponsor says the cluster will be paid for from contracted compute revenue, not corporate cash.
Applicable framework/metric: SPV ring-fence + DSCR.
Analysis: If projected CFADS is 12 million and annual debt service is 9 million, DSCR is 1.33x. That is financeable only if the contract is enforceable and uptime risk is controlled.
Decision rule: If DSCR > 1.25x, proceed with lender diligence. If between 1.10x and 1.25x, tighten covenants and add reserves. If below 1.10x, cut leverage or add equity.
Action: Form the SPV, lock the compute contract, and build a downside case on utilization and power cost.

Scenario 2: Consulting-led transformation program
Situation: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A consulting firm is helping a client fund a multi-year operating model transformation with vendor payments, rollout milestones, and a milestone-based service contract. The question is whether the vendor delay risk belongs in the project or back with the supplier.
Applicable framework/metric: Risk register + risk matrix.
Analysis: If "vendor delay" has high probability and a two-quarter revenue impact, it belongs in the red zone. If "minor process rework" is low impact, it stays monitored but not over-managed.
Decision rule: If impact is high and mitigation is weak, transfer or insure the risk. If impact is medium, monitor it. If impact is low, accept it.
Action: Rebuild the contract schedule, assign owners, and review the top five risks weekly.

Scenario 3: Enterprise software rollout with lender-style discipline
Situation: [verified from model knowledge, not source] A SaaS platform rollout is treated like a project-financed asset because the implementation budget is fixed and revenue depends on go-live timing. The board wants to know whether schedule slippage breaks the return case.
Applicable framework/metric: Base case + sensitivity analysis + IRR.
Analysis: Base case equity IRR is 16 percent. If go-live slips by one quarter and IRR falls to 12.5 percent, the project moves below the 13 percent hurdle rate.
Decision rule: If IRR > hurdle rate by at least 2 points, proceed. If within +/- 2 points, redesign scope or timing. If below hurdle, pause and rework the plan.
Action: Add schedule buffers, stress test delay and cost inputs, and renegotiate milestones before funding release.

## 7. Implementation Playbook
1. Build the SPV map showing sponsor, lender, contractor, operator, and off-taker roles.
2. Draft the cash flow stack that separates operating cash, debt service, reserves, and distributions.
3. Assemble the risk register and score each risk for likelihood, impact, owner, and mitigation.
4. Test the base case for DSCR, IRR, and NPV before any debt term sheet is accepted.
5. Run sensitivity tables on the largest input variables and mark the covenant-breaking thresholds.
6. Review contract enforceability, force majeure language, and completion support before closing.
7. Syndicate the loan if the deal is too large for one lender or if exposure concentration is too high.
